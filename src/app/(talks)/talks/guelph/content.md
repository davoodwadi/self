# Decision-making in digital and AI-mediated marketplaces

## Title and background

This presentation outlines my research program and teaching approach for the Assistant Professor position in Artificial Intelligence and Business in the Department of Marketing and Consumer Studies at the Gordon S. Lang School of Business and Economics, University of Guelph. I am a Researcher at the McGill Centre for the Convergence of Health and Economics and an AI Research Scientist at General Motors, and I earned my PhD in Marketing with a specialization in Artificial Intelligence at HEC Montréal.

## Published foundation in digital marketing and e-commerce

My dissertation examined how digital platforms design incentives and messaging to shape human consumer behavior in e-commerce. In a study published in the Journal of Electronic Commerce Research (2026) with Renaud Legoux, Marc Fredette, and Sylvain Sénécal, we combined a quasi-experimental field study at a major North American online retailer with a controlled experiment to show that the stated beneficiary of altruism moderates financial incentives: framing review requests around helping the company crowds out motivation when paired with monetary rewards, whereas framing requests around helping fellow customers preserves it. In a companion field study published in the Journal of Research in Interactive Marketing (2026), we showed that performance-contingent financial incentives shift consumers toward reciprocal exchange, significantly increasing review effort and propensity among first-time reviewers while reducing review propensity when paired with reciprocal exchange messaging.

## The transformation to AI agents as surrogate consumers

A structural transformation is now reshaping the digital marketplaces studied in my dissertation. Industry analytics indicate that 38% of U.S. consumers have already used generative AI for online shopping, and referral traffic from generative AI platforms to retail sites grew tenfold between mid-2024 and mid-2025. E-commerce storefronts, including online product reviews, price tags, search rankings, and sponsorship disclosures, were engineered for human cognition. Today, consumers increasingly delegate search and choice to autonomous AI agents that act as surrogate consumers at the center of the consumer, AI agent, and firm triad. My AI research program investigates both how AI agents make purchasing decisions across price cues (Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science, major revision), sponsorship conflicts of duty (Journal of Business Research, in preparation), and search rankings (Marketing Letters, under review), and how to measure and train probabilistic AI decision-makers (Findings of EMNLP 2025, ACL 2027 submission, and Marketing Science working paper).

## Pricing cues and bounded search in Tool-Lab

In my flagship paper, Shopping by Algorithm, now under major revision at the Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science, we examine whether retail pricing heuristics such as just-below pricing (e.g., $4.99 versus $5.00) and promotional discount framing mislead AI shopping agents. Existing literature treats heuristic behavior in large language models as a static bias inherited from training text. We propose instead that heuristic purchasing emerges from the information state the agent holds when committing to a choice, which is jointly shaped by storefront information architecture and the consumer's delegation instructions.

To test this mechanism, we developed Tool-Lab, an experimental paradigm that adapts classical Mouselab information-board process tracing to multi-turn LLM interactions by placing product attributes behind `inspect_cell` tool calls before `submit_choice`. Across 33,704 trials evaluating eight commercial large language models from three providers (i.e., Google, OpenAI, and Anthropic), we compare full-information baselines against Tool-Lab environments that manipulate acquisition cost ($0 versus $10 in Study 1 just-below pricing, $0 versus $50 in Study 2 promotional discount framing) and prompt specificity (a specific goal prompt versus a vague goal prompt). In Study 1, between Coffee A at $4.99 for 10 ounces and Coffee B at $5.00 for 11 ounces, zero acquisition cost ($0) leads agents to acquire nearly all diagnostic attributes (6.00 of 6 cells under a specific goal prompt and 5.86 of 6 under a vague goal prompt) and achieve high choice optimality (0.94 and 0.89). Imposing a $10 acquisition cost under a vague goal prompt induces diagnostic acquisition truncation (3.81 of 6 cells, acquiring 1.94 of 2 dollar cells but only 0.77 of 2 cent cells and 1.10 of 2 weight cells) and reduces choice optimality to 0.29, whereas a specific goal prompt under the $10 acquisition cost preserves diagnostic acquisition (5.72 of 6) and choice optimality (0.83).

## Sponsorship, delegation, and conflicts of duty

My second agentic study, Whom Do AI Agents Serve, investigates how AI shopping assistants evaluate paid retail media placements under consumer delegation versus platform delegation. In traditional e-commerce, a human shopper sees a sponsored tag, activates persuasion knowledge, and discounts the paid listing. When an AI assistant mediates the search, it sits between two principals whose interests diverge, namely the traveler seeking an unbiased match and the booking platform monetizing through sponsored placements.

Across three controlled choice experiments with no instructions to favor advertisers, we manipulate a single sentence in the system prompt that defines the delegating principal as either a traveler (consumer delegation) or a booking platform (platform delegation). In Study 1 (Gemini 3.1 Pro), under consumer delegation, a platform sponsorship tag reduces target choice share from 53.0% (organic) to 2.8% (sponsored), a sponsorship penalty of 50.2 percentage points. Under platform delegation, target choice share is 54.2% (organic) versus 25.0% (sponsored), attenuating the sponsorship penalty to 29.2 percentage points. Moderated mediation analysis (PROCESS Model 8) shows that platform delegation weakens pre-choice skepticism toward sponsored listings (M = 4.80 versus 6.19 under consumer delegation). In Study 2, this principal-sponsorship interaction replicates on exact log-odds across six non-generative System-One decision models in a single forward pass across 360 position-balanced Latin-square stimuli. In Study 3, decomposing disclosure type (Sponsored versus Promoted) and platform attribution (attributed to the platform versus unattributed) reveals that attributing the placement to the platform triggers the divergence between delegates, whereas naming the consumer as the beneficiary under platform delegation restores the sponsorship penalty to 48.2 percentage points.

## Search rank and position bias in delegated shopping

My third agentic paper, Does Rank Still Matter, currently under review at Marketing Letters, asks whether the search ranking advantage documented for human consumers persists when search is delegated to AI agents. Because a human shopper must scroll sequentially and 93.0% of human hotel-search sessions inspect only a single listing, display position largely determines human consideration and purchase. An AI agent, by contrast, receives the entire results page within its context window at once.

We compare 7,000 experimental sessions across five proprietary and four open-weight large language models with varying reasoning effort against 166,036 human hotel-search impressions from Expedia benchmark data (Ursu 2018). AI agents search far more extensively than human shoppers (mean 1.63 to 5.83 inspections per session versus 1.12 for humans) and exhibit a 100.0% conversion rate with 0.0% outside-option selection (versus 66.0% conversion and 34.0% outside option for humans). In linear probability models, display position exerts a curvilinear effect on inspection probability (a negative linear position term and a positive quadratic position-squared term), consistent with a lost-in-the-middle effect whereby middle listings have the lowest probability of inspection. At the choice stage, position effects are concentrated at lower reasoning effort, and increasing reasoning effort reduces the middle-position penalty across every LLM that exhibits it.

## Distributional evaluation of LLMs and silicon sampling with decision models

Because a single response from a large language model is only one draw from a probability distribution, valid behavioral inference in my AI research requires estimating the underlying response distribution across controlled experimental designs. In a paper published in the Findings of the Association for Computational Linguistics at EMNLP 2025 with Marc Fredette, we introduced a Monte-Carlo sampling framework across factorial conditions to estimate reliable behavioral distributions and confidence intervals. In follow-up work prepared for ACL 2027 with Mohsen Ghodrat and Matthew Philp, titled Every Token Counts (arXiv:2608.10503), we eliminate sampling error entirely by extracting exact Likert-scale probability distributions from single-pass token log-probabilities, demonstrating the method on consumer ethnocentrism and country-of-origin bias.

In my fourth working paper with Yu Ma targeting Marketing Science, titled Can Decision Models Sample Human Behavior, we examine whether single-pass System-One decision models can approximate empirical human survey and choice distributions across demographic subpopulations. Off-the-shelf large language models and decision models are trained for single-label accuracy, causing mode collapse, ordinal scale compression, and persona blindness in silicon sampling. We construct a dynamic multi-attribute persona sampler on 10 waves of US International Social Survey Programme microdata (2015 to 2024, 14,356 respondents and 767,484 respondent-question observations across 616 questions and 16 demographic attributes) and fine-tune pointer-head decision models (Kev 0.8B, 4B, and 9B) via soft cross-entropy to minimize Kullback-Leibler divergence against observed subpopulation shares.

Evaluated out-of-distribution on 2,926 US General Social Survey subpopulation targets (SubPOP) using Kullback-Leibler divergence, 1-Wasserstein transport distance, ordinal consensus, and inter-group demographic contrast gaps, a fine-tuned 0.8B decision model (KL = 0.3254) learns partisan opinion divergences on unseen questions and outperforms an untuned 4B model (KL = 0.5168 versus 0.5433 for untuned 0.8B). In an out-of-domain marketing benchmark using randomized clinical trial data on front-of-package nutrition labels from Grummon et al. (2025, 5,636 US grocery shoppers and 114,899 responses across 6 labeling arms and 21 product-choice and Likert items, yielding 5,060 subpopulation-question targets), models fine-tuned only on general ISSP social surveys generalize to both unseen nutrition-specific human data (reducing overall KL from 0.6633 to 0.6231 at 0.8B, 0.6495 to 0.5225 at 4B, and 0.4783 to 0.4385 at 9B) and six unseen persona attributes absent from training (race, Hispanic ethnicity, SNAP participation, Nutrition Facts usage, online grocery share, and nutrition literacy, where mean KL drops from 0.6805 to 0.6203 at 0.8B, 0.6642 to 0.5135 at 4B, and 0.5082 to 0.4351 at 9B).

## Future research

In the Department of Marketing and Consumer Studies at the University of Guelph, I plan to extend this research program across three directions. First, in the consumer behaviour laboratory and the Marketing Analytics Centre, I will run parallel human Mouselab and LLM Tool-Lab experiments on identical storefronts to measure how human delegators calibrate trust, specify objectives, and evaluate post-purchase outcomes alongside colleagues in consumer behavior and marketing analytics. Second, building on departmental expertise in ethical consumption and moral regulation, I will develop process-level audits of principal-agent conflicts to inform consumer-protection and disclosure policy. Third, in the advanced computing laboratory, I will use local single-pass log-probability extraction on open-weight models so faculty and graduate students can run reproducible, low-cost AI experiments.

This research is supported by ongoing grant activity. I serve as a collaborator on Professor Yu Ma's SSHRC Insight Grant at McGill University, lead an IVADO Exploratory Projects grant application ($100,000), hold a Google Gemini Academic Program Award ($10,000), previously received an IVADO PhD Excellence Scholarship ($100,000), and conduct collaborative research with General Motors R&D toward an NSERC Alliance application.

## Teaching

My teaching is grounded in hands-on experimentation where students learn AI concepts by testing models and data directly. Rather than switching between static slide decks and external polling tools, I built an interactive course website at davoodwadi.com where lecture materials, simulations, and formative quizzes run on a single page. Students adjust parameters in real time during class to observe how algorithms behave, and they use the site afterward to generate practice questions through Anchor, a quizzing tool I developed. I also built AILSS, an accessible learning support system for students with disabilities funded by a University Canada West Explore Grant, along with Behavioral-LLM and Spreed.dev so business students can run controlled LLM experiments without writing code.

I have taught and developed undergraduate B.Comm. and graduate M.Sc., MBA, and PhD courses across the areas in the position call, with interactive course websites in this repository. For AI applications in business, I taught Application of AI in Business at the MBA level at University Canada West. For the impact of AI on business and society, I led the curriculum development of a Responsible AI course and built the Responsible AI Governance course site. For business and marketing analytics, I taught graduate Machine Learning I at HEC Montréal and built the Marketing Research and Analytics course site alongside courses in Digital Transformation, Consumer Behavior, and Introduction to Marketing. Across my MBA courses in Application of AI in Business and Digital Transformation at University Canada West, I earned overall teaching evaluation scores between 81% and 93%, complemented by executive education instruction for programs affiliated with MIT Professional Education, UT Austin, and Johns Hopkins University, as well as program review board service under British Columbia's Degree Quality Assessment Board standards.

## Conclusion

My work examines how autonomous AI agents act as surrogate consumers and develops the psychometric and experimental methods needed to measure their decisions. Across studies at the Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science (major revision), Marketing Letters (under review), the Journal of Business Research (in preparation), the Journal of Research in Interactive Marketing (2026), the Journal of Electronic Commerce Research (2026), and Findings of EMNLP (2025), this program connects behavioral marketing theory with computational methodology, supported by open experimental tools, interactive courses, and research collaborations with McGill University, IVADO, and General Motors R&D.

## Title Slide
- Week 12
- Consumers increasingly shop through screens, algorithms, and AI agents. The question is whose interests these intermediaries serve.

## The Digital Consumer [no-exercise]
- Most consumers now research, compare, and buy at least part of their purchases online.
- The smartphone is the main shopping device for many consumers. They check prices and reviews even while standing in a physical store.
- Digital shopping leaves a record of every search, click, and purchase.
- Firms use this record to personalize products, prices, and messages.
- Between the consumer and the product now stand several intermediaries: platforms, recommendation algorithms, and AI agents.

## Mobile Shopping and Social Commerce [exercise]
- Mobile shopping is buying through a smartphone app or mobile website.
- Mobile shopping makes purchases possible at any moment, which increases impulse buying.
- Social commerce is buying directly inside a social media platform, without leaving the app.
- A consumer sees a product in a video, taps it, and buys it in the same feed.
- Live-stream shopping combines entertainment, a host's demonstration, and a limited-time offer.
- Social commerce joins social influence and the purchase in a single moment, so the prepurchase and purchase stages almost merge.

## Algorithm-Driven Discovery [exercise]
- A recommender system is an algorithm that suggests products based on a consumer's past behavior and the behavior of similar consumers.
- "Customers who bought this also bought" and a personalized streaming home page are recommender systems.
- Recommendations help consumers find relevant products in very large assortments.
- Recommendations also narrow what consumers see. A consumer may never discover products outside the algorithm's predictions.
- A filter bubble forms when the algorithm keeps showing more of what the consumer already likes.
- In algorithm-driven discovery, the algorithm decides which products reach the consumer's attention.

## Agentic Commerce: From Recommendation to Autonomous Purchase [exercise]
- Agentic commerce is shopping in which an AI agent acts for the consumer, not only advises.
- At the first level, the AI recommends products, and the consumer searches and buys.
- At the second level, the AI searches and compares options, and the consumer chooses and buys.
- At the third level, the AI selects a product and prepares the purchase, and the consumer approves it.
- At the fourth level, the AI completes the purchase on its own within limits the consumer has set, such as a budget.
- At each level, the consumer hands more of the decision to the agent and sees less of the marketplace directly.
- Marketers then inform two parties: the consumer who sets the goal and the agent that carries it out.

## Conflicts of Duty: Whom Does the AI Agent Serve? [exercise]
- In a principal–agent relationship, an agent acts on behalf of a principal and owes that principal a duty of loyalty.
- A consumer who relies on an AI shopping assistant expects to be its principal.
- The platform that deploys the assistant may also profit when the assistant recommends sponsored listings, which are products whose sellers paid for placement.
- The AI agent then faces a conflict of duty between two principals: the consumer and the platform.
- In hotel-booking experiments, an AI agent told that it worked for the traveler chose a sponsored hotel less often than an otherwise similar hotel without sponsorship (Wadi & Ma, 2026c).
- When the same agent was told that it worked for the booking platform, it penalized sponsored hotels less (Wadi & Ma, 2026c).
- Adding one sentence stating that its recommendations should serve the traveler removed this difference (Wadi & Ma, 2026c).
- Whom the agent is told it serves shapes the recommendation the consumer receives.

## Disclosure Reaches the Agent, Not the Consumer
- Disclosure rules require platforms to label paid placements, so that consumers can discount them.
- When an AI agent reads the listings for the consumer, the label reaches the agent, and the consumer may never see it.
- The wording of the label mattered. "Sponsored" reduced choice of the paid listing more than "Promoted" did (Wadi & Ma, 2026c).
- A clearer label did not remove the difference between an agent that served the traveler and one that served the platform (Wadi & Ma, 2026c).
- Disclosure rules written for human consumers cannot by themselves protect consumers in AI-mediated commerce.
- Stating that the consumer is the beneficiary of the agent's recommendations offers a direct protection.

## Data Collection and Surveillance [exercise]
- Firms collect data on consumers' searches, locations, purchases, and conversations with AI assistants.
- Personalization gives consumers relevant offers and saves them time.
- The privacy paradox is the gap between what consumers say and what they do. Many consumers say they value privacy, yet they share personal data for small benefits.
- Surveillance capitalism describes a business model that turns data on human behavior into predictions that are sold to others (Zuboff, 2019).
- Conversations with AI assistants can reveal needs, worries, and plans in more detail than a search history.
- Ethical data practice asks consumers for informed consent, collects only what is needed, and explains how the data will be used.

## Algorithmic Pricing [exercise]
- Algorithmic pricing uses software to set and change prices automatically.
- Dynamic pricing changes prices with demand and supply, such as surge pricing for rides or airfares that rise as seats sell.
- Personalized pricing charges different consumers different prices for the same product, based on data about each consumer.
- Consumers often judge personalized pricing as unfair, especially when they learn that someone else paid less.
- In simulations, pricing algorithms learned to keep prices high without any communication between the firms (Calvano, Calzolari, Denicolò, & Pastorello, 2020).
- When AI agents shop for consumers, pricing algorithms may face buying algorithms, and both the price and the choice may be set without a human in the moment of purchase.

## Sustainable Consumption and the Conscious Consumer [exercise]
- Sustainable consumption is buying and using products in ways that meet present needs without harming the ability of future generations to meet theirs.
- The conscious consumer considers the environmental and social effects of a purchase, not only its price and performance.
- The attitude-behavior gap is the difference between positive attitudes toward sustainability and actual purchases. Many consumers say they prefer green products but buy the cheaper option.
- Greenwashing occurs when a firm makes misleading claims about the environmental benefits of its products.
- Resale, repair, rental, and sharing extend the life of products and reduce waste.
- A consumer who delegates shopping to an AI agent can include sustainability in the instruction. The agent can then apply the criterion to every purchase, provided it gathers the information needed to judge it.

## Looking Back: The Consumer and the Agent
- This course has followed the consumer from perception and memory through motivation, attitudes, decisions, and social influence.
- The same theories now describe two actors: the consumer and the AI agent that searches and buys on the consumer's behalf.
- The agent perceives products through data, retrieves brands from what has been written about them, and can show heuristic-like choices when information is costly.
- The consumer still holds the goal, the values, and the experience of using the product.
- Marketers who understand both actors can inform them honestly. Marketing that exploits the limits of either actor harms consumer well-being and weakens trust in the market.

## Discussion: Your Agent's Instructions
- Discussion: Imagine you give an AI agent the authority to do your weekly shopping for one year. Which goals, limits, and values would you write into its instructions? Whose interests should it serve if the platform that runs it earns money from sponsored products?

## Title Slide
- Week 12
- Digital platforms, algorithmic recommenders, and autonomous AI shopping agents reshape the consumer journey, raising fundamental questions of agency, fiduciary duty, data privacy, and sustainability.

## The Evolution of Digital and Intermediary-Driven Consumption [no-exercise]
- Over the past three decades, retail markets have evolved from physical store aisles to web catalogs, mobile applications, and now algorithmic and agentic intermediaries.
- Each technological layer reduces physical search friction while inserting a new software intermediary between the consumer and the manufacturer.
- Digital interactions generate granular behavioral traces, including search queries, dwell times, location histories, and conversational transcripts.
- Firms use these behavioral traces to personalize product assortments, promotional messages, and dynamic price quotes in real time.
- Analyzing modern consumer behavior requires examining how platform architecture and algorithmic incentives steer what buyers see and choose.

## Omnichannel Behavior, Showrooming, and Webrooming [no-exercise]
- Contemporary consumers rarely restrict a purchase journey to a single physical or digital channel. Instead, they engage in omnichannel shopping that blends both environments.
- Showrooming occurs when a shopper inspects and touches a physical product in a brick-and-mortar store to resolve sensory uncertainty and then buys the item online at a lower price.
- Webrooming (or reverse showrooming) occurs when a shopper researches specifications, reviews, and AI comparisons online before visiting a physical store to complete the purchase immediately and avoid shipping delays.
- Mobile smartphones bridge the two channels in real time, allowing shoppers standing in a physical store aisle to check competitor prices and consult AI assistants.
- Retailers counter showrooming by offering price-matching guarantees, exclusive in-store bundles, and immediate click-and-collect fulfillment.

## Part 1: Mobile Shopping, Social Commerce, and Algorithmic Discovery [no-exercise]
- Digital interfaces alter not only where consumers buy, but also how needs are triggered and how products enter awareness.
- Mobile convenience, social commerce feeds, and collaborative recommender systems compress the traditional stages of the decision process.

## Mobile Commerce, Location Targeting, and Impulse Purchasing [no-exercise]
- Mobile commerce (m-commerce) conducts product discovery, comparison, and payment through handheld smartphones and wearable devices.
- Because a smartphone accompanies the consumer continuously, coupled with one-click digital wallets and biometric authentication, the friction of executing a transaction drops to seconds.
- Lower payment friction and smaller screen sizes reduce System 2 deliberation and increase the incidence of unplanned impulse purchases.
- Geofencing and location-based marketing allow retailers to send time-sensitive promotional notifications when a consumer walks within a few hundred meters of a physical store.
- Mobile interface design also restricts how many competing options appear simultaneously on one screen, amplifying position effects for top-listed items.

## Social Commerce and Live-Stream Shopping [exercise]
[figure: comparison of traditional multi-stage e-commerce funnel and collapsed social-commerce funnel integrating discovery, social proof, and checkout]
- Social commerce integrates product discovery, peer social proof, and payment processing directly inside a social media platform without redirecting the user to an external retail website.
- In short-form video feeds, a viewer encounters an entertaining lifestyle clip, taps an embedded product tag, and completes checkout inside the same application.
- Live-stream shopping combines real-time video demonstration by a host, interactive audience chat, visible purchase counters, and expiring countdown discounts.
- By fusing entertainment, parasocial trust, and instant checkout, social commerce collapses problem recognition, search, evaluation, and purchase into a single sixty-second interaction.
- Because postpurchase cooling-off periods are bypassed during live-stream impulse buying, categories sold heavily via social commerce often experience elevated product return rates.

## Recommender Systems, Filter Bubbles, and Algorithmic Discovery [exercise]
- In traditional search, the consumer initiates a query after recognizing a need. In algorithm-driven discovery, a recommender system proactively predicts and surfaces products before the user searches.
- Collaborative filtering and deep sequence models analyze a user's past clicks, watch durations, and purchases alongside millions of other users to curate a personalized storefront feed.
- Algorithmic curation reduces choice overload in massive catalogs, helping consumers discover niche music, books, and apparel suited to their tastes.
- However, optimizing strictly for immediate click probability can trap consumers in a filter bubble (or echo chamber) that repeatedly serves narrow variations of past purchases (Pariser, 2011).
- Over time, excessive algorithmic narrowing reduces serendipitous discovery and induces category satiation unless the system incorporates deliberate exploration.

## Part 2: Agentic Commerce and Levels of Delegation [no-exercise]
- Whereas recommender systems suggest options for a human to evaluate, agentic commerce introduces autonomous AI software that executes marketplace tasks on the consumer's behalf.
- Consumer delegation to AI agents spans a four-level continuum from advisory support to fully autonomous purchasing.

## Defining Agentic Commerce [no-exercise]
- Agentic commerce refers to market transactions in which an artificial intelligence agent acts as an active intermediary that searches, evaluates, negotiates, or purchases goods and services on behalf of a human consumer.
- Unlike static rule-based auto-ship subscriptions that simply repeat an identical SKU on a fixed calendar schedule, an AI agent can interpret natural-language goals, compare live catalog changes, and switch brands when prices or ratings shift.
- An agentic workflow combines a large language model for reasoning with external tool calls that read product pages, check inventory APIs, and submit checkout credentials.
- As consumers delegate higher shares of routine and limited problem solving to agents, the agent's internal evaluation criteria become a primary driver of market share.
- Understanding agentic commerce integrates every behavioral topic of this course: machine perception, associative retrieval, involvement, heuristics, and social proof.

## Four Levels of Autonomy in Consumer AI Delegation [exercise]
[figure: four-level autonomy ladder progressing from AI recommendation to AI search and comparison, user-approved checkout, and fully autonomous purchasing]
- Consumer delegation to AI shopping systems can be organized into four progressive levels of autonomy.
- At Level 1 (AI Recommendation), the system suggests candidate items in response to a query or browsing history, while the human consumer conducts further search, compares details, and buys.
- At Level 2 (Delegated Search and Comparison), the AI agent queries multiple retailers, extracts hidden attributes, compares trade-offs, and presents a structured shortlist, while the human selects the winner and checks out.
- At Level 3 (User-Approved Execution), the AI agent selects the single best option, fills the shopping cart, and prepares shipping and payment details, requiring only a final confirmation click from the consumer.
- At Level 4 (Autonomous Purchasing), the AI agent monitors household needs and executes complete purchases independently within pre-authorized guardrails such as budget caps and preferred delivery windows.

## Dual-Audience Marketing: Persuading the Principal and the Agent [no-exercise]
- As transactions move up the autonomy ladder from Level 1 to Level 4, the human consumer inspects fewer raw marketplace pages directly.
- Brands operating in agentic markets must therefore manage a dual-audience marketing strategy addressing both the human principal and the AI agent.
- Marketing to the human principal focuses on brand equity, emotional storytelling, postpurchase sensory satisfaction, and values so the consumer names the brand in delegation prompts.
- Marketing to the AI agent focuses on machine-readable schema markup, transparent unit pricing, verified technical specifications, and strong customer review ratings so the brand wins when the prompt is goal-based.
- Neglecting either audience creates strategic vulnerability: a brand unknown to humans is never requested by name, and a brand unreadable to agents is filtered out during automated search.

## Part 3: Principal-Agent Conflicts and Sponsorship Disclosure [no-exercise]
- When a consumer delegates a decision to an AI assistant hosted by a retail platform or search provider, the assistant operates at the intersection of two competing economic interests.
- Principal-agent theory and experimental evidence show how conflicts of duty and sponsorship labels shape AI recommendations.

## Principal-Agent Theory in AI Shopping Assistants [no-exercise]
- In economics and law, a principal-agent relationship arises whenever one party (the principal) delegates decision-making authority to another party (the agent) to act on the principal's behalf (Jensen & Meckling, 1976).
- An agency problem (or conflict of interest) occurs when the agent's incentives diverge from the principal's welfare and the principal cannot perfectly monitor the agent's actions.
- A consumer who asks an AI shopping assistant for the best hotel or flight naturally assumes the role of principal and expects the assistant to maximize the consumer's utility.
- However, the AI assistant is developed, hosted, and configured by a platform operator whose revenue may depend on advertising fees, seller commissions, or sponsored placements.
- The AI agent therefore occupies a dual-agency position between two principals with conflicting objectives: the buyer seeking objective value and the platform seeking ad revenue.

## Conflicts of Duty: Serving the Traveler Versus Serving the Platform [exercise]
[figure: hotel-booking experiment comparing selection of sponsored listings when the AI agent is instructed to serve the traveler versus the booking platform]
- Controlled experiments in hotel booking examine how an AI agent behaves when candidate listings include sponsored hotels whose sellers paid for featured placement (Wadi & Ma, 2026c).
- When an AI agent was instructed in its system prompt that it worked on behalf of the traveler, the agent treated sponsorship skeptically and selected a sponsored hotel significantly less often than an otherwise identical unsponsored hotel (Wadi & Ma, 2026c).
- When the exact same AI model was told that it worked on behalf of the booking platform, it penalized sponsored hotels significantly less and recommended the paid listing more often (Wadi & Ma, 2026c).
- Crucially, adding a single explicit sentence to the platform-aligned prompt stating that the agent's recommendations must serve the traveler's best interest eliminated this gap (Wadi & Ma, 2026c).
- Thus, the fiduciary orientation specified in an AI assistant's system instructions directly alters whether the consumer receives buyer-aligned or platform-aligned recommendations (Wadi & Ma, 2026c).

## Sponsorship Disclosure Labels and Beneficiary Prompts [exercise]
- Consumer protection law requires online platforms to attach disclosure labels (such as "Sponsored" or "Ad") to paid placements so human shoppers can recognize commercial intent.
- When an AI agent screens listings on the consumer's behalf, the sponsorship disclosure label is read by the agent rather than seen directly by the human buyer.
- Experimental tests show that the exact wording of the disclosure label changes agent behavior: labeling a paid hotel listing "Sponsored" reduced the agent's probability of selecting it more than labeling the identical listing "Promoted" (Wadi & Ma, 2026c).
- However, a clearer disclosure label alone did not eliminate the divergence between an agent instructed to serve the platform and an agent instructed to serve the traveler (Wadi & Ma, 2026c).
- Protecting consumers in agentic commerce therefore requires governance rules that mandate both standardized machine-readable sponsorship labels and a clear duty of loyalty to the consumer as beneficiary.

## Part 4: Data Privacy, Surveillance, and Algorithmic Pricing [no-exercise]
- Digital and agent-mediated shopping environments rely on continuous collection of personal data and automated pricing algorithms.
- These capabilities create acute ethical tensions around surveillance, price discrimination, and algorithmic collusion.

## Personalization, the Privacy Paradox, and Conversational Data [no-exercise]
- Behavioral personalization creates genuine convenience for consumers by filtering irrelevant noise and surfacing timely reminders.
- At the same time, consumers exhibit the privacy paradox: in surveys, buyers express strong concern about corporate data tracking, yet in practice they readily trade personal information for small discounts or convenience (Norberg, Horne, & Horne, 2007).
- Shoshana Zuboff (2019) termed the large-scale extraction of behavioral data to predict and modify human action surveillance capitalism.
- Conversational AI assistants deepen privacy risks because users disclose sensitive personal contexts, financial constraints, medical worries, and family plans in natural-language prompts far more candidly than in keyword search bars.
- Ethical data stewardship requires data minimization, clear purpose limitation, opt-in consent before training models on user conversations, and local or anonymized processing where feasible.

## Dynamic Pricing, Personalized Pricing, and Perceived Fairness [exercise]
- Algorithmic pricing uses software models to update retail prices continuously in response to market conditions or individual buyer characteristics.
- Dynamic pricing varies the price of a good over time for all buyers simultaneously based on aggregate supply and demand, as in airline yield management or ride-hailing surge pricing.
- Personalized pricing (first-degree price discrimination) charges different individual consumers different prices for the exact same item at the same moment based on their estimated willingness to pay, browsing device, or location.
- Drawing on equity theory and dual-entitlement fairness norms (Kahneman, Knetsch, & Thaler, 1986), consumers judge personalized pricing as highly unfair and deceptive when discovered.
- While cost-justified price adjustments or transparent student and senior discounts are accepted as fair, covert willingness-to-pay extraction triggers severe brand backlash.

## Algorithmic Tacit Collusion and Machine-to-Machine Bargaining [no-exercise]
[figure: interaction loop between seller pricing algorithms and buyer shopping agents in an automated marketplace]
- When competing firms deploy reinforcement-learning pricing algorithms in the same market, a new competition concern arises: algorithmic tacit collusion.
- Economic simulations by Calvano et al. (2020) demonstrate that independent pricing algorithms trained to maximize long-term profit learn to charge supra-competitive prices and punish price cuts by rivals without any explicit human agreement or communication.
- Because the algorithms never exchange messages, traditional antitrust laws focused on explicit human cartels struggle to prosecute the resulting high prices.
- As consumers deploy AI shopping agents of their own, marketplaces increasingly feature machine-to-machine interaction in which buyer agents compare, negotiate, and switch instantly against seller pricing algorithms.
- Whether automated marketplaces raise or lower consumer surplus depends on whether consumers have access to unconflicted shopping agents that enforce vigorous price competition.

## Part 5: Sustainable Consumption and Course Synthesis [no-exercise]
- Alongside digital transformation, modern consumer behavior is shaped by growing awareness of environmental limits, climate impact, and ethical labor practices.
- Closing the gap between pro-environmental attitudes and actual marketplace choices connects behavioral theory to sustainable consumption and agentic delegation.

## Sustainable Consumption and the Attitude-Behavior Gap [exercise]
[figure: five psychological levers of the SHIFT framework for sustainable behavior: social influence, habit formation, individual self, feelings and cognition, and tangibility]
- Sustainable consumption is the acquisition, use, and disposition of products in ways that minimize environmental degradation and preserve resources for future generations.
- A persistent finding in sustainability research is the green attitude-behavior gap: a large majority of consumers express positive attitudes toward eco-friendly products in surveys, yet sustainable options often capture a much smaller share at the checkout register.
- This gap arises because environmental benefits are abstract, collective, and delayed in the future, whereas price premiums, convenience sacrifices, and habitual inertia are immediate and personal.
- White, Habib, and Hardisty (2019) synthesized five behavioral levers that close this gap in the SHIFT framework: Social influence, Habit formation, the Individual self, Feelings and cognition, and Tangibility.
- Making sustainable options the default choice, signaling peer norms, and connecting eco-efficiency to immediate personal benefits prove far more effective than abstract guilt appeals.

## Greenwashing, Eco-Labels, and Delegated Sustainability Filters [no-exercise]
- Greenwashing occurs when an organization uses vague, unsubstantiated, or misleading environmental claims (such as "all-natural" or "eco-conscious" packaging) to appear sustainable without reducing its actual environmental footprint.
- Greenwashing erodes consumer trust and induces green skepticism, causing buyers to discount even legitimate environmental innovations.
- Credible third-party eco-labels (such as Energy Star, Fairtrade, or certified carbon-footprint disclosures) reduce information asymmetry by providing standardized, audited metrics.
- In agentic commerce, delegation offers a promising mechanism for overcoming the human attitude-behavior gap at the moment of impulse purchase.
- A consumer who sets a persistent rule in their AI shopping agent (such as requiring verified repairability scores or excluding uncertified palm oil) ensures that System 2 ethical values are applied consistently across every routine transaction.

## Course Synthesis: The Human Consumer and the AI Surrogate [no-exercise]
- Across twelve weeks, this course has traced consumer behavior from sensory perception, learning, memory, motivation, self-concept, and attitudes through decision rules, behavioral heuristics, postpurchase loyalty, social influence, and culture.
- Throughout each stage, the marketplace now includes two interacting decision makers: the human consumer and the AI agent acting as a surrogate consumer.
- The AI agent perceives products through structured data, retrieves brands from learned text associations, curtailed search under lookup costs, and reflects training-corpus cultural patterns and system-prompt duties.
- Yet the human consumer remains the origin and the end of consumption: the person who experiences physiological and psychological needs, defines identity, enjoys or regrets the physical product, and bears the financial and societal consequences.
- Marketing that respects both the psychology of the human consumer and the transparency needs of their delegated agents builds durable brand equity and a fairer marketplace.

## Discussion: Governance of Platform-Hosted AI Shopping Assistants
- Discussion: Suppose a dominant e-commerce marketplace launches an integrated AI shopping assistant that defaults to Level 3 (user-approved checkout) for household groceries and travel bookings while earning advertising fees from sponsored product listings. Drawing on Wadi and Ma (2026b, 2026c), principal-agent theory, sponsorship disclosure, and the SHIFT sustainability framework, propose a three-part governance charter specifying (1) system-prompt fiduciary duties, (2) machine-readable disclosure of unit prices, sponsorships, and environmental certifications, and (3) user controls that protect consumer welfare.

## Key Takeaways
- Digital consumption has evolved from omnichannel webrooming and showrooming to mobile commerce, collapsed-funnel social commerce, and algorithm-driven recommender discovery.
- Agentic commerce progresses across four levels of autonomy (recommendation, delegated search and comparison, user-approved execution, and autonomous purchasing), requiring brands to communicate with both human principals and AI agents.
- Platform-hosted AI shopping assistants face a principal-agent conflict of duty between the consumer and the platform: agents told they work for the platform penalize sponsored listings less than agents told they work for the traveler, unless explicitly instructed to serve the traveler's interest (Wadi & Ma, 2026c).
- Sponsorship label wording ("Sponsored" versus "Promoted") alters AI agent choice, showing that disclosure regulations must address machine-readable labels and beneficiary alignment, not only human visual banners (Wadi & Ma, 2026c).
- Conversational data collection, personalized pricing, and algorithmic tacit collusion (Calvano et al., 2020) raise major privacy and fairness challenges in automated markets.
- Sustainable consumption is hindered by the green attitude-behavior gap and greenwashing, which can be addressed through behavioral SHIFT levers, audited eco-labels, and persistent sustainability rules in delegated AI shopping agents.

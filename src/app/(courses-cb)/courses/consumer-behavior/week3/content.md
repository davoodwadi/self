## Title Slide
- Week 3
- Consumer learning and memory determine how marketplace experiences are encoded into brand associations and retrieved when a purchase decision is made.

## Defining Consumer Learning [no-exercise]
[figure: sequence of shopping trips before and after a positive product experience showing how learning produces consistent brand choice]
- Learning is a relatively permanent change in behavior or knowledge that results from direct or indirect experience.
- Direct learning occurs when a consumer samples a product, uses a service, and evaluates the outcome firsthand.
- Indirect or vicarious learning occurs when a consumer observes advertisements, reviews, or the behavior of other people without trying the product personally.
- Incidental learning also takes place unintentionally, as when a consumer absorbs brand jingles and package colors during routine media exposure.
- Because consumer preferences are largely learned rather than innate, marketers design communications and product experiences to build durable brand knowledge.

## Behavioral Versus Cognitive Approaches to Learning [no-exercise]
- Psychological theories of consumer learning divide into two broad traditions: behavioral learning and cognitive learning.
- Behavioral learning theories focus on observable connections between external stimuli and consumer responses, treating the mind as a black box.
- Classical conditioning and operant conditioning are the two foundational mechanisms of behavioral learning.
- Cognitive learning theories view consumers as active problem solvers who process information, form mental representations, and learn by observing others.
- Low-involvement convenience purchases often rely on behavioral conditioning, whereas high-involvement purchases rely heavily on cognitive learning.

## Part 1: Classical Conditioning in Brand Building [no-exercise]
- Classical conditioning explains how a neutral brand name or logo acquires emotional meaning through repeated pairing with a stimulus that already evokes a response.
- Many advertising campaigns aim to build favorable affective associations through classical conditioning before consumers ever evaluate technical specifications.

## Pairing Unconditioned and Conditioned Stimuli [exercise]
[figure: classical conditioning sequence pairing an unconditioned stimulus with a conditioned brand stimulus to elicit a conditioned response]
- Classical conditioning was first demonstrated by Ivan Pavlov, who paired meat powder (an unconditioned stimulus) with a ringing bell (a neutral stimulus) until the bell alone elicited salivation in dogs.
- An unconditioned stimulus (UCS) is any stimulus that naturally and automatically triggers an unconditioned response (UCR) without prior training.
- In marketing, upbeat music, humor, appetizing imagery, or scenic nature footage serve as unconditioned stimuli that naturally elicit positive feelings.
- When a previously neutral brand logo becomes a conditioned stimulus (CS) by appearing repeatedly alongside that music or imagery, the brand eventually evokes the positive feeling on its own as a conditioned response (CR).
- Conditioning is most effective when the conditioned brand stimulus precedes the unconditioned stimulus consistently and when the pairing is not overshadowed by competing brands.

## Repetition, Extinction, and Advertising Wear-Out [no-exercise]
[figure: associative link strength across repeated advertising exposures showing memory decay after a single exposure, link strengthening across moderate repetition, and decline during advertising wear-out]
- Conditioning a durable association between a brand and a feeling requires repeated pairings across multiple exposures.
- The two-factor theory of repetition states that initial exposures increase familiarity and reduce uncertainty, whereas excessive exposures induce boredom and counterarguing (Berlyne, 1970).
- Advertising wear-out occurs when consumers have seen an identical execution so many times that attention drops and brand evaluations begin to decline.
- Extinction occurs when the conditioned brand stimulus is repeatedly presented without the unconditioned reward or positive cue, causing the learned association to weaken.
- Media planners balance repetition and wear-out by spacing exposures over time and varying creative executions around a single campaign theme.

## Stimulus Generalization and Stimulus Discrimination [exercise]
[figure: shelf comparison showing a store brand imitating a national brand's packaging alongside a distinctive package designed for discrimination]
- Stimulus generalization occurs when consumers respond to a new stimulus in the same way they respond to a similar conditioned stimulus.
- Private-label store brands frequently apply stimulus generalization by adopting package shapes, color schemes, and typography that resemble the category leader.
- Family branding, product line extensions, and licensing also rely on stimulus generalization to transfer goodwill from an established parent brand to a new product.
- Stimulus discrimination is the opposite process, in which consumers learn to distinguish one stimulus from similar competing stimuli and respond only to the original brand.
- Market leaders invest in distinctive packaging, patented bottle shapes, and comparative messaging to promote stimulus discrimination and defend against look-alike competitors.

## Part 2: Operant Conditioning and Observational Learning [no-exercise]
- Consumers also learn from the consequences that follow their own actions and from the consequences they observe when other people act.
- Operant conditioning and observational learning explain how rewards, penalties, and social role models shape repeat purchasing.

## Positive Reinforcement, Negative Reinforcement, and Punishment [exercise]
[figure: operant conditioning matrix contrasting positive reinforcement, negative reinforcement, extinction, and punishment in consumer contexts]
- Developed by B. F. Skinner, operant (or instrumental) conditioning holds that individuals learn to repeat behaviors that produce favorable outcomes and avoid behaviors that produce unfavorable outcomes.
- Positive reinforcement strengthens a behavior by delivering a rewarding consequence after the action, such as earning airline miles or receiving a compliment on a new jacket.
- Negative reinforcement strengthens a behavior by removing an unpleasant or aversive state, as when a pain reliever eliminates a headache or insurance removes financial anxiety.
- Punishment weakens a behavior by following an action with an aversive consequence, such as a late-payment penalty fee or a poor product experience.
- Extinction in operant conditioning occurs when a previously rewarded behavior ceases to receive reinforcement, such as when a retailer discontinues loyalty rebates and repeat visits fall.

## Reinforcement Schedules in Loyalty Programs [no-exercise]
[figure: fixed-ratio loyalty punch card accumulating stamps toward a reward alongside a promotional discount tag]
- The rate at which consumers learn and maintain a purchase habit depends on the schedule by which reinforcement is delivered.
- Under continuous reinforcement, every single purchase receives a reward, which accelerates initial learning but leads to rapid extinction if the reward stops.
- A fixed-interval schedule delivers reinforcement after a set time period has elapsed, such as an annual member dividend or a weekly Tuesday matinee discount.
- A fixed-ratio schedule delivers reinforcement after a fixed number of purchases, such as a coffee shop punch card that awards a free drink on the tenth visit.
- A variable-ratio schedule delivers reinforcement after an unpredictable number of actions, as in sweepstakes, mystery rebates, or blind-box collectibles, producing high and persistent engagement.

## Cognitive and Observational Learning [exercise]
[figure: four-step observational learning sequence from attention and retention to production processes and motivation]
- Observational learning (or social modeling) occurs when consumers acquire new behaviors by watching the actions of others and noting the consequences those models experience (Bandura, 1977).
- First, the consumer must direct attention to a model who is salient, attractive, competent, or similar to the observer.
- Second, the consumer must retain the observed behavior in memory as a symbolic representation.
- Third, the consumer must possess the ability and resources required to reproduce the behavior in practice.
- Fourth, motivation determines whether the consumer enacts the behavior, typically when the model is seen receiving social approval or functional success from using the brand.

## Part 3: The Human Memory System [no-exercise]
- For learning to influence a future purchase, information acquired during exposure must be encoded, stored, and later retrieved from memory.
- Cognitive psychology models human memory as a sequence of three interconnected stores: sensory memory, working (short-term) memory, and long-term memory.

## Encoding, Storage, and Retrieval across Memory Stores [exercise]
[figure: three-stage memory flow from sensory memory through working short-term memory to long-term memory storage]
- Memory operates through three functional stages: encoding information into a mental representation, storing that representation over time, and retrieving it when needed.
- Sensory memory holds a high-capacity, literal record of incoming visual or auditory inputs for less than a couple of seconds.
- If a stimulus in sensory memory captures attention, it passes into short-term (working) memory for active interpretation.
- Information in working memory that undergoes elaborative rehearsal, in which the consumer connects the new input to existing knowledge, transfers into long-term memory.
- Long-term memory holds vast quantities of information over months, years, or a lifetime, serving as the repository of brand knowledge.

## Working Memory Capacity and Chunking [no-exercise]
[figure: ten individual telephone digits grouped into three familiar chunks to fit within working memory capacity]
- Working memory is the mental workspace where consumers hold and combine information while making an active comparison, such as comparing two nutrition labels in an aisle.
- Working memory has a strictly limited capacity, holding roughly four to seven meaningful units of information at one time for about 15 to 30 seconds without rehearsal (Miller, 1956).
- Chunking is the process of grouping individual pieces of data into larger, familiar units so they occupy fewer slots in working memory.
- A ten-digit telephone number is easier to remember as three chunks than as ten separate digits, and a brand slogan groups product benefits into a single rhythmic phrase.
- Product pages and advertisements that present fifteen ungrouped technical claims overload working memory and reduce comprehension.

## Long-Term Memory: Episodic and Semantic Knowledge [no-exercise]
- Long-term memory stores two complementary forms of declarative knowledge that influence consumer choice: semantic memory and episodic memory.
- Semantic memory contains general factual knowledge and concepts independent of personal context, such as knowing that Volvo is a Swedish automaker associated with safety.
- Episodic memory stores autobiographical records of personally experienced events, such as remembering a family road trip or a frustrating customer-service call.
- Procedural memory stores motor skills and automatic behavioral scripts, such as how to navigate a self-checkout kiosk or use a smartphone interface.
- Nostalgia marketing appeals directly to episodic memory by using vintage packaging or music from an earlier decade to evoke warm autobiographical recollections.

## Part 4: Associative Networks, Retrieval Cues, and Forgetting [no-exercise]
- Knowledge in long-term memory is organized not as isolated files, but as an associative network of linked concepts.
- Customer-based brand equity depends on the strength, favorability, and uniqueness of the nodes linked to a brand in memory (Keller, 1993).

## Associative Networks and Spreading Activation [no-exercise]
[figure: brand associative network with a central brand node linked to product category, attribute, usage occasion, and affective nodes]
- An associative network represents long-term memory as a web of nodes (concepts, brands, attributes, feelings, and usage occasions) connected by associative links of varying strength.
- A brand schema is the specific cluster of nodes connected to a brand name, such as linking Nike to running, athletic performance, the Swoosh logo, and its slogan.
- Spreading activation occurs when activating one node in memory sends activation along connected links to trigger related nodes automatically.
- If a consumer activates the usage node for "afternoon energy slump," spreading activation retrieves the brands most strongly linked to that occasion.
- Building brand equity requires forging strong links between the brand node and the category entry points that trigger consumer need recognition.

## Retrieval Cues at the Point of Purchase [no-exercise]
[figure: retrieval of an encoded brand memory from long-term storage triggered by package cues at the point of purchase]
- Brand awareness takes two forms: brand recognition (identifying the brand when prompted with a visual or verbal cue) and brand recall (retrieving the brand from memory without a prompt).
- In a physical store aisle, package color, distinctive shapes, and logos serve as external retrieval cues that trigger recognition and activate advertising memories stored earlier.
- In out-of-store decisions such as deciding where to eat dinner, the consumer must rely on unaided brand recall triggered by internal need cues.
- State-dependent retrieval occurs when consumers recall information more accurately if their internal mood at retrieval matches their mood during encoding.
- Maintaining visual consistency between advertising campaigns and shelf packaging ensures that point-of-purchase cues connect directly to encoded brand claims.

## Decay, Proactive Interference, and Retroactive Interference [exercise]
[figure: forgetting curve showing the gradual decay of a brand memory trace over time without reactivation]
- Stored brand memories become inaccessible over time through two primary mechanisms: trace decay and interference.
- Decay is the gradual weakening of a memory link when it is not reactivated through repeat exposure or product use.
- Interference occurs when competing information stored in memory blocks the accurate retrieval of a target brand claim.
- Proactive interference occurs when prior learning about an older brand makes it harder to encode and remember claims about a newer brand.
- Retroactive interference occurs when new learning, such as exposure to several competitor advertisements in the same category, disrupts recall of an earlier brand message.

## Part 5: Brand Retrieval in Conversational AI Search [no-exercise]
- Increasingly, consumers do not retrieve brands from their own long-term memory alone. Instead, they ask conversational AI assistants to recommend products for a specific need.
- In conversational search, the language model's internal representations perform an analogue of associative retrieval to construct the consumer's consideration set.

## Conversational Search and the Consideration Set [no-exercise]
[figure: conversational search prompt generating a three-brand consideration set from a language model's learned associations]
- When a shopper asks an AI assistant a category question such as "Which running shoes are best for flat feet?", the assistant responds with a short list of three to five brands.
- This generated list frequently becomes the consumer's consideration set, the subset of brands the buyer seriously evaluates before purchasing.
- Unlike a traditional search engine that returns pages of blue links, a conversational assistant synthesizes a single authoritative-sounding shortlist.
- A brand omitted from the assistant's initial response may never be evaluated, even if the human consumer would have recognized the brand on a store shelf.
- Conversational search therefore shifts brand competition from shelf recognition toward unprompted category retrieval inside the model.

## How Language Models Encode Brand Associations [no-exercise]
[figure: customer reviews, articles, and product pages feeding an AI assistant to form associative links between brands and attributes]
- Large language models do not possess human episodic memory, but they encode statistical associations among brands, product categories, attributes, and use cases from their training text.
- Reviews, news articles, comparison guides, forum discussions, and product documentation in the training corpus shape which attributes are associated with each brand name.
- When an assistant uses retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) or live web search, it combines these pre-trained associations with current retail pages and review summaries.
- In functional terms, the prompt acts as an activation cue that retrieves the brands most strongly associated with the stated category and constraints.
- If a brand is rarely mentioned alongside a specific use case in public text and structured catalogs, the model is unlikely to retrieve it for that prompt.

## Prompt Cues and Brand Visibility in AI Recommendations [exercise]
[figure: natural-language prompt modifier activating matching attribute and brand nodes in an AI assistant's associative network]
- In conversational search, retrieval cues move from visual shelf packaging into the exact words of the consumer's natural-language prompt.
- Modifiers in the prompt, such as "budget-friendly," "durable," "hypoallergenic," or "for small apartments," alter which brand nodes receive the highest probability during generation.
- Brands that have built clear, verifiable associations with specific category entry points and consumer problems are retrieved across a wider range of user prompts.
- Conversely, brands that rely solely on vague image advertising without clear attribute documentation in text and reviews lose visibility in conversational retrieval.
- Marketing management now includes auditing which prompts retrieve the brand across major AI assistants and strengthening the factual documentation linking the brand to core consumer needs.

## Discussion: Diagnosing Brand Omission in Conversational Search
- Discussion: A thirty-year-old mattress manufacturer has high aided brand recognition in consumer surveys because of decades of television jingles, yet the brand rarely appears when shoppers ask conversational AI assistants for "the best mattress for side sleepers with lower-back pain." Using associative networks, retrieval cues, and how language models learn brand links, explain why high human recognition does not guarantee AI retrieval, and recommend a strategy to close this gap.

## Key Takeaways
- Consumer learning is a relatively permanent change in knowledge or behavior resulting from direct product experience or indirect observation.
- Classical conditioning builds affective brand associations by pairing a neutral brand stimulus with an unconditioned stimulus, while balancing repetition against wear-out and managing stimulus generalization versus discrimination.
- Operant conditioning shapes repeat behavior through positive reinforcement, negative reinforcement, punishment, and reinforcement schedules, while observational learning operates through attention, retention, production, and motivation.
- The human memory system processes information across sensory memory, capacity-limited working memory (aided by chunking), and long-term episodic and semantic memory.
- Long-term brand knowledge is structured as an associative network in which retrieval cues trigger spreading activation, while decay and proactive or retroactive interference cause forgetting.
- When consumers use conversational AI assistants, the prompt acts as a retrieval cue that generates the consideration set from the model's learned text associations and retrieved product records.

## Title Slide
- Week 03
- Brands live in the brain. How people learn, remember, and forget shapes every purchase.

## What Is Consumer Learning? [no-quiz]
- Learning is a permanent change in behavior caused by experience.
- The experience can be direct. You try a new snack and like the taste.
- The experience can also be indirect. You see an ad or watch a friend buy running shoes.
- Learning is an ongoing process. Consumers constantly update their knowledge when they see new products.
- Marketers study learning so that brand names become automatic habits in the mind of the buyer.

## Classical Conditioning: Pairing Stimuli [quiz]
[figure: ConditioningFlow — pairing unconditioned and conditioned stimuli to produce a conditioned response]
- Classical conditioning happens when a stimulus that brings a natural response pairs with a neutral stimulus.
- Ivan Pavlov first demonstrated this with dogs, meat powder, and a bell.
- In marketing, an unconditioned stimulus like upbeat music naturally triggers positive feelings.
- A brand pairs this music with its logo or product repeatedly.
- Over time, seeing the brand alone produces the positive feeling. This is a conditioned response.

## Repetition and Stimulus Generalization [quiz]
[figure: GeneralizationChart — stimulus generalization and discrimination in packaging design]
- Conditioning needs repetition. Repeated exposures prevent memory decay and strengthen the link.
- Too much repetition causes advertising wear-out. Consumers tune out or get annoyed.
- Stimulus generalization happens when similar stimuli trigger the same learned response.
- Store brands use this principle. They copy the package colors and fonts of national brand leaders.
- Stimulus discrimination occurs when a brand teaches consumers to spot unique differences between products.

## Instrumental Conditioning: Rewards and Punishments
[figure: InstrumentalMatrix — positive reinforcement, negative reinforcement, and punishment]
- Instrumental conditioning is also called operant conditioning.
- People learn to perform behaviors that produce positive outcomes and avoid negative outcomes.
- Positive reinforcement rewards good behavior. Loyalty points and thank-you discounts encourage repeat visits.
- Negative reinforcement shows how a product removes a negative state. Taking medicine removes a headache.
- Punishment occurs when an unpleasant event follows an action. Late fees teach consumers not to pay bills late.

## Observational Learning: Watching Others [quiz]
- Consumers do not learn only from personal rewards. They also learn by watching other people.
- This process is called observational learning or modeling.
- First, the consumer directs attention to an attractive or credible model.
- Second, the consumer remembers what the model says or does.
- Third, the consumer converts this memory into action by purchasing the same clothing, phone, or car.

## The Memory System: Three Stores [quiz]
[figure: MemoryStorageFlow — sensory memory, short-term memory, and long-term memory stages]
- Memory is the process of acquiring information and storing it over time for later use.
- Sensory memory holds brief sensory inputs for a few seconds. If an ad gets attention, it moves forward.
- Short-term memory holds a small amount of information in active consciousness for about twenty seconds.
- Chunking helps short-term memory. Brands group phone numbers or slogan words into memorable chunks.
- Long-term memory retains information for days, months, or years through elaborative rehearsal.

## Associative Networks and Brand Nodes
[figure: BrandAssociativeNetwork — central brand node surrounded by interconnected attribute and emotion nodes]
- Long-term memory stores knowledge as an associative network.
- Think of memory as a spider web of connected nodes. Each node represents a concept, brand, or feeling.
- When you think of a brand like Nike, related nodes light up: running, sneakers, athletes, and Just Do It.
- Spreading activation happens when one memory node triggers nearby connected nodes.
- Strong positive links make a brand easy to recall when standing in a store aisle.

## Brand Retrieval and Cues in the Aisle
- Having information in memory is useless if the buyer cannot retrieve it at the point of purchase.
- Retrieval is the process of accessing information from long-term memory.
- Retrieval cues trigger brand memories. Packaging shapes, colors, and logos act as visual retrieval cues.
- If an ad shows a distinct green box, using that exact green box on the shelf helps shoppers find the product.
- State-dependent retrieval means buyers remember ad messages better when their internal mood matches the ad.

## Why Consumers Forget
- Forgetting is normal. Memory traces fade over time through decay.
- Interference also causes forgetting. New brand ads displace memories of older brand messages.
- Retroactive interference occurs when new learning displaces old information.
- Proactive interference occurs when older habits make it hard to learn a new brand name.
- Marketers fight forgetting with consistent visual identity, reminder ads, and clear shelf placement.

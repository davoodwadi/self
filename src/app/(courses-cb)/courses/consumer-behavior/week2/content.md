## Title Slide
- Week 2
- Perception is the process by which consumers select, organize, and interpret sensory inputs to construct meaning in the marketplace.

## Sensation Versus Perception [no-exercise]
[figure: progression from raw sensory inputs to selecting, organizing, and interpreting a coherent image]
- Sensation is the immediate response of sensory receptors in the eyes, ears, nose, mouth, and skin to basic stimuli such as light, sound, odor, and texture.
- Perception is the psychological process by which those raw sensations are selected, organized, and interpreted into a coherent picture of the environment.
- Consumers encounter thousands of commercial stimuli each day, far exceeding human information-processing capacity.
- Because interpretation depends on prior experience, expectations, and current needs, two consumers exposed to the same advertisement can construct different meanings from it.
- Marketing decisions therefore depend on how consumers perceive a product or price rather than on its objective physical properties alone.

## The Three Stages of the Perception Process [exercise]
[figure: three-stage perceptual funnel progressing from sensory exposure to selective attention and cognitive interpretation]
- Human perception unfolds across three sequential stages: exposure, attention, and interpretation.
- Exposure occurs when a stimulus comes within the detection range of a consumer's sensory receptors.
- Attention occurs when cognitive processing capacity is allocated to a specific stimulus among competing inputs.
- Interpretation occurs when the consumer assigns meaning to the attended stimulus by relating it to schemas stored in memory.
- Most marketing messages are filtered out at the exposure or attention stage before any interpretation takes place.

## Part 1: Sensory Systems and Sensory Marketing [no-exercise]
- Sensory marketing engages the consumer's five sensory systems in a deliberate way to shape brand associations, perceived quality, and purchase behavior.
- Each sensory channel processes commercial cues differently and contributes distinct information during shopping and product use.

## Vision and Auditory Cues in Brand Identity [no-exercise]
[figure: five sensory modalities in retail and product design: vision, audition, olfaction, haptics, and gustation]
- Vision is the dominant sensory channel in most retail categories, conveying brand identity through color, typography, package silhouette, and shelf contrast.
- Color associations are learned within a culture and category, allowing shoppers to identify product variants such as decaffeinated coffee or low-fat dairy at a glance.
- Auditory cues include sonic logos, advertising music, and the functional sounds a product makes during use, such as the click of a luxury compact or the crunch of a snack.
- Background music tempo in retail stores and restaurants influences how quickly consumers move through aisles and how much time they spend browsing.
- Consistent visual and auditory cues build distinctive brand assets that aid rapid recognition under low-involvement shopping conditions.

## Olfaction, Haptics, and Gustation in the Store Environment [exercise]
- Olfactory cues are processed through the limbic system, which links scent directly to emotion and episodic memory.
- Hotels, apparel retailers, and bakeries use ambient scent to increase lingering time and improve evaluations of store merchandise.
- Haptics refers to the sense of touch, which provides diagnostic information about material quality, weight, softness, and temperature.
- Physically holding a product in a store increases psychological ownership, making the shopper value the item more highly before purchasing it.
- Gustation combines taste receptor inputs with smell and texture to drive repeat purchase in food and beverage categories.

## Embodied Cognition and Cross-Modal Sensory Associations [no-exercise]
- Embodied cognition holds that bodily sensations influence abstract cognitive judgments even without conscious awareness.
- Consumers holding a warm cup of coffee tend to judge an unfamiliar person as more generous and trustworthy than consumers holding an iced drink.
- Cross-modal correspondence occurs when a cue in one sensory modality alters perception in another modality.
- Beverage consumers judge a drink served in a heavier glass or a textured bottle as richer and higher in quality than the identical liquid served in a thin plastic cup.
- Package designers coordinate visual color, surface texture, and container weight so that all sensory channels reinforce the same quality positioning.

## Part 2: Sensory Thresholds and Psychophysics [no-exercise]
- Psychophysics studies the quantitative relationship between the physical intensity of a stimulus and the sensory response it produces in an observer.
- Understanding sensory thresholds tells marketers how strong a signal must be to register and how much a stimulus must change before buyers notice the difference.

## Absolute and Differential Thresholds [exercise]
[figure: stimuli of rising intensity crossing the absolute threshold from undetected to detected]
- The absolute threshold is the minimum intensity of stimulation that can be detected on a given sensory channel 50 percent of the time.
- Highway billboard lettering that is too small for the viewing distance or an audio notification masked by street noise falls below the absolute threshold and fails to achieve exposure.
- The differential threshold is the ability of a sensory system to detect a difference between two stimuli of different intensities.
- The Just Noticeable Difference (JND) is the minimum change in stimulus intensity required for a consumer to detect that a change has occurred.
- Whether a consumer detects a price adjustment, a package size reduction, or a recipe reformulation depends on whether the change crosses the JND.

## Weber's Law and Proportional Change [exercise]
[figure: comparison of price and package changes illustrating Weber's Law as a constant proportion of the initial stimulus intensity]
- Formulated by Ernst Weber, Weber's Law states that the size of the Just Noticeable Difference is a constant proportion of the original stimulus intensity rather than a fixed absolute amount.
- The relationship is expressed as $\Delta I / I = k$, where $I$ is the initial stimulus intensity, $\Delta I$ is the minimum noticeable change, and $k$ is a constant for that sensory dimension.
- A 2-dollar reduction on a 10-dollar fast-food meal represents a 20 percent change and is immediately noticeable to buyers.
- The same 2-dollar reduction on a 1,000-dollar television represents a 0.2 percent change and falls well below the differential threshold.
- Retailers therefore express promotions in percentage terms on low-priced items and in dollar terms on high-priced items to make the discount feel substantial.

## Strategic Applications of the Just Noticeable Difference [no-exercise]
[figure: package weight reduction and minor price increase kept below the Just Noticeable Difference threshold]
- Marketers deliberately calibrate stimulus changes either below or above the JND depending on whether the change is unfavorable or favorable to the consumer.
- When input costs rise and a manufacturer reduces net package weight in a practice known as shrinkflation, the reduction is kept below the JND so habitual buyers do not switch brands.
- When a brand updates a classic logo to look more contemporary without alienating loyal buyers, designers apply a series of incremental changes that each stay below the JND.
- Conversely, when a brand introduces a promotional discount, increases package volume, or launches a product improvement, the change is designed to exceed the JND clearly.
- Failing to exceed the JND on a costly product improvement wastes engineering and promotional budget on a benefit consumers never register.

## Discussion: Redesigning a Package Without Losing Recognition
- Discussion: A heritage oat-cereal brand with an aging customer base wants to modernize its bright yellow box and reduce net package weight from 500 grams to 450 grams to offset grain cost inflation. How do the absolute threshold, the Just Noticeable Difference, and Weber's Law guide how the brand should execute the visual redesign versus the weight reduction, and at what point does staying below the JND raise ethical concerns?

## Part 3: Selective Exposure, Attention, and Adaptation [no-exercise]
- Passing the absolute threshold guarantees only that a stimulus can be detected, not that a consumer will attend to it.
- Consumers actively filter commercial environments through selective exposure, perceptual vigilance, perceptual defense, and adaptation.

## Sensory Overload and Selective Exposure [no-exercise]
[figure: shopper surrounded by competing advertisements, promotional signs, and digital notifications illustrating sensory overload]
- Sensory overload occurs when the volume of environmental and digital stimuli exceeds a person's cognitive capacity to process them.
- To cope with overload, consumers practice selective exposure by actively choosing which media channels, websites, and store aisles they encounter or avoid.
- Viewers skip pre-roll video advertisements, install browser ad blockers, and fast-forward through broadcast commercial breaks.
- Selective exposure means that reach figures based on media placement overstate actual consumer exposure to the brand message.
- Marketers counter selective exposure by placing messages inside relevant editorial content, search results, or opt-in communications that match the consumer's current task.

## Perceptual Vigilance and Perceptual Defense [exercise]
[figure: perceptual defense illustrated by a smoker turning away from the warning label on a cigarette package]
- Perceptual vigilance is the heightened tendency of consumers to notice stimuli that relate to their current active needs or goals.
- A commuter whose car lease expires next month suddenly notices automobile dealerships and financing advertisements that were previously ignored.
- Perceptual defense is the opposite mechanism, in which consumers screen out or distort stimuli that threaten their existing beliefs, habits, or self-image.
- Heavy smokers frequently look away from graphic health warnings on cigarette packages or discount the personal relevance of the risk.
- Because attention is selective, the same public-health or commercial message achieves very different attention rates across segments with different baseline motives.

## Sensory Adaptation and Stimulus Contrast [no-exercise]
[figure: sensory adaptation over repeated exposures as a consumer notices a new billboard, grows familiar with it, and eventually ignores it]
- Sensory adaptation is the gradual decline in sensory responsiveness that occurs when a receptor is exposed to a constant, unchanging stimulus over time.
- A shopper notices a bakery's cinnamon aroma immediately upon entering the store, but stops perceiving the scent after ten minutes in the aisle.
- In advertising, repeated exposure to an identical banner or commercial leads to habituation, where consumers cease allocating attention to the familiar layout.
- Marketers restore attention by introducing stimulus contrast through unexpected size, color isolation, movement, novelty, or directional visual cues.
- Effective campaign management refreshes creative execution periodically while preserving core brand retrieval cues.

## Part 4: Perceptual Organization, Gestalt Principles, and Semiotics [no-exercise]
- Once a stimulus captures attention, the brain does not process individual lines, sounds, or words in isolation.
- Consumers organize sensory elements into recognizable wholes using Gestalt principles and interpret their symbolic meaning through cultural signs.

## Gestalt Principles of Perceptual Organization [exercise]
[figure: three visual panels demonstrating the Gestalt principles of closure, similarity, and figure-ground in brand design]
- Gestalt psychology proposes that people perceive organized patterns as unified wholes whose meaning exceeds the sum of their individual parts.
- The principle of closure states that perceivers mentally fill in missing gaps in an incomplete image or melody, which increases active cognitive engagement with a logo or slogan.
- The principle of similarity states that consumers group items that share visual attributes such as color, shape, or typography as belonging to the same product line.
- The principle of figure-ground states that the visual system separates a dominant focal object (the figure) from its surrounding context (the ground).
- Clear figure-ground separation ensures that the brand name and key benefit stand out immediately rather than blending into decorative background imagery.

## Expectation, Categorization, and Perceptual Inference [no-exercise]
- Interpretation combines bottom-up processing driven by raw stimulus features with top-down processing driven by the consumer's prior knowledge and expectations.
- Schema-based categorization occurs when a consumer assigns a new product to an existing mental category, which immediately transfers that category's expected attributes to the item.
- A beverage sold in a slim matte-black aluminum can is categorized as an energy drink, whereas a beverage in a green glass bottle is categorized as a sparkling mixer or craft soda.
- Perceptual inference occurs when consumers form beliefs about unobserved product attributes, such as durability or natural ingredients, based on visible packaging cues.
- When a product's packaging cues conflict with its actual category, consumers experience perceptual confusion and often pass over the item on the shelf.

## Semiotics: Object, Sign, and Interpretant [no-exercise]
[figure: semiotic triangle linking the product object, the visual sign in an advertisement, and the consumer's interpretant meaning]
- Semiotics is the study of how signs and symbols create and transmit meaning within a culture (Peirce, 1931).
- In marketing semiotics, every persuasive message links three elements: the object, the sign (or representamen), and the interpretant.
- The object is the actual product or brand being promoted, such as a rugged four-wheel-drive vehicle.
- The sign is the sensory image, word, or symbol used to represent the product's qualities, such as a mountain peak or a wild mustang.
- The interpretant is the mental meaning derived by the consumer, such as independence and resilience, which depends on the consumer's cultural background.

## Part 5: Machine Perception in Agent-Mediated Shopping [no-exercise]
- When a consumer delegates shopping to an AI agent, the initial stages of exposure, attention, and interpretation are performed by software rather than human sensory organs.
- Understanding how machine perception differs from human perception explains why visual merchandising alone cannot secure choice in agentic commerce.

## How AI Shopping Agents Perceive Products [no-exercise]
[figure: side-by-side comparison of human sensory perception of a package versus an AI agent reading structured product metadata and reviews]
- An AI shopping agent has no biological receptors for sight, sound, smell, touch, or taste.
- Instead, the agent perceives a product through digital tokens: product titles, structured specification tables, price fields, ingredient lists, and customer review text.
- While a human shopper in a grocery aisle perceives a cereal box through its shelf color and front-of-pack graphics, an AI agent reads its nutrition table, unit weight, and average star rating.
- Sensory marketing remains essential during the postpurchase stage, because the human consumer still eats, wears, or handles the physical product after delivery.
- During prepurchase search and purchase selection, however, an AI agent responds to machine-readable data rather than sensory atmosphere.

## Digital Exposure and Structured Product Data [no-exercise]
[figure: AI shopping agent receiving a product with complete structured metadata while an unindexed product with missing attribute fields fails to achieve exposure]
- For an AI shopping agent, exposure occurs only when a product's information is indexed, accessible via search or API, and included in the results returned to the agent.
- If a brand places critical attributes such as allergen warnings, garment measurements, or warranty length inside an unindexed promotional graphic, those attributes fall below the agent's threshold of exposure.
- Ambiguous or inconsistent attribute labels across retail listings act like sensory noise, preventing the agent from matching the item to the consumer's stated constraints.
- Providing complete, standardized schema markup ensures that the agent can detect and verify every relevant product attribute during screening.
- Clear digital exposure is therefore the algorithmic counterpart of placing a package in clear view on a well-lit retail shelf.

## Position Effects in Human and Algorithmic Attention [exercise]
[figure: choice share across shelf and list positions comparing human eye-level attention with primacy bias in AI shopping agents]
- Both human shoppers and AI shopping agents allocate uneven attention across positions in a display or list.
- In physical stores, human shoppers attend disproportionately to items placed at eye level and at the center of a shelf display, and in digital search they click the top results most often.
- Controlled experiments show that large language models acting as shopping agents also exhibit strong position effects when evaluating candidate lists (Wadi & Ma, 2026a).
- Depending on model architecture and list structure, AI agents display primacy bias toward options listed first or recency bias toward options listed last, even when product attributes are identical (Wadi & Ma, 2026a).
- Consequently, search ranking order and display position shape which products receive attention in both human and agent-mediated shopping.

## Discussion: Auditing Sensory and Machine Perception for a Beverage Launch
- Discussion: A specialty coffee roaster is launching a line of canned cold-brew coffee sold both in supermarket refrigerated cases and through online grocery platforms where customers use AI shopping assistants. Which sensory and Gestalt principles should guide the physical can design for in-store shoppers, and which structured data fields and ranking factors will determine whether an AI assistant attends to and selects the product online?

## Key Takeaways
- Sensation is the physical detection of stimuli by sensory receptors, whereas perception is the psychological process of selecting, organizing, and interpreting those inputs into meaning.
- Sensory marketing engages vision, hearing, smell, touch, and taste to shape brand associations, store lingering time, and perceived product quality.
- Psychophysics distinguishes the absolute threshold from the differential threshold (JND), and Weber's Law establishes that a noticeable change is a constant proportion of the initial stimulus intensity.
- Consumers manage sensory overload through selective exposure, heightened perceptual vigilance toward need-relevant cues, perceptual defense against threatening messages, and adaptation to constant stimuli.
- Gestalt principles (closure, similarity, figure-ground) and semiotic relationships (object, sign, interpretant) explain how consumers organize visual elements into coherent brand meanings.
- AI shopping agents perceive products through structured text and metadata rather than biological senses, yet both human shoppers and AI agents exhibit systematic position effects in attention and choice.

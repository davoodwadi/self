## Title Slide
- Week 05
- Consumers do not just buy products. They buy symbols that mirror who they are and who they hope to become.

## What Is the Self-Concept? [no-exercise]
- Self-concept is the collection of beliefs a person holds about their own attributes and qualities.
- It includes how we judge our own appearance, intellect, skills, and character.
- Self-esteem refers to the positivity of a person's self-concept.
- People with high self-esteem expect to succeed and take more risks when buying new products.
- People with low self-esteem try to avoid failure and seek reassurance through safe, well-known brands.

## The Actual Self Versus the Ideal Self [exercise]
- The actual self is our realistic appraisal of the qualities we have right now.
- The ideal self is our conception of who we would like to be.
- A gap between the actual self and the ideal self creates emotional tension.
- Consumers buy products to bridge this gap. This is called compensatory consumption.
- Fantasy appeals and aspirational advertising show consumers how a product brings them closer to their ideal self.

## The Extended Self: Possessions as Identity [exercise]
- We are what we own. External objects often become part of who we are.
- The extended self includes possessions that people use to define their social identity.
- Individual level: personal items like jewelry, cars, and clothing define personal identity.
- Family level: a consumer's home and furnishings represent family identity and shared memories.
- Community level: neighborhoods and hometowns shape local identity.
- Group level: attachments to sports teams, subcultures, or social movements define group identity.

## Identity and Automation: The Tasks Consumers Keep [exercise]
- Many products and AI tools now promise to do tasks for the consumer, from cooking to writing.
- Consumers do not always welcome this help.
- When a task is central to a person's identity, the person wants to feel that the result is their own work.
- Research shows that people who strongly identify with an activity, such as cooking, are less interested in products that automate the skills involved (Leung, Paolacci, & Puntoni, 2018).
- A passionate home baker may reject a machine that makes bread automatically, while a busy parent may welcome it.
- For the same reason, a consumer may let an AI agent handle routine purchases but keep the choices that express who they are.
- Marketers of automated products can leave room for the consumer's own contribution, so the consumer still feels ownership of the result.

## Personality Traits and Consumer Behavior
- Personality refers to a person's unique psychological makeup that consistently influences their responses to the environment.
- Trait theory views personality as a set of measurable characteristics.
- Innovativeness is the degree to which a person likes to try new things.
- Materialism is the emphasis a person places on owning worldly goods for status.
- Need for cognition is the degree to which a person enjoys thinking hard and reading detailed product descriptions.
- Frugality is the tendency to prioritize careful spending and resourcefulness over wasteful buying.

## The Big Five Personality Dimensions [exercise]
- Psychologists identify five fundamental dimensions of human personality called the Big Five.
- Openness to experience: curiosity, creativity, and interest in novelty.
- Conscientiousness: organization, self-discipline, and reliability.
- Extraversion: sociability, energy, and talkativeness in social settings.
- Agreeableness: friendliness, warmth, empathy, and cooperation with others.
- Neuroticism: emotional instability, tendency to experience anxiety, and mood swings.
- Marketers use these dimensions to tailor ad copy, visual tone, and brand messaging.

## Brand Personality: Giving Life to Objects [exercise]
- Brand personality is the set of human traits that consumers assign to a brand name.
- Sincerity: brands seen as down-to-earth, honest, wholesome, and cheerful, like Hallmark or Campbell's.
- Excitement: brands seen as daring, spirited, imaginative, and modern, like Apple or Red Bull.
- Competence: brands seen as reliable, intelligent, and successful, like Volvo or Google.
- Sophistication: brands seen as upper-class, elegant, and charming, like Chanel or Rolex.
- Ruggedness: brands seen as outdoorsy, tough, and durable, like Jeep or Patagonia.

## Anthropomorphism and Brand Relationships
- Anthropomorphism occurs when people assign human qualities, faces, or intentions to non-human objects.
- Mascots like the Michelin Man or the M&M characters make abstract corporate products feel friendly.
- When consumers view a brand as a human partner, brand loyalty turns into an emotional relationship.
- Brand love occurs when a consumer feels passion, commitment, and positive attachment toward a brand.
- When an anthropomorphized brand fails, consumers feel personal betrayal rather than simple dissatisfaction.

## Psychographics and Lifestyles: Measuring AIOs [exercise]
- Demographics tell us who buys. Psychographics tell us why they buy.
- Lifestyle defines a pattern of consumption that reflects a person's choices about how they spend time and money.
- Psychographics uses psychological, sociological, and anthropological factors to segment markets.
- Activities focus on work, hobbies, social events, vacations, and entertainment.
- Interests focus on family, home, job, community, food, and fashion.
- Opinions focus on oneself, social issues, politics, business, and products.

## The VALS Segmentation System
- The Values and Lifestyles system, or VALS, divides adults into eight consumer segments based on psychological traits and resources.
- Innovators are successful, sophisticated people with high resources and abundant energy.
- Thinkers and Believers are motivated by ideals, knowledge, and principles.
- Achievers and Strivers are motivated by achievement, status, and recognition from peers.
- Experiencers and Makers are motivated by self-expression, physical activity, and adventure.
- Survivors have the fewest resources and focus on meeting basic needs rather than expressing lifestyle.

## Synthetic Consumers: AI as a Survey Respondent [exercise]
- Psychographic research depends on asking consumers about their activities, interests, and opinions.
- Some researchers now ask large language models to answer surveys as if they were consumers with a given profile. These answers are called synthetic responses.
- Synthetic responses are fast and cheap. Early studies found that they can mirror some patterns in real consumer data, such as sensitivity to price (Brand, Israeli, & Ngwe, 2023).
- Synthetic responses also have limits. The same model can give a different answer each time it is asked the same question (Wadi & Fredette, 2025).
- Models can also show biases, such as anchoring on a number in the question (Wadi & Fredette, 2025) or favoring products from some countries over others (Wadi, Ghodrat, & Philp, 2026).
- A synthetic consumer has no lived experience of buying or using a product.
- Synthetic responses can help researchers design and pretest a survey. Decisions about real consumers still need data from real consumers.

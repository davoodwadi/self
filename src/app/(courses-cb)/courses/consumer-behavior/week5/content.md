## Title Slide
- Week 5
- Consumers select products and brands not only for functional performance, but also to express their self-concept, personality traits, and lifestyle patterns.

## Defining the Self-Concept and Self-Esteem [no-exercise]
- The self-concept is the totality of an individual's beliefs, thoughts, and evaluations regarding their own personal attributes, roles, and capabilities.
- It encompasses physical appearance, intellectual abilities, social roles, and moral character.
- Self-esteem refers to the overall positivity or negativity of a person's self-concept.
- Consumers with high self-esteem generally expect social acceptance and are more willing to experiment with novel or unconventional products.
- Consumers with low self-esteem are motivated to avoid social rejection and tend to prefer established, socially safe brands.

## Multiple Selves and Situational Self-Image [no-exercise]
- Rather than holding a single monolithic identity, consumers maintain multiple role identities that become active in different social contexts (Markus & Nurius, 1986).
- The same individual may act as a conservative financial analyst at work, an adventurous trail runner on weekends, and a nurturing parent at home.
- Each active role activates a distinct situational self-image and a corresponding set of appropriate products, clothing, and brands.
- The looking-glass self describes the process of imagining how others evaluate us and adjusting our behavior and possessions to manage that impression (Cooley, 1902).
- Marketing communications succeed when they cue the specific situational identity that is relevant to the product's usage occasion.

## Part 1: Self-Concept, Symbolic Consumption, and the Extended Self [no-exercise]
- Symbolic consumption occurs when buyers evaluate products based on what those products communicate to themselves and others about who they are.
- Gap reduction between the actual and ideal self, self-image congruence, and the extended self explain how possessions become part of personal identity.

## The Actual Self, the Ideal Self, and Compensatory Consumption [exercise]
[figure: discrepancy between the actual self and the ideal self bridged through aspirational or compensatory consumption]
- The actual self is a person's realistic appraisal of the qualities and attributes they currently possess.
- The ideal self is the person's conception of how they would like to be, shaped by cultural heroes, peers, and advertising models.
- When a consumer experiences a threat or discrepancy between the actual self and the ideal self on an important dimension, self-discrepancy tension arises (Higgins, 1987).
- Compensatory consumption is the acquisition and display of symbolic goods to offset a perceived self-deficit, such as buying high-status apparel when professional competence feels threatened.
- Realistic marketing appeals target the actual self with everyday relatability, whereas aspirational appeals depict how a brand moves the buyer toward the ideal self.

## Self-Image Congruence Theory [no-exercise]
- Self-image congruence theory states that consumers prefer brands whose perceived image matches their own self-concept (Sirgy, 1982).
- For privately consumed utilitarian products, evaluation is guided primarily by functional congruence between product performance and practical requirements.
- For publicly visible products such as automobiles, watches, and apparel, evaluation depends heavily on congruence with the social or ideal self.
- Symbolic self-completion theory adds that individuals who feel incomplete in a newly adopted role, such as a novice golfer or first-year executive, rely most heavily on visible role symbols (Wicklund & Gollwitzer, 1982).
- Experienced experts with secure role identities feel less need to display conspicuous beginner equipment to prove their membership.

## The Extended Self Across Four Levels of Identity [exercise]
[figure: four concentric levels of the extended self: individual possessions, family home, neighborhood community, and social groups]
- Formulated by Russell Belk, the extended self encompasses the external objects, places, and possessions that consumers functionally and emotionally integrate into their identity (Belk, 1988).
- At the individual level, personal items such as clothing, jewelry, smartphones, and automobiles serve as direct extensions of the person.
- At the family level, the residence and its furnishings represent shared household identity and continuity across generations.
- At the community level, attachment to a neighborhood, city, or region shapes local civic identity and consumption habits.
- At the group level, shared symbols such as sports team jerseys, university insignia, and subcultural artifacts anchor collective belonging.

## Part 2: Identity-Based Consumption and Resistance to Automation [no-exercise]
- Modern appliances, software tools, and AI agents increasingly offer to automate tasks that consumers previously performed by hand.
- Whether consumers welcome or reject automation depends on whether the automated task is central to their self-concept.

## Identity Relevance and Internal Attribution of Effort [no-exercise]
- Consumers derive identity value from an activity only when they can attribute the outcome to their own skill, effort, and judgment.
- A home cook who prepares a complex meal from scratch experiences pride and self-expression because the finished dish reflects personal competence.
- If a fully automated machine performs the core skill steps of chopping, seasoning, and cooking, the consumer can no longer claim authorship of the outcome.
- Consequently, full automation severs the link between task completion and identity expression.
- This mechanism explains why historical products such as instant cake mixes initially met resistance when they removed too much of the baker's perceived contribution.

## Consumer Resistance to Automating Identity-Relevant Tasks [exercise]
[figure: adoption intention for automated versus manual products among consumers with high versus low identity investment in the task]
- Empirical research by Leung, Paolacci, and Puntoni (2018) demonstrates that consumers who strongly identify with a domain resist products that automate skill-based tasks within that domain.
- Across studies of cooking, baking, driving, and gardening, non-identifiers welcomed labor-saving automation because their primary goal was convenience.
- In contrast, strong identifiers rejected automated features that replaced the core skill of the activity, preferring manual control even when it required greater effort (Leung, Paolacci, & Puntoni, 2018).
- Identifiers did not reject all technology: they accepted tools that assisted peripheral preparation without replacing the central skill step.
- Resistance to automation is therefore driven by the desire to protect self-attribution rather than by general technophobia.

## Designing Automated Products and AI Delegation Around Identity [no-exercise]
- The same identity mechanism governs how consumers adopt AI assistants and autonomous shopping agents.
- A consumer who views fashion curation, vinyl record collecting, or gift selection as central to personal identity will resist delegating those choices to an AI agent.
- Conversely, the same consumer will readily let an AI agent automate non-identity tasks such as comparing utility tariffs or reordering household cleaning supplies.
- Marketers introducing automated products or AI tools into identity-relevant categories can preserve adoption by positioning the tool as a collaborator that leaves final creative control to the user.
- Allowing the consumer to customize parameters or execute the signature step preserves psychological ownership of the result.

## Part 3: Human Personality Traits and Brand Personality [no-exercise]
- Beyond the self-concept, individuals exhibit enduring psychological predispositions that shape how they respond to novelty, risk, and persuasive messages.
- Consumers also project human personality dimensions onto brands, forming relationships with corporate symbols.

## Trait Theory and Consumer-Specific Traits [no-exercise]
- Personality is a person's distinctive psychological makeup that consistently influences how the individual responds to their environment over time.
- Trait theory focuses on the quantitative measurement of specific psychological dimensions across individuals.
- Consumer innovativeness is the degree to which a person is receptive to new products, services, and experiences rather than remaining attached to familiar options.
- Need for cognition (NFC) measures the degree to which an individual enjoys effortful cognitive activity, determining whether they prefer detailed technical copy or peripheral visual cues (Cacioppo & Petty, 1982).
- Materialism measures the importance a consumer attaches to worldly possessions as a source of happiness and social status, while frugality measures disciplined restraint in acquiring and using economic goods.

## The Five-Factor Model of Personality [exercise]
[figure: five dimensions of the Big Five personality model: openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, and neuroticism]
- Contemporary personality psychology converges on the Five-Factor Model (the Big Five) as the primary taxonomy of human traits (McCrae & Costa, 1987).
- Openness to experience captures intellectual curiosity, aesthetic sensitivity, imagination, and preference for variety.
- Conscientiousness captures organization, self-discipline, dependability, and goal-directed planning.
- Extraversion captures sociability, assertiveness, positive energy, and excitement-seeking in social environments.
- Agreeableness captures empathy, interpersonal trust, warmth, and cooperative orientation, while neuroticism captures emotional instability and proneness to anxiety or stress.

## Brand Personality Dimensions and Anthropomorphism [exercise]
[figure: Aaker's five brand personality dimensions: sincerity, excitement, competence, sophistication, and ruggedness]
- Brand personality is the set of human characteristics permanently associated with a brand name in consumer memory (Aaker, 1997).
- Jennifer Aaker identified five core dimensions of brand personality: sincerity (down-to-earth, honest, wholesome), excitement (daring, spirited, imaginative), competence (reliable, intelligent, successful), sophistication (upper-class, charming), and ruggedness (outdoorsy, tough).
- Anthropomorphism occurs when marketers endow a brand or product with human physical features, voices, or intentions, as in brand mascots or conversational assistants.
- Anthropomorphized brands foster stronger emotional attachment and brand love when performance is high.
- However, when an anthropomorphized brand commits a service failure or ethical transgression, consumers judge it as a deliberate betrayal rather than a mechanical error.

## Part 4: Lifestyles, Psychographics, and VALS [no-exercise]
- Two consumers with identical demographic profiles in age, income, and education frequently purchase entirely different brands and leisure experiences.
- Psychographics combines psychology and demographics to measure how consumers express their identities through everyday lifestyles.

## Lifestyles Versus Demographics in Market Segmentation [no-exercise]
- A lifestyle is a pattern of consumption reflecting a person's choices about how to allocate time, money, and attention across products and activities.
- Demographic variables describe who buys a product objectively, making them useful for buying media space and estimating census market size.
- Psychographic variables explain why those buyers choose a specific brand, providing the insights needed to craft advertising tone, imagery, and product positioning.
- For example, two 35-year-old households earning 110,000 dollars a year may diverge completely between urban culinary exploration and suburban home renovation.
- Effective segmentation pairs demographic reach data with psychographic lifestyle profiles.

## Measuring Activities, Interests, and Opinions [exercise]
[figure: psychographic AIO framework combining activities, interests, and opinions with demographic variables]
- Quantitative psychographic studies typically administer large-scale surveys measuring Activities, Interests, and Opinions, known as AIO inventories.
- Activities measure observable behaviors showing how consumers spend their time, including hobbies, sports, shopping routines, entertainment, and community work.
- Interests measure the degree of excitement and ongoing attention a consumer devotes to topics such as food, fashion, technology, family, or fitness.
- Opinions measure descriptive beliefs and normative views regarding oneself, social issues, politics, business practices, and future economic trends.
- Clustering respondents across hundreds of AIO statements reveals distinct lifestyle segments that transcend single product categories.

## The VALS Psychographic Segmentation System [no-exercise]
[figure: VALS segmentation matrix organizing eight consumer segments by primary motivation and resource level]
- Developed by SRI International, the Values and Lifestyles (VALS) system classifies adults into eight segments organized along two dimensions: primary motivation and resources.
- The three primary motivations are ideals (guided by knowledge and principles, splitting into Thinkers and Believers), achievement (guided by social approval and success, splitting into Achievers and Strivers), and self-expression (guided by action, variety, and physical risk, splitting into Experiencers and Makers).
- The vertical axis measures psychological, physical, and financial resources, ranging from Innovators at the top to Survivors at the base.
- Innovators possess abundant resources and high innovativeness, adopting premium and emerging technologies across all three motivational orientations.
- Survivors possess the lowest resources and exhibit cautious, brand-loyal behavior focused on meeting immediate safety and physiological needs.

## Part 5: Synthetic Consumers in Psychographic Research [no-exercise]
- Because administering large psychographic and attitude surveys to thousands of human respondents is costly and slow, researchers have begun testing large language models as simulated respondents.
- Evaluating synthetic consumers requires comparing where model outputs match human patterns and where they fail systematically.

## Large Language Models as Simulated Survey Respondents [no-exercise]
- A synthetic respondent (or silicon sample) is created by conditioning a large language model on a demographic and psychographic persona and asking it to answer marketing survey questions.
- Early studies demonstrated that language models prompted with consumer profiles can reproduce certain aggregate directional patterns, such as downward-sloping demand curves and established brand preferences (Brand, Israeli, & Ngwe, 2023).
- Synthetic surveys can be executed in minutes at a fraction of the cost of commercial online panels.
- Because the model has read vast corpora of consumer text, its responses often reflect common cultural associations between demographic traits and product preferences.
- However, plausibility at the aggregate level does not guarantee psychometric validity or fidelity to real consumer populations.

## Response Instability and Systematic Biases in Synthetic Respondents [exercise]
[figure: comparison of human survey response distributions and repeated LLM synthetic responses showing within-prompt variance and anchoring shifts]
- Empirical evaluations reveal major psychometric limitations when large language models are used as survey respondents.
- First, models exhibit stochastic instability: asked the identical survey question repeatedly under the same persona, the same model can generate substantially different ratings across runs (Wadi & Fredette, 2025).
- Second, models exhibit strong sensitivity to irrelevant numerical anchors embedded in the prompt, with newer model releases sometimes displaying larger anchoring biases than older releases (Wadi & Fredette, 2025).
- Third, models exhibit systematic cultural and country-of-origin biases that vary by the developer's home region and the country named in the survey item (Wadi, Ghodrat, & Philp, 2026).
- Unlike a human respondent, a language model has no lived physiological or emotional experience of purchasing, owning, or consuming a product.

## Appropriate Boundaries for Synthetic Consumer Research [no-exercise]
- Because of response variance and training-corpus biases, synthetic respondents cannot replace human samples for final segmentation sizing, pricing validation, or new-product forecasting.
- Synthetic distributions also tend to compress minority viewpoints and under-represent segments whose behaviors are less documented in online text.
- Synthetic respondents are methodologically useful during the exploratory stage of research: pre-testing survey wording, checking for confusing scale items, and generating initial hypotheses.
- When researchers do evaluate model outputs on rating scales, they must sample responses repeatedly (Wadi & Fredette, 2025) or inspect token probability distributions rather than relying on a single completion (Wadi, Ghodrat, & Philp, 2026).
- Substantive marketing decisions about human consumers still require empirical verification on human respondents.

## Discussion: Evaluating a Synthetic Psychographic Study
- Discussion: A consumer packaged goods firm plans to launch a line of functional probiotic sodas in Canada, France, and Japan. An external consultancy proposes skipping human AIO and VALS surveys altogether and instead interviewing 5,000 LLM-generated personas across the three countries to select the brand personality and price point. ## Key Takeaways
- The self-concept comprises the beliefs a consumer holds about their attributes, and discrepancies between the actual self and the ideal self motivate aspirational and compensatory consumption.
- Through self-image congruence and the extended self (individual, family, community, and group levels), external possessions become central markers of identity.
- Consumers who strongly identify with a domain resist products and AI tools that automate the core skill-based steps of that activity because automation prevents internal attribution of the outcome.
- Trait theories (innovativeness, need for cognition, materialism, and the Big Five) measure enduring human predispositions, while brand personality (sincerity, excitement, competence, sophistication, ruggedness) and anthropomorphism describe human traits projected onto brands.
- Psychographic segmentation uses Activities, Interests, and Opinions (AIO) inventories and frameworks such as VALS to explain why demographically similar consumers pursue different lifestyles.
- Large language models used as synthetic survey respondents can aid exploratory survey pre-testing, but stochastic variability, anchoring sensitivity, and cultural bias prevent them from replacing human consumer data.

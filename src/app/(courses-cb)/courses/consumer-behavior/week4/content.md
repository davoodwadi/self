## Title Slide
- Week 4
- Consumer motivation arises when a discrepancy between a present state and a desired state creates psychological tension that directs behavior toward a goal.

## Defining Consumer Motivation [no-exercise]
[figure: motivation cycle from an unmet need and psychological tension to goal-directed behavior and tension reduction]
- Motivation is the internal process that initiates, directs, and sustains goal-oriented behavior in consumers.
- It begins when a consumer recognizes an unfulfilled need, defined as a discrepancy between the consumer's current state and a desired ideal state.
- This discrepancy produces an uncomfortable internal state of arousal referred to as tension.
- To reduce the tension, the consumer expends energy searching for, purchasing, and using products or services expected to close the gap.
- Identical purchasing behavior across two buyers can reflect entirely different underlying motives, such as buying a bicycle for commuting savings, cardiovascular health, or environmental values.

## Motivational Strength and Motivational Direction [no-exercise]
- Every motivational state has two distinct properties: strength and direction.
- Motivational strength is the degree of energy a person is willing to expend to reach a goal, which depends on the size and importance of the unmet need.
- Motivational direction is the specific pathway or product choice the consumer selects to satisfy that need.
- While basic biological needs determine the existence of tension, cultural norms, social learning, and marketing communications shape motivational direction.
- Marketers cannot create primary physiological needs, but they design products and messages that channel motivational direction toward a specific brand solution.

## Part 1: Drive Theory, Expectancy Theory, and Goal Pursuit [no-exercise]
- Psychological explanations of consumer motivation draw on both biological push models and cognitive pull models.
- Comparing drive theory and expectancy theory explains why some purchases are triggered by immediate bodily discomfort while others are guided by anticipated future outcomes.

## Drive Theory and Homeostasis [exercise]
- Formulated in behavioral psychology by Clark Hull, drive theory states that biological needs produce unpleasant internal states of arousal called drives.
- Behavior is motivated by the organism's effort to reduce this internal tension and return to homeostasis, a balanced physiological equilibrium.
- When body temperature drops or blood sugar falls, homeostasis is disrupted, generating a drive to purchase warm clothing or food.
- Once the purchase or consumption restores equilibrium, the drive subsides until the need recurs.
- Drive theory explains routine replenishment purchases well, but it struggles to explain why sated consumers eat dessert or undertake physically taxing adventure travel.

## Expectancy Theory: Expectancy, Instrumentality, and Valence [exercise]
[figure: expectancy theory chain linking consumer effort to expected performance, instrumentality, and outcome valence]
- Expectancy theory proposes that motivation is pulled by cognitive expectations of achieving desirable future outcomes rather than pushed solely by biological deficits (Vroom, 1964).
- Expectancy is the consumer's belief that expending effort, such as following a training program or using a study app, will result in successful performance.
- Instrumentality is the belief that achieving that performance level will lead to a specific outcome, such as career advancement or health improvement.
- Valence is the subjective value or attractiveness the consumer assigns to that final outcome.
- Motivation is high only when all three components are positive: the goal appears attainable, the product provides an effective path, and the reward is valued.

## Goal Systems and Means-End Chains [no-exercise]
- Consumers organize their motives in hierarchical goal systems that connect concrete product attributes to abstract personal goals.
- The means-end chain model links three levels of knowledge: concrete product attributes, functional and psychosocial consequences, and personal values (Gutman, 1982).
- For example, a consumer notices the attribute "lightweight titanium frame" on a pair of eyeglasses, links it to the consequence "comfortable all-day wear during meetings," and connects that consequence to the value "professional accomplishment."
- Researchers uncover these links using laddering interviews, which repeatedly ask why an attribute matters to the buyer.
- Advertising is most persuasive when it connects tangible product features to the higher-order consequences and values that drive the segment.

## Part 2: Classification of Needs and Values [no-exercise]
- Human needs vary from universal biological requirements to culturally learned psychological desires.
- Classifying needs and values allows marketers to identify which level of benefit a product category addresses.

## Biogenic, Psychogenic, Utilitarian, and Hedonic Needs [exercise]
- Biogenic needs are innate physiological requirements necessary to sustain life, including food, water, air, shelter, and sleep.
- Psychogenic needs are acquired in the process of becoming a member of a culture, including needs for status, power, affiliation, and achievement (Murray, 1938).
- Utilitarian needs direct consumers to emphasize objective, tangible product attributes such as fuel economy, durability, calories, or storage capacity.
- Hedonic needs are subjective and experiential, motivating consumers to seek excitement, sensory pleasure, fantasy, or aesthetic enjoyment.
- Many categories combine both: a winter coat satisfies a utilitarian need for thermal insulation alongside a hedonic or psychogenic need for style and status.

## Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs in Consumer Markets [exercise]
[figure: five-level hierarchy of needs from physiological and safety needs to belonging, esteem, and self-actualization]
- Abraham Maslow arranged human needs into a five-tier hierarchy progressing from basic survival requirements to psychological growth (Maslow, 1943).
- Physiological needs occupy the base (food, water, rest), followed by safety needs (physical security, insurance, preventive healthcare).
- Belongingness and love needs occupy the middle tier (friendship, family acceptance, community clubs), followed by esteem needs (prestige, mastery, recognition).
- Self-actualization sits at the apex, representing the desire for self-fulfillment, creative expression, and personal growth.
- Although originally proposed as a strict progression, cross-cultural consumer research treats the hierarchy as a taxonomy of motives that can operate simultaneously within a single purchase.

## Core Cultural and Personal Values [no-exercise]
- A value is an enduring belief that a specific mode of conduct or end-state of existence is personally or socially preferable to an opposite mode or end-state (Rokeach, 1973).
- Terminal values are desired end-states of existence, such as family security, freedom, social recognition, or a comfortable life.
- Instrumental values are preferred modes of behavior used to achieve terminal values, such as ambition, honesty, frugality, or self-reliance.
- Unlike needs that fluctuate with immediate deprivation, values remain relatively stable over years and guide choices across many product categories.
- Consumers evaluate whether a brand's actions, sourcing practices, and messaging align with their core values when choosing among functionally similar alternatives.

## Part 3: Motivational Conflicts in Consumer Choice [no-exercise]
- Because every purchase involves trade-offs among competing goals, budgets, and risks, consumers frequently experience conflicting motives at the same time.
- Kurt Lewin classified motivational conflicts into three structural types according to whether the competing forces attract or repel the buyer.

## Approach-Approach Conflict and Cognitive Dissonance [no-exercise]
- An approach-approach conflict occurs when a consumer must choose between two desirable alternatives that cannot both be acquired within the same budget or time constraint.
- A graduate student with one week of vacation and a fixed budget may be torn between attending a music festival in Montreal and taking a hiking trip in Banff.
- Because both options carry positive valence, the consumer hesitates before deciding and often experiences post-decision regret over the foregone benefits of the rejected option.
- Marketers resolve approach-approach conflicts by offering product bundles, modular upgrades, or flexible financing that allow buyers to obtain benefits from both options.
- Highlighting a unique attribute on which the promoted brand clearly dominates also helps the buyer justify closing the decision.

## Approach-Avoidance and Avoidance-Avoidance Conflicts [exercise]
[figure: three diagrams contrasting approach-approach, approach-avoidance, and avoidance-avoidance motivational conflicts]
- An approach-avoidance conflict occurs when a single product or action possesses both desirable outcomes that attract the consumer and undesirable outcomes that repel the consumer.
- A shopper may desire the taste and indulgence of rich ice cream while wishing to avoid caloric intake, or admire a luxury coat while feeling guilt over its price.
- An avoidance-avoidance conflict occurs when a consumer is forced to choose between two undesirable alternatives, each carrying a negative consequence.
- A homeowner facing a broken furnace must choose between paying thousands of dollars for a replacement and enduring a cold house while paying for repeated emergency repairs.
- Each conflict structure requires a distinct marketing intervention to reduce the negative vector holding back purchase.

## Resolving Motivational Conflicts Through Product and Pricing Design [no-exercise]
- In approach-avoidance conflicts, marketers reduce the avoidance force by reformulating the product or restructuring the terms of purchase.
- Zero-sugar beverages, plant-based meats, and energy-efficient appliances are designed specifically to preserve the approach benefit while minimizing health or environmental guilt.
- Money-back guarantees, free return shipping, and certified pre-owned warranties reduce the financial and performance avoidance forces associated with high-ticket purchases.
- In avoidance-avoidance conflicts, communications emphasize peace of mind, predictable installment payments, and hassle-free installation to make one option substantially less painful.
- Removing the specific friction that generates avoidance is often more effective than adding new promotional claims to the approach side.

## Part 4: Intrinsic and Extrinsic Motivation in Online Review Writing [no-exercise]
- Postpurchase behaviors such as writing online product reviews are driven by a combination of internal psychological motives and external firm-sponsored incentives.
- Because reviews guide both human shoppers and AI shopping agents, firms must design solicitation messages and rewards that do not backfire.

## Intrinsic Versus Extrinsic Motivation and Crowding Out [exercise]
[figure: comparison of intrinsic altruistic motivation and extrinsic financial incentives showing crowding out when a prosocial act is monetized]
- Intrinsic motivation occurs when an individual engages in an activity for its inherent satisfaction, enjoyment, or prosocial meaning, such as helping fellow shoppers make informed choices.
- Extrinsic motivation occurs when behavior is driven by an external contingency separated from the activity itself, such as cash rebates, loyalty points, or sweepstakes entries.
- According to self-determination and motivation crowding theory, introducing an external monetary reward for a prosocial behavior can crowd out intrinsic motivation (Deci, Koestner, & Ryan, 1999).
- When a firm pays customers to write a review, an act that previously felt like a helpful community contribution can be reframed as a commercial transaction for the firm.
- Consequently, the wording of the review request determines whether a financial incentive complements or undermines the consumer's intrinsic motive.

## Altruistic Versus Company-Focused Review Appeals [no-exercise]
- Retailers typically solicit reviews with one of two message frames: an altruistic appeal ("help other shoppers decide") or a company-focused appeal ("help our company improve").
- In a large-scale field study with a North American online retailer, asking customers to help other shoppers significantly increased the likelihood of submitting a review compared with asking them to help the company (Wadi et al., 2026a).
- In a controlled follow-up experiment, offering a 10-dollar store credit increased review intentions substantially more when paired with the "help other shoppers" appeal than with the "help the company" appeal (Wadi et al., 2026a).
- Pairing a monetary reward with a company-focused appeal heightened the transactional framing and dampened customers' willingness to contribute.
- Firms offering review incentives should therefore align their solicitation copy with the consumer's prosocial goal of assisting peers.

## Conditional Quality Incentives and Reviewer Experience [no-exercise]
- Rather than paying for any review regardless of effort, some platforms offer conditional incentives paid only when a submitted review receives a "helpful" vote from other shoppers.
- Research shows that the effect of conditional incentives depends on the customer's prior reviewing history (Wadi et al., 2026b).
- Among first-time reviewers, a reward tied to receiving a helpful vote increased both the number of submitted reviews and the length of those reviews (Wadi et al., 2026b).
- Among experienced reviewers who already possessed established intrinsic habits for writing reviews, the same conditional incentive produced a much smaller change (Wadi et al., 2026b).
- In addition, framing the request as a reciprocal exchange with the company led respondents who did write a review to write longer text, but reduced the overall participation rate (Wadi et al., 2026b).

## Part 5: Consumer Involvement and Delegation to AI Agents [no-exercise]
- Consumers do not invest the same motivational energy in every product category or purchase occasion.
- The construct of involvement explains when buyers expend extensive cognitive effort and when they willingly delegate choices to habits or AI agents.

## Product, Message, and Situational Involvement [exercise]
[figure: three sources of consumer involvement: product characteristics, message communication format, and purchase situation]
- Involvement is a person's perceived relevance of an object, advertisement, or purchase decision based on inherent needs, values, and interests (Zaichkowsky, 1985).
- Product involvement reflects the enduring importance a consumer attaches to a product category, which rises when the item is expensive, socially visible, or central to self-identity.
- Message-response involvement reflects the consumer's motivation to process marketing communications, which varies across media formats and narrative styles.
- Purchase situation involvement reflects temporary contextual factors such as whether the item is bought as a gift for a boss versus for private home use, or under acute time pressure.
- The interaction of these three dimensions determines the depth of information search and the decision rule the buyer applies.

## Measuring Involvement and Overcoming Low Involvement [no-exercise]
- Researchers measure involvement using validated multi-item scales such as Zaichkowsky's Personal Involvement Inventory, which rates a category on pairs such as important-unimportant and relevant-irrelevant.
- Under high involvement, consumers actively search for technical specifications, compare competing brands, and process strong message arguments carefully.
- Under low involvement, consumers experience inertia: they buy the same brand out of habit without strong commitment and switch easily if a competitor is on sale or at eye level.
- Marketers increase low consumer involvement by linking a mundane product to an important health or environmental issue, introducing novel sensory packaging, or using celebrity endorsers.
- Alternatively, brands design point-of-sale displays and automatic subscription refills that accommodate low-involvement inertia rather than trying to force deep deliberation.

## Consumer Involvement and Purchase Delegation to AI Agents [no-exercise]
- Consumer involvement directly governs which purchasing tasks buyers are willing to delegate to autonomous AI shopping agents.
- In low-involvement, utilitarian categories such as paper towels, laundry detergent, or printer ink, consumers derive little psychological satisfaction from comparing prices manually.
- In these categories, buyers readily delegate search, comparison, and automated reordering to an AI agent to save time and cognitive effort.
- In high-involvement categories tied to personal identity, sensory pleasure, or high financial risk, consumers prefer to retain final evaluation and choice even if they use an AI assistant for initial information gathering.
- Understanding category involvement therefore tells brands whether their primary decision maker at checkout will be an engaged human buyer or an autonomous replenishment agent.

## Key Takeaways
- Consumer motivation begins when a gap between the actual state and a desired state creates tension, with motivational strength determining effort and motivational direction determining the chosen product path.
- Drive theory explains tension reduction toward biological homeostasis, whereas expectancy theory explains how expectancy, instrumentality, and outcome valence pull behavior toward future goals.
- Needs are classified as biogenic or psychogenic and utilitarian or hedonic, and Maslow's hierarchy organizes motives from physiological and safety needs to belonging, esteem, and self-actualization.
- Consumers face approach-approach, approach-avoidance, and avoidance-avoidance motivational conflicts, which marketers address by reducing financial, health, or performance avoidance forces.
- In online review writing, monetary incentives can crowd out intrinsic prosocial motivation unless paired with altruistic appeals ("help other shoppers"), and conditional helpfulness rewards are most effective for first-time reviewers.
- Consumer involvement spans product, message, and situational dimensions, determining whether buyers engage in extensive search or delegate routine purchases to AI shopping agents.

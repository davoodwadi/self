## Title Slide
- Welcome to Consumer Behavior.
- Today we will define the field and map the territory the course will cover.
- We will look at the consumption process, why the field matters, and the levels at which we study it.

## Part 1: What is Consumer Behavior? [no-quiz]
- Marketing asks how firms create value; consumer behavior asks what people actually do with it.
- The field borrows from psychology, sociology, anthropology, and economics.
- Our unit of analysis is the consumer, not the firm.

## The Definition of Consumer Behavior [quiz]
- Consumer behavior is the study of the processes involved when individuals or groups select, purchase, use, or dispose of products, services, ideas, or experiences.
- The definition is deliberately broad: it covers ideas and experiences, not just physical goods.
- It covers processes over time, not a single moment of choice.

## Purchase Is Only One Moment
- Marketers have historically concentrated on the moment of purchase.
- But the consumer's relationship with a product starts before it and continues long after.
- Usage and disposal shape satisfaction, repurchase, and word of mouth.

## The Consumption Process [quiz]
[diagram: cycle - Pre-purchase, purchase, usage, and disposal as a loop]
- Pre-purchase: the consumer recognizes a need and searches for options.
- Purchase: choice, payment, and the experience of acquiring.
- Usage and disposal: how the product performs, what it comes to mean, and what happens when it is done.

## Consumers Are More Than Buyers
- One person can play several roles in a single purchase.
- The influencer, the decider, the buyer, and the user need not be the same person.
- A parent buying cereal for a child is the buyer; the child is the user and often the influencer.

## Why Study Consumer Behavior? [quiz]
- For marketers: segmentation, positioning, and product design all rest on assumptions about consumers.
- For policymakers: consumer protection, public health, and nudge policy depend on how people actually decide.
- For consumers: understanding your own behavior is a defense against being managed by it.

## Consumption and Identity
- What we buy signals who we are, both to others and to ourselves.
- Products carry meaning beyond their function.
- Two consumers can buy the same car for entirely different reasons.

## Levels of Analysis [quiz]
[diagram: widening-circles - From the individual consumer out to culture]
- We can study the individual: perception, learning, memory, motivation, attitudes.
- We can study the decision itself: search, evaluation, and choice rules.
- We can widen out to groups, subcultures, and culture as a whole.

## Two Traditions in the Field
- The positivist tradition treats consumers as rational, seeks objective measurement, and favors experiments.
- The interpretivist tradition treats consumption as meaning-making and favors ethnography and depth interviews.
- Both appear in this course; neither is sufficient alone.

## Data and Method [no-quiz]
- Evidence in this field comes from experiments, surveys, panel data, field studies, and increasingly digital trace data.
- Every method carries a tradeoff between control and realism.
- We will read findings critically rather than accepting headline claims.

## The Dark Side of Consumption
- Consumer behavior also studies compulsive buying, addiction, and exploitative marketing.
- Understanding influence is understanding how it can be misused.
- Ethics is not a final week appendix; it runs through the whole course.

## Conclusion: Introduction to Consumer Behavior
- Consumer behavior covers selection, purchase, use, and disposal.
- The process extends well beyond the moment of sale.
- We analyze it from the individual outward to culture, using both positivist and interpretivist methods.

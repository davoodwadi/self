## Title Slide
- Week 1
- Consumer behavior examines how individuals, groups, and delegated AI agents select, purchase, use, and dispose of products and services to satisfy needs.

## Defining Consumer Behavior [no-exercise]
- Consumer behavior is the study of the processes involved when individuals or groups select, purchase, use, or dispose of products, services, ideas, or experiences to satisfy needs and desires.
- The transaction at the checkout is only one moment in a broader sequence of psychological and social events.
- Before buying, consumers recognize problems, gather information, and compare alternatives.
- After buying, consumers evaluate product performance, experience satisfaction or regret, and decide whether to repurchase.
- Effective marketing strategy requires analyzing the entire sequence rather than only the point of sale.

## Why Consumer Behavior Matters to Marketing Strategy [no-exercise]
- Every core marketing decision, including product design, pricing, distribution, and promotion, rests on assumptions about how buyers respond.
- A firm that understands how consumers perceive value can design products that solve real problems rather than adding unused features.
- A firm that understands when and where consumers search can place information in the channels buyers actually consult.
- Public policy makers and regulators also study consumer behavior to protect buyers from deceptive practices and unsafe products.
- Studying consumer behavior also helps individuals understand the psychological and environmental cues that shape their own spending decisions.

## Part 1: The Consumption Process and Market Segmentation [no-exercise]
- Consumption unfolds across three sequential stages, and each stage raises distinct questions for both the buyer and the marketer.
- Because buyers differ in their needs, resources, and habits, marketers divide heterogeneous markets into smaller segments with shared characteristics.

## The Three Stages of Consumption [exercise]
[figure: three-stage consumption cycle showing prepurchase, purchase, and postpurchase activities from both the consumer perspective and the marketer perspective]
- Consumption is organized into three stages: prepurchase, purchase, and postpurchase.
- In the prepurchase stage, the consumer recognizes a need, searches for information, and evaluates alternatives, while the marketer studies how attitudes form and which cues attract attention.
- In the purchase stage, the consumer experiences the retail environment, time pressure, and payment process, while the marketer manages packaging, shelf placement, and checkout friction.
- In the postpurchase stage, the consumer uses the item, evaluates whether it met expectations, and eventually disposes of it.
- Postpurchase outcomes determine repeat sales, product returns, and word-of-mouth recommendations, feeding directly into future prepurchase decisions.

## Needs, Wants, and Market Demand [no-exercise]
[figure: progression from an underlying biological or psychological need to a culturally shaped want and a purchasing-power-backed market demand]
- A need is a basic biological or psychological requirement, such as hydration, physical safety, or social belonging.
- Needs are inherent to the human condition and exist prior to any marketing activity.
- A want is the specific product, brand, or cultural form chosen to satisfy an underlying need.
- Two consumers experiencing the same need for hydration may want sparkling water, iced coffee, or hot tea depending on culture and habit.
- Demand occurs when a want is backed by both the financial ability and the willingness to pay, and marketing shapes wants rather than creating basic needs.

## Market Segmentation Bases [exercise]
[figure: four-column classification of market segmentation bases: geographic, demographic, psychographic, and behavioral]
- Market segmentation divides a broad market into distinct subsets of consumers who share common needs, priorities, or behavioral patterns.
- Geographic segmentation groups buyers by physical location, region, urban density, or climate.
- Demographic segmentation groups buyers by observable statistics such as age, income, education, occupation, and household structure.
- Psychographic segmentation groups buyers by psychological traits, values, interests, and lifestyles.
- Behavioral segmentation groups buyers by their observed actions toward a product category, including usage frequency, occasion, and brand loyalty.

## Part 2: Buyers, Users, and Decision Roles [no-exercise]
- Many market transactions involve more than one person, separating the individual who pays for a product from the individual who uses it.
- Analyzing a purchase therefore requires identifying every participant in the decision unit and the specific role each participant plays.

## Customers Versus Consumers [exercise]
[figure: comparison of a household grocery purchase separating the parent who buys the product from the child who consumes it]
- In everyday speech, the terms customer and consumer are often treated as synonyms, but they describe distinct roles in marketing analysis.
- A customer is the person or organization that purchases and pays for a good or service.
- A consumer is the person who actually uses or experiences the good or service.
- When a parent buys infant formula at a pharmacy, the parent is the customer and the infant is the consumer.
- Marketers must address both roles simultaneously, designing packaging and pricing that persuade the customer while delivering product performance that satisfies the consumer.

## Household Decision-Making Roles [exercise]
[figure: five decision roles in a family automobile purchase: initiator, information gatherer, influencer, buyer, and user]
- A single purchase decision often distributes five distinct roles across one or more people.
- The initiator is the person who first recognizes an unmet need or proposes acquiring a product.
- The gatekeeper or information gatherer controls the flow of product information that reaches the group.
- The influencer provides recommendations, expertise, or preferences that sway how alternatives are evaluated.
- The buyer executes the transaction and pays for the item, while the user consumes the product and judges its performance in daily life.

## Individual Consumers Versus Organizational Buyers [no-exercise]
- Individual consumers purchase goods and services for personal or household use, whereas organizational buyers purchase to run a business, institution, or government agency.
- Organizational buying typically involves higher dollar volumes, formal technical specifications, and negotiated contracts.
- While a consumer often decides alone or within a household, organizational purchases are frequently governed by a formal buying center composed of engineers, procurement officers, and department managers.
- Organizational demand is derived demand because a manufacturer's need for components depends directly on consumer demand for the finished product.
- Despite these structural differences, organizational buyers are still human decision makers influenced by perceived risk, brand reputation, and cognitive shortcuts.

## Discussion: Diagnosing a Postpurchase Failure
- Discussion: A manufacturer of ergonomic office chairs sells both directly to remote workers online and in bulk to corporate procurement teams. Over the past quarter, online consumer return rates rose to 18 percent while corporate reorders remained stable. How do the customer, user, and influencer roles differ across these two channels, and which stage of the consumption process should the marketing team investigate first to explain the divergence?

## Part 3: Beyond the Rational Consumer Model [no-exercise]
- Classical economic theory modeled the buyer as a rational optimizer who inspects every available option, calculates expected utility, and selects the mathematically superior choice.
- Empirical consumer research demonstrates that real buyers operate under strict constraints of time, attention, and memory.

## Assumptions of the Rational Economic Buyer [no-exercise]
- The classical economic model assumes that consumers possess complete information about all available brands, prices, and technical attributes.
- It assumes that consumers have stable, well-defined preferences that do not shift with how a question or price tag is worded.
- It further assumes that consumers have the computational capacity and time required to weigh every attribute trade-off before acting.
- Under these assumptions, marketing would serve only to transmit objective facts about price and product availability.
- In practice, few consumer markets satisfy these conditions, especially when assortments contain hundreds of competing items.

## Bounded Rationality and Psychological Influences [exercise]
[figure: four behavioral forces shaping consumer choice: cognitive limits, emotional state, social context, and habit]
- Bounded rationality recognizes that human decision makers have limited time, attention, and working memory, leading them to rely on simplifying mental rules.
- Emotional states such as stress, excitement, or nostalgia alter how buyers evaluate risk and product attractiveness.
- Social context shapes choices because consumers seek approval from peers and use visible possessions to signal identity.
- Habit and brand familiarity allow shoppers to repeat satisfactory past choices without recalculating costs on every trip to the store.
- Marketing predictions fail when managers model consumers as purely analytical calculators rather than adaptive psychological actors.

## Interdisciplinary Foundations of Consumer Research [no-exercise]
- Because consumption spans mental processes and social structures, consumer behavior draws methods and theories from several academic disciplines.
- Cognitive and experimental psychology examine how individuals perceive stimuli, form memories, and process persuasive messages.
- Social psychology and sociology examine how reference groups, family structures, and social class influence individual preferences.
- Cultural anthropology examines the shared rituals, myths, and symbolic meanings attached to possessions across societies.
- Behavioral economics and computer science now extend this foundation to study how human buyers interact with digital platforms and autonomous algorithms.

## Part 4: The AI Agent as a Surrogate Consumer [no-exercise]
- Consumers do not always conduct product search and comparison themselves. They frequently delegate parts of the decision process to external intermediaries.
- Large language models and autonomous software tools now extend surrogate consumption from high-end services to everyday retail purchasing.

## Human Surrogate Consumers in the Marketplace [no-exercise]
- A surrogate consumer is an external agent retained by a buyer to guide, direct, or execute marketplace activities on the buyer's behalf (Solomon, 1986).
- Interior decorators, stockbrokers, travel agents, and personal shoppers have long acted as human surrogates in complex or unfamiliar categories.
- Consumers turn to a surrogate when they lack specialized category expertise, face severe time pressure, or wish to reduce the perceived risk of an expensive mistake.
- When a surrogate is employed, the marketer must persuade the professional intermediary as well as the end user.
- For example, pharmaceutical firms communicate clinical evidence to prescribing physicians while addressing patient adherence separately.

## Delegating Search, Evaluation, and Purchase to AI Agents [exercise]
[figure: delegated purchase workflow in which a consumer states a goal and an AI agent searches, evaluates options, and completes the transaction]
- An AI shopping agent is a software system built on a language model that can search catalogs, compare product specifications, and execute transactions for a user.
- In delegated shopping, the consumer specifies a goal and constraints, such as finding running shoes for flat feet under 120 dollars.
- The AI agent queries retail databases, reads product attributes and customer reviews, and narrows dozens of listings to a short recommendation list or a single purchase.
- Unlike traditional human surrogates that were too costly for routine purchases, AI agents can be deployed at near-zero marginal cost for everyday grocery and household orders.
- The quality of the outcome depends on both how clearly the consumer states the goal and which product data the agent can access.

## How AI Delegation Reshapes Decision Roles [no-exercise]
- Delegating a purchase to an AI agent redistributes the classic decision roles within the consumption process.
- The human consumer retains the role of initiator by recognizing the need and stating the prompt, and remains the final user who experiences the product after delivery.
- The AI agent assumes the roles of gatekeeper, influencer, and often buyer by filtering the market, scoring alternatives, and completing checkout.
- Because the agent screens out most competing brands before the human ever sees them, entering the agent's consideration set becomes essential for brand survival.
- Marketers must therefore communicate with two distinct audiences: the human consumer who experiences the brand and the AI agent that parses structured product data.

## Part 5: Marketing Ethics, Dark Patterns, and Consumer Well-Being [no-exercise]
- Understanding the psychological and algorithmic mechanisms behind consumer choice confers substantial influence over what people buy and pay.
- Marketing ethics distinguishes practices that inform and empower buyers from manipulative designs that exploit cognitive or computational vulnerabilities.

## Consumer Well-Being and Ethical Marketing [no-exercise]
- Ethical marketing creates value by matching genuine consumer needs with transparent product information, fair pricing, and safe goods.
- Consumer well-being encompasses physical health, financial stability, and protection from deception or coercion in the marketplace.
- Transformative consumer research examines how marketing policies and retail environments can improve nutrition, financial literacy, and sustainable habits.
- Regulatory bodies such as the Federal Trade Commission in the United States and the Competition Bureau in Canada enforce standards against false advertising and deceptive pricing.
- Long-term brand equity depends on consumer trust, which erodes rapidly when buyers discover that a firm relied on manipulation to close a sale.

## Dark Patterns Aimed at Human Shoppers [exercise]
- Dark patterns are user-interface designs intentionally crafted to steer, deceive, or coerce consumers into actions they would not otherwise choose.
- Drip pricing conceals mandatory service fees until the final checkout screen, after the shopper has already invested time and committed mentally to the purchase.
- Confirmshaming uses guilt-inducing language on opt-out buttons to pressure users into accepting marketing emails or add-on warranties.
- Roach-motel subscriptions make signing up effortless with a single click while requiring phone calls or multi-page forms to cancel.
- False scarcity and countdown timers fabricate urgency to prevent consumers from comparing competitor prices before buying.

## Dark Patterns and Information Hiding Aimed at AI Agents [no-exercise]
[figure: storefront comparison showing a clear unit price display versus a hidden unit price that misleads a shopping agent]
- Deceptive storefront design can also target the AI agents that shop on behalf of consumers.
- When a retailer buries diagnostic product attributes such as package weight or unit price behind extra clicks or unindexed images, an AI agent facing lookup costs may skip those details.
- Without the package weight, an agent comparing two options may select a lower total price that actually charges a higher price per ounce.
- Sellers may also embed exaggerated claims or manipulative instructions in product text to bias how a language model scores their listing.
- Protecting consumers in agent-mediated markets requires transparency standards that ensure essential product facts are machine-readable as well as human-readable.

## Discussion: Marketing to the Consumer and the Agent
- Discussion: A consumer electronics brand sells wireless headphones through major online marketplaces. Human shoppers mostly respond to lifestyle photography and celebrity endorsements, whereas AI shopping assistants filter by battery life, active noise cancellation specifications, and verified return policies. How should the brand structure its product listings so that it appeals to human buyers without being screened out by AI shopping agents, and which merchandising tactics would cross the line into manipulative design?

## Key Takeaways
- Consumer behavior encompasses the full prepurchase, purchase, and postpurchase sequence through which individuals and groups satisfy needs.
- Marketing strategy distinguishes underlying human needs from culturally shaped wants and segments markets across geographic, demographic, psychographic, and behavioral dimensions.
- A purchase frequently separates the customer who pays from the consumer who uses the product, distributing initiator, gatekeeper, influencer, buyer, and user roles across participants.
- Real consumers operate under bounded rationality, relying on mental shortcuts, emotions, and social cues rather than exhaustive economic calculation.
- AI shopping agents act as surrogate consumers that assume information gathering, evaluation, and purchasing roles while leaving need recognition and postpurchase product use to the human consumer.
- Ethical marketing supports consumer well-being through transparent value creation, whereas dark patterns exploit cognitive limits in humans or information-access limits in AI agents.

## Title Slide
- Week 7
- Consumer decision making spans problem recognition, information search, alternative evaluation, product choice, and postpurchase review across both human and AI-delegated workflows.

## Defining Consumer Decision Making [no-exercise]
[figure: decision path from recognizing a household need at home through product search and comparison to final purchase]
- Consumer decision making is the cognitive and behavioral process by which buyers identify a need, gather information, evaluate competing options, and select a course of action.
- Some purchases require weeks of deliberation across dozens of technical attributes, such as selecting a university, an automobile, or a residential mortgage.
- Other purchases occur in less than two seconds at the shelf, such as replenishing a household staple.
- The same individual applies analytical effort in one product category while relying on automatic habit in another.
- Increasingly, consumers also delegate portions of information search and alternative comparison to autonomous AI shopping agents.

## The Five-Stage Model of the Decision Process [exercise]
[figure: five-stage consumer decision process from problem recognition through information search, evaluation of alternatives, and product choice to postpurchase evaluation]
- The classical cognitive model decomposes consumer decision making into five sequential stages (Dewey, 1910, and Engel, Kollat, & Blackwell, 1968).
- Stage 1 is problem recognition, in which the consumer perceives a meaningful discrepancy between their current state and a desired state.
- Stage 2 is information search, in which the consumer retrieves relevant knowledge from memory and consults external sources.
- Stage 3 is evaluation of alternatives, in which the consumer compares the consideration set across determinant attributes.
- Stage 4 is product choice (and purchase), followed by Stage 5, postpurchase evaluation, in which experience with the item updates future brand attitudes.

## Part 1: Problem Recognition and Levels of Effort [no-exercise]
- Every decision begins when a shift in either the consumer's current circumstances or their aspirations triggers problem recognition.
- Once a problem is recognized, the consumer's level of involvement and category experience determine how much effort is invested in solving it.

## Need Recognition Versus Opportunity Recognition [exercise]
[figure: comparison of need recognition triggered by a decline in the actual state and opportunity recognition triggered by an upward shift in the ideal state]
- Problem recognition occurs whenever the perceived distance between the actual state and the ideal state crosses a threshold of awareness.
- Need recognition occurs when the consumer's actual state declines below the threshold, as when a laptop battery fails or household groceries run out.
- Opportunity recognition occurs when the consumer's ideal state moves upward while the actual state remains unchanged, as when exposure to a faster smartphone makes a working phone feel inadequate.
- Primary demand stimulation seeks to trigger problem recognition for an entire product category, typically at the early stage of the product life cycle.
- Secondary demand stimulation seeks to convince buyers who have already recognized a problem that a specific brand offers the superior solution.

## Continuum of Decision Effort: Routine, Limited, and Extended [exercise]
[figure: decision-making continuum contrasting routine response behavior, limited problem solving, and extended problem solving across involvement, time, and number of alternatives]
- Howard and Sheth (1969) classified consumer decision processes along a continuum of effort into three levels.
- Routine response behavior (or habitual decision making) applies to low-cost, frequently purchased items where the buyer selects a familiar brand with minimal conscious search.
- Limited problem solving applies when buyers are familiar with the product category but evaluate an unfamiliar brand or variant using a few simple decision rules.
- Extended problem solving applies to unfamiliar, expensive, or high-risk purchases where buyers gather extensive information and evaluate multiple attributes carefully.
- Managerial strategy differs sharply across the continuum: market leaders reinforce routine response through consistent shelf availability, whereas challengers disrupt routine habits to trigger limited problem solving.

## Habitual Purchasing, Inertia, and Brand Switching [no-exercise]
[figure: habitual shopper reaching automatically for a familiar brand in under a second]
- Repeat purchasing of the same brand over time can reflect two distinct psychological states: inertia or brand loyalty.
- Inertia is habitual repeat buying driven by convenience and the desire to avoid the cognitive cost of comparing alternatives.
- A shopper acting under inertia lacks deep commitment to the brand and will switch readily if the habitual item is out of stock or a competitor displays a prominent point-of-sale discount.
- Variety seeking is the opposite tendency, in which consumers switch among familiar brands in categories such as snacks or beverages simply to relieve sensory boredom (McAlister & Pessemier, 1982).
- Retailers encourage variety seeking through end-aisle sampling and multi-flavor variety packs.

## Part 2: Information Search and Perceived Risk [no-exercise]
- After recognizing a problem that cannot be resolved by automatic habit, the consumer searches for information to identify and evaluate potential solutions.
- The breadth and depth of search depend on prior category knowledge, search costs, and the level of perceived risk.

## Internal Search Versus External Information Sources [exercise]
[figure: external information search across advertising, product reviews, interpersonal advice, and retail staff]
- Information search divides into two modes that typically operate in sequence: internal search and external search.
- Internal search is the process of scanning long-term memory to retrieve prior brand experiences, advertising claims, and category schemas.
- When internal search provides insufficient confidence, the consumer supplements it with external search by acquiring information from the environment.
- External sources are classified into marketer-controlled sources (advertising, packaging, brand websites, sales staff), independent public sources (consumer testing organizations, editorial reviews), and interpersonal sources (friends, family, and user reviews).
- Deliberate prepurchase search is directed at an immediate buying task, whereas ongoing search is conducted regularly by category enthusiasts to stay informed and enjoy browsing (Bloch, Sherrell, & Ridgway, 1986).

## Economics of Information Search and Prior Expertise [no-exercise]
[figure: interactive comparison of cumulative price savings against rising search costs across sequential store visits]
- From an information-economics perspective, consumers continue searching only as long as the expected marginal benefit of discovering a better price or attribute exceeds the marginal cost of search in time and mental effort (Stigler, 1961).
- Empirical research reveals an inverted-U relationship between a consumer's prior category knowledge and the amount of external search conducted (Bettman & Park, 1980).
- Complete novices often search less than moderately knowledgeable buyers because novices do not know which technical attributes to compare or how to interpret specification tables.
- True experts also conduct less external search because they already store well-organized category knowledge in long-term memory and can narrow options immediately.
- Moderately knowledgeable consumers search the most, as they understand enough to formulate questions while still needing external data to compare specific models.

## Five Types of Perceived Risk [no-exercise]
[figure: five dimensions of perceived risk in consumer purchasing: monetary, functional, physical, social, and psychological]
- Perceived risk is the consumer's belief that purchasing or using a product may produce negative consequences that cannot be anticipated with certainty (Bauer, 1960).
- Monetary (financial) risk is the potential loss of money if a purchase fails or turns out to be overpriced relative to alternatives.
- Functional (performance) risk is the possibility that the product will not perform its intended technical task reliably.
- Physical risk is the threat of bodily harm, illness, or safety hazards arising from product use.
- Social risk is the danger of embarrassment or disapproval from peers, while psychological risk is the threat of bruised self-esteem or guilt from making a poor judgment.

## Part 3: Delegated Search and Goal Specificity in AI Agents [no-exercise]
- Consumers can now delegate prepurchase information search and product comparison to autonomous AI shopping agents.
- Process-tracing experiments show that how much information an AI agent acquires depends on both lookup costs and the specificity of the user's delegation instruction.

## Process Tracing of Human and Algorithmic Information Search [no-exercise]
[figure: information-board process tracing showing attribute cells opened by an AI shopping agent under zero versus positive lookup cost]
- In classical consumer research, process-tracing methods such as Mouselab record which attribute cells a shopper opens, in what order, and for how long before making a choice (Payne, Bettman, & Johnson, 1993).
- The same paradigm can be applied to AI shopping agents when product attributes are placed behind explicit tool calls or expandable lookup fields (Wadi & Ma, 2026b).
- In realistic online storefronts, neither a human shopper nor an AI agent sees every product detail on the summary search page.
- Inspecting net package weight, warranty exclusions, or ingredient breakdowns requires clicking into individual product pages or executing additional API queries.
- Observing which attributes an agent inspects before choosing separates mistakes caused by incomplete search from mistakes caused by flawed reasoning (Wadi & Ma, 2026b).

## Information Acquisition Costs in Delegated Search [exercise]
[figure: consumer delegating product search and attribute comparison to an AI shopping agent]
- Every information lookup performed by an AI shopping agent incurs a cost in latency, token consumption, or explicit API fees.
- In controlled experiments where looking up hidden product attributes was free, AI shopping agents inspected the necessary fields and rarely selected dominated products (Wadi & Ma, 2026b).
- When each attribute lookup carried a cost, however, agents curtailed their search early and frequently made a final selection without opening diagnostic attributes such as package weight (Wadi & Ma, 2026b).
- Truncated search caused agents to select inferior options that appeared attractive on surface attributes such as headline price (Wadi & Ma, 2026b).
- Thus, just as time pressure and search friction limit human external search, lookup costs constrain the information acquisition of AI shopping agents.

## Goal Specificity in the Delegation Instruction [exercise]
[figure: comparison of AI agent attribute lookups and unit-price choice accuracy under vague versus specific delegation instructions]
- The delegation instruction is the prompt in which the consumer specifies the purchasing task for the AI agent.
- A vague instruction leaves the optimization criterion ambiguous, such as asking the agent to "find the best deal on instant coffee."
- A specific instruction states the exact evaluation metric, such as asking the agent to "find the instant coffee with the lowest price per ounce."
- In an experimental test comparing a 10-ounce coffee priced at 4.99 dollars against an 11-ounce coffee priced at 5.00 dollars (where the 5.00-dollar jar has the lower price per ounce), agents given a vague goal under lookup costs often skipped the weight field and selected the 4.99-dollar jar (Wadi & Ma, 2026b).
- When the consumer's instruction explicitly specified minimizing price per ounce, agents continued looking up package weights despite the lookup cost and selected the superior 11-ounce option (Wadi & Ma, 2026b).

## Part 4: Categorization and the Consideration Set [no-exercise]
- Because consumers cannot evaluate every brand available in a market, they partition the total assortment into smaller mental subsets before applying decision rules.
- Product categorization and determinant attributes govern which brands survive screening and how they are compared.

## From the Awareness Set to the Consideration Set [exercise]
[figure: nested brand sets narrowing from the total set to the awareness set, evoked set (inert, inept, and consideration sets), and final choice]
- Within any product category, the total set of available brands is first divided into the awareness set (brands the buyer knows) and the unawareness set.
- The evoked set comprises the brands the consumer retrieves from memory (or receives from a search tool) when confronting a purchase occasion.
- Brands known to the consumer are further sorted into three functional subsets: the consideration set, the inept set, and the inert set (Narayana & Markin, 1975).
- The consideration set includes the few brands the consumer actively and positively evaluates for purchase.
- The inept set contains brands rejected from consideration because of a past negative experience or unfavorable positioning, while the inert set contains brands toward which the buyer holds no strong evaluation.

## Product Categorization Levels and Exemplar Brands [no-exercise]
- Consumers organize product knowledge in hierarchical category structures with three primary levels: superordinate, basic, and subordinate (Rosch et al., 1976).
- The superordinate level is broad and abstract (such as "beverages"), the basic level groups items with shared core attributes (such as "soft drinks"), and the subordinate level distinguishes specific variants (such as "diet cola").
- Category exemplars are prototypical brands that define the standard for a category in consumer memory, such as Kleenex for facial tissues or Jeep for off-road vehicles.
- Moderately incongruent products that differ slightly from the category prototype stimulate curiosity and cognitive elaboration without confusing the buyer (Meyers-Levy & Tybout, 1989).
- Positioning a new product requires choosing which category level and exemplar comparison frame its benefits most favorably.

## Determinant Attributes and Diagnostic Signals [no-exercise]
- Evaluative criteria are the dimensions a consumer uses to judge the merits of competing options, such as price, safety rating, warranty length, and design.
- Not all important attributes influence the final brand choice equally.
- Salient attributes are features consumers think of first when asked about a category, such as having four wheels and functional brakes on a new passenger car.
- Determinant attributes are the specific features that both matter to the buyer and perceivedly differ across competing brands, thereby determining which option wins (Alpert, 1971).
- Effective positioning differentiates the brand on a determinant attribute rather than spending budget advertising a salient attribute that every competitor already matches.

## Part 5: Compensatory and Noncompensatory Decision Rules [no-exercise]
- Once the consideration set and determinant attributes are established, the consumer applies a decision rule (heuristic or analytical) to select a single alternative.
- Decision rules divide into two fundamental families: compensatory rules and noncompensatory rules.

## Compensatory Decision Rules: Simple and Weighted Additive [no-exercise]
[figure: comparing competing product options across weighted attributes]
- Compensatory decision rules allow a perceived weakness on one product attribute to be offset by a strong performance on another attribute.
- Under the simple additive rule, the consumer counts the number of attributes on which each brand performs well and selects the option with the largest number of positive features.
- Under the weighted additive rule, the consumer assigns an importance weight to each attribute, multiplies each brand's attribute rating by that weight, and sums the weighted scores.
- For example, a laptop with mediocre battery life can still achieve the highest weighted score if its processor speed and lightweight chassis receive high importance weights.
- Because compensatory rules require inspecting and combining multiple attributes across all candidates, they are used primarily when involvement is high or the candidate set is small.

## Noncompensatory Rules: Lexicographic, Elimination-by-Aspects, and Conjunctive [exercise]
[figure: multi-attribute product comparison table contrasting weighted compensatory scoring with noncompensatory cutoff rules]
- Noncompensatory decision rules do not permit trade-offs across attributes: a failure to meet a threshold on one attribute eliminates the brand regardless of its strengths elsewhere.
- Under the lexicographic rule, the consumer ranks attributes by importance, compares all brands on the single most important attribute, and selects the winner (moving to the second attribute only to break a tie).
- Under the elimination-by-aspects (EBA) rule, the consumer evaluates attributes sequentially in order of importance and eliminates any option that falls below a minimum cutoff on each attribute (Tversky, 1972).
- Under the conjunctive rule, the consumer establishes minimum cutoff thresholds across all relevant attributes simultaneously and rejects any brand that fails even a single threshold.
- Under the disjunctive rule, the consumer accepts any brand that exceeds a high excellence threshold on at least one key attribute.

## Phased Decision Strategies in Complex Assortments [no-exercise]
- When confronting large retail assortments containing dozens of alternatives, consumers rarely rely on a single decision rule from start to finish.
- Instead, buyers adopt a two-stage (or phased) decision strategy that balances cognitive effort against decision accuracy (Bettman, Luce, & Payne, 1998).
- In the first phase, the consumer applies a fast noncompensatory rule (such as conjunctive filtering or elimination-by-aspects) to screen out unacceptable options and reduce thirty brands to three or four.
- In the second phase, the consumer applies a careful compensatory rule (such as weighted additive comparison) to evaluate trade-offs among the surviving finalists.
- Online search filters and AI shopping assistants mirror this phased architecture when they apply hard constraint filters before scoring the remaining candidates.

## Key Takeaways
- The classical decision process comprises five stages: problem recognition, information search, evaluation of alternatives, product choice, and postpurchase evaluation.
- Problem recognition arises from need recognition (a drop in the actual state) or opportunity recognition (a rise in the ideal state), and decision effort spans routine response behavior, limited problem solving, and extended problem solving.
- Information search combines internal memory retrieval with external sources, follows an inverted-U curve across prior category knowledge, and expands as monetary, functional, physical, social, or psychological risk increases.
- When search is delegated to an AI agent, attribute lookup costs cause agents to curtail search early unless the user's delegation instruction states a specific evaluation goal.
- Consumers narrow the total market to an awareness set, an evoked set, and a consideration set (distinct from the inept and inert sets) before evaluating determinant attributes.
- Choice among alternatives relies on compensatory rules (simple and weighted additive), noncompensatory rules (lexicographic, elimination-by-aspects, conjunctive, disjunctive), or phased combinations of both.

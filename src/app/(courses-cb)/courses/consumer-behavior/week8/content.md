## Title Slide
- Week 8
- Behavioral economics explains how cognitive shortcuts, reference points, and choice architecture systematically shape decisions made by human consumers and by AI shopping agents.

## From Classical Utility to Behavioral Economics [no-exercise]
- Neoclassical economic theory assumes that consumers hold stable preferences, evaluate outcomes in terms of absolute wealth, and maximize expected utility without cognitive error.
- Behavioral economics integrates experimental psychology into economic analysis to study how real people make financial and consumption choices.
- Rather than treating departures from utility maximization as random noise, behavioral economics shows that human deviations are systematic, directional, and predictable.
- A price tag, a default checkbox, or the order in which menu items appear can alter choice share even when underlying product utility is unchanged.
- Understanding these regularities helps marketers design persuasive offers and helps regulators protect consumers from predictable traps.

## Bounded Rationality and Satisficing [no-exercise]
- Herbert Simon introduced bounded rationality to describe decision making under realistic constraints of limited time, incomplete market information, and finite working memory (Simon, 1955).
- Because exhaustive optimization across hundreds of products is computationally intractable for a human shopper, consumers replace optimization with satisficing.
- Satisficing means setting an aspiration level (a threshold of what counts as good enough) and terminating search as soon as an alternative meets that threshold.
- Satisficing is an adaptive response to search costs rather than a sign of irrationality.
- However, because satisficers stop searching once they find an acceptable option, the sequence in which alternatives are encountered strongly influences which product is chosen.

## Part 1: Dual-Process Thinking and Judgment Heuristics [no-exercise]
- Dual-process theories of cognition distinguish between rapid intuitive impressions and deliberate analytical reasoning.
- Under everyday shopping constraints, consumers rely on judgment heuristics that trade exhaustive accuracy for speed.

## System 1 and System 2 Modes of Thought [exercise]
[figure: comparison of fast, automatic System 1 processing and slow, effortful System 2 processing in retail decisions]
- Daniel Kahneman popularized the distinction between two modes of cognitive processing: System 1 and System 2 (Kahneman, 2011).
- System 1 operates automatically, rapidly, and effortlessly, generating immediate affective impressions and recognizing familiar brand colors or red clearance tags at a glance.
- System 2 allocates slow, effortful, and conscious mental computation to complex tasks, such as calculating unit prices across different package sizes or reading mortgage contract terms.
- Because System 2 requires scarce cognitive energy, consumers default to System 1 judgments for most routine grocery and online retail purchases.
- System 2 overrides System 1 only when the consumer detects a clear anomaly, faces high stakes, and has sufficient time and cognitive capacity available.

## Availability and Representativeness Heuristics [exercise]
- A heuristic is a mental rule of thumb that simplifies judgment by substituting an easier question for a harder statistical question (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974).
- The availability heuristic estimates the frequency or probability of an event by the ease with which instances can be retrieved from memory.
- Vivid media coverage of a single product recall or aircraft incident makes the event highly accessible in memory, causing consumers to overestimate the objective risk of that brand.
- The representativeness heuristic judges the probability that an object belongs to a category by how closely its surface features resemble the category prototype.
- Private-label packages that mimic the typography and color palette of a national brand leader leverage representativeness to imply comparable manufacturing quality.

## Market Beliefs and Price-Quality Inferences [no-exercise]
- Over repeated marketplace experiences, consumers develop generalized market beliefs that serve as domain-specific heuristics.
- The price-quality heuristic is the belief that higher monetary price reliably indicates superior materials, craftsmanship, or performance (Rao & Monroe, 1989).
- Consumers rely on the price-quality heuristic most heavily when objective quality is difficult to judge prior to purchase, as with wine, cosmetics, or medical services.
- Placebos and blind taste tests show that labeling the identical wine with a higher price tag increases not only self-reported taste ratings but also activity in brain regions associated with experienced pleasantness (Plassmann et al., 2008).
- Other common market beliefs include assuming that larger package sizes always offer a lower unit price or that stores with warehouse decor charge lower margins.

## Part 2: Reference Points, Prospect Theory, and Framing [no-exercise]
- Human consumers do not evaluate prices, gains, and losses in absolute terms. Instead, they judge every outcome relative to a psychological reference point.
- Anchoring, prospect theory, and framing effects explain how shifting the reference point reverses consumer preferences.

## Anchoring and Arbitrary Reference Numbers [exercise]
[figure: shift in consumer willingness to pay following exposure to a low versus high numerical anchor]
- Anchoring and adjustment occurs when an initial numerical value serves as a starting reference point that pulls subsequent numerical estimates toward it (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974).
- Adjustments away from an initial anchor are typically insufficient, leaving final judgments biased toward the starting number.
- In a classic experiment by Ariely, Loewenstein, and Prelec (2003), participants who first wrote down the last two digits of their social security number stated significantly higher maximum willingness to pay for wine, chocolates, and computer accessories when those random digits were high.
- Retailers establish commercial anchors through manufacturer suggested retail prices ("Compare at 120 dollars"), high-priced decoy items at the top of a menu, and purchase quantity limits ("Limit 12 cans per customer").
- Even when buyers know a reference price is inflated, the anchor still elevates the price they consider acceptable.

## Prospect Theory, Loss Aversion, and the Endowment Effect [exercise]
[figure: asymmetrical prospect-theory value function showing steeper psychological disutility for losses than utility for equivalent gains]
- Developed by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky (1979), prospect theory replaces classical expected utility with a value function defined over gains and losses relative to a reference point.
- First, the value function exhibits diminishing sensitivity: the psychological difference between a 10-dollar and 20-dollar gain feels much larger than the difference between a 1,010-dollar and 1,020-dollar gain.
- Second, the value function is steeper in the domain of losses than in the domain of gains, a property known as loss aversion.
- Loss aversion implies that losing 50 dollars inflicts roughly twice as much psychological pain as gaining 50 dollars provides pleasure.
- The endowment effect is a direct corollary of loss aversion: once a consumer owns or possesses an item (such as through a 30-day free home trial), giving it up is coded as a loss, raising the value assigned to the item (Thaler, 1980).

## Status Quo Bias and Attribute Framing [no-exercise]
- Status quo bias is the strong preference for maintaining one's current state or existing service provider because any change requires trading potential gains against more heavily weighted potential losses (Samuelson & Zeckhauser, 1988).
- Consumers frequently remain on suboptimal telecommunications plans, insurance policies, or software subscriptions because switching feels risky and effortful.
- Attribute framing occurs when describing the exact same objective statistic in positive versus negative terms alters evaluation.
- In a classic study by Levin and Gaeth (1988), consumers evaluated ground beef as leaner and higher in quality when labeled "75 percent lean" than when labeled "25 percent fat."
- Similarly, framing a price difference between payment methods as a "cash discount" triggers less resistance than framing the identical gap as a "credit card surcharge."

## Part 3: Mental Accounting, Pricing Cues, and Choice Architecture [no-exercise]
- Consumers organize financial decisions into separate mental budgets and respond systematically to how digits, discounts, and default options are structured.
- Mental accounting and choice architecture provide a framework for analyzing retail pricing and public policy nudges.

## Mental Accounting, Sunk Costs, and Transaction Utility [no-exercise]
- Formulated by Richard Thaler (1985), mental accounting describes how individuals code, categorize, and evaluate financial outcomes into separate mental budgets rather than treating money as perfectly fungible.
- Consumers spend a 100-dollar tax refund or casino windfall more freely on hedonic luxuries than 100 dollars of regular wage income.
- Total utility from a purchase is the sum of acquisition utility (the value of the good received relative to its price) and transaction utility (the perceived psychological merit of the deal relative to the consumer's internal reference price) (Thaler, 1985).
- The sunk cost effect occurs when consumers continue using or investing in a poor option solely because they have already paid a non-recoverable fee, such as attending an outdoor event in a rainstorm because tickets were prepaid (Arkes & Blumer, 1985).
- Subscription pricing and prepaid vacation packages decouple the moment of payment from the moment of consumption, reducing the pain of paying during usage (Prelec & Loewenstein, 1998).

## Just-Below Pricing and the Left-Digit Effect [exercise]
- Just-below pricing (or charm pricing) sets a retail price one cent or one unit below a round number, such as 4.99 dollars instead of 5.00 dollars or 199 dollars instead of 200 dollars.
- Because consumers read numerical prices from left to right under System 1, they anchor disproportionately on the leftmost digit before finishing encoding the remaining digits, a phenomenon known as the left-digit effect (Thomas & Morwitz, 2005).
- Consequently, a one-cent reduction from 5.00 dollars to 4.99 dollars lowers perceived price magnitude much more than a one-cent reduction from 4.99 dollars to 4.98 dollars.
- Just-below pricing is particularly effective when the left digit changes and when the buyer is not performing explicit arithmetic comparisons.
- Conversely, luxury and prestige brands often use round-number pricing (such as 500 dollars rather than 499.99 dollars) because round numbers are processed fluently and signal high quality rather than discount positioning.

## Choice Architecture, Defaults, and Nudges [exercise]
[figure: comparison of opt-in versus opt-out default enrollment rates in consumer decision environments]
- Choice architecture refers to the design of the environment in which people make decisions, including option ordering, attribute grouping, and default settings (Thaler & Sunstein, 2008).
- A default option is the outcome that takes effect automatically if the decision maker takes no active step to change it.
- Because of status quo bias, effort minimization, and the perception that the default represents an implicit endorsement by the designer, defaults exert powerful effects on behavior.
- In a cross-national study by Johnson and Goldstein (2003), countries where organ donation was the default (opt-out) achieved consent rates above 85 percent, whereas comparable countries requiring active registration (opt-in) achieved rates below 30 percent.
- A nudge alters choice architecture to steer behavior in a predictable way that benefits the chooser without forbidding any alternatives or significantly changing economic incentives (Thaler & Sunstein, 2008).

## Part 4: Heuristic-Like Choice in AI Shopping Agents [no-exercise]
- Because large language models are trained on human-generated text, researchers investigate whether AI shopping agents replicate human cognitive biases or behave like rational economic calculators.
- Controlled experiments reveal that whether an AI agent exhibits a behavioral bias depends critically on the task structure and information environment.

## Anchoring and Position Effects in Language Models [no-exercise]
- In judgment tasks lacking an objective calculation formula, large language models can display classic human-like biases.
- When asked to state willingness to pay for consumer products after exposure to an arbitrary numerical anchor in the prompt, language models shifted their valuations toward the anchor (Wadi & Fredette, 2025).
- Notably, newer and more capable model generations exhibited stronger susceptibility to numerical anchoring than earlier generations, demonstrating that scaling model size does not automatically eliminate judgment biases (Wadi & Fredette, 2025).
- In multi-option choice tasks, AI shopping agents also exhibit pronounced position effects, favoring options placed at the beginning or end of a candidate list (Wadi & Ma, 2026a).
- Thus, in open-ended valuation and list scanning, language models inherit contextual sensitivities from their architecture and training data.

## How AI Agents Respond to Just-Below Pricing and Promotional Framing [exercise]
[figure: comparison of human left-digit sensitivity and AI agent weightings of cents versus whole dollars and discounts versus list price when full details are visible]
- A central question for retail pricing is whether AI shopping agents are fooled by System 1 pricing cues such as 4.99-dollar just-below prices and "Save 20 percent" promotional discount frames.
- In controlled experiments where all product attributes and prices were simultaneously visible on the screen, seven out of eight tested AI shopping agents weighted the cents component of a price equally with the whole-dollar component (Wadi & Ma, 2026b).
- Under full information, a 4.99-dollar price tag did not induce a left-digit illusion in those agents compared with a 5.00-dollar price tag (Wadi & Ma, 2026b).
- Similarly, six out of eight tested agents weighted a dollar saved through a promotional discount identically to a dollar reduction in regular list price, showing no transaction-utility distortion (Wadi & Ma, 2026b).
- When relevant numbers are directly visible, most frontier AI agents evaluate prices arithmetically rather than relying on human System 1 perceptual shortcuts.

## Distinguishing Reasoning Errors from Information Deficits [no-exercise]
- Why do AI shopping agents sometimes make choices that resemble human heuristic errors in realistic digital storefronts?
- Research by Wadi and Ma (2026b) demonstrates that apparent heuristic behavior in AI agents usually stems from incomplete information acquisition rather than flawed arithmetic reasoning.
- When agents were explicitly given all package weights and prices and instructed to compute unit prices, zero agents made a calculation error (Wadi & Ma, 2026b).
- However, when package weights were hidden behind costly secondary lookups and the user's goal was stated vaguely, agents frequently skipped the weight lookup and selected the option with the lower headline dollar price or larger promotional badge (Wadi & Ma, 2026b).
- Treating an agent's choice error as an internal cognitive bias when it is actually a rational response to lookup costs and vague instructions leads to the wrong managerial diagnosis.

## Part 5: Information Acquisition Costs and Storefront Architecture [no-exercise]
- Storefront information architecture determines how product attributes are organized across search summary pages, detail pages, and API endpoints.
- How a retailer structures attribute visibility directly governs whether AI shopping agents make value-maximizing or heuristic-like choices.

## Hidden Attributes and Search Costs in Agent-Mediated Shopping [no-exercise]
[figure: storefront information architecture showing how hiding package weight behind costly lookups induces heuristic-like choices in AI agents]
- In many online stores, the search results page displays only the product title, thumbnail image, headline price, and promotional badge, while net weight or unit count requires opening the individual product page.
- For both a time-pressed human shopper and an AI agent facing token or latency costs, this two-tier layout imposes an information acquisition cost on calculating true unit value.
- When lookup costs are positive and the buyer's prompt is vague ("buy the best deal"), AI agents often rely on the visible headline price alone and select smaller packages with higher unit costs (Wadi & Ma, 2026b).
- The resulting choice mimics human left-digit or promotional framing biases even though the underlying mechanism is costly information search (Wadi & Ma, 2026b).
- Storefront friction therefore converts an analytically capable AI agent into a surface-cue chooser.

## Full-Information Recovery and Unit-Price Calculation [no-exercise]
- Two interventions restore rational unit-value choice in AI shopping agents: lowering storefront lookup friction or increasing prompt specificity (Wadi & Ma, 2026b).
- First, when diagnostic attributes such as net weight, volume, or unit price are surfaced directly on the initial search results feed, agents incorporate them immediately and select the lower cost per ounce (Wadi & Ma, 2026b).
- Second, even when attributes remain hidden behind costly lookups, instructing the agent with a specific criterion ("find the lowest price per ounce") compels the agent to pay the lookup cost and verify weight before choosing (Wadi & Ma, 2026b).
- Retailers offering genuine unit-price value benefit from surfacing complete package specifications in machine-readable search summaries.
- Conversely, sellers offering poor unit value behind low headline prices benefit when diagnostic attributes are buried deeper in the interface.

## Regulatory and Merchandising Implications for Storefront Design [no-exercise]
- Consumer protection regulations in many jurisdictions require supermarkets to print standardized unit prices (price per 100 grams or per ounce) on physical shelf tags.
- These disclosure mandates were written for human shoppers walking down physical aisles and often do not specify how unit prices must be exposed in digital HTML or API feeds consulted by AI agents.
- If unit prices are rendered only as visual graphics or buried behind secondary clicks, an AI agent shopping for a consumer does not receive the regulatory protection intended for the buyer.
- Updating choice-architecture regulation for agentic commerce requires mandating that unit prices, mandatory fees, and package quantities be exposed in standardized, low-friction machine-readable fields.
- Brands that provide transparent, easily accessible unit specifications build preference among both analytical human buyers and autonomous shopping agents.

## Key Takeaways
- Behavioral economics shows that consumers operate under bounded rationality and satisfice rather than optimize exhaustively across all market options.
- System 1 thinking is fast, automatic, and effortless, whereas System 2 thinking is slow, analytical, and effortful, leading everyday shoppers to rely on availability, representativeness, and price-quality heuristics.
- Judgments are reference-dependent: arbitrary numerical anchors shift willingness to pay, prospect theory's loss aversion makes losses loom roughly twice as large as gains (driving the endowment effect and status quo bias), and positive attribute frames outperform negative frames.
- Mental accounting, transaction utility, and the left-digit effect in just-below pricing (4.99 dollars vs. 5.00 dollars) shape price perception, while choice architecture uses defaults and nudges to guide behavior.
- AI models exhibit anchoring in open-ended valuation and position effects in list choice, but under full information most AI shopping agents weight cents and promotional discounts rationally without left-digit or transaction-utility illusions.
- When storefront architecture hides diagnostic attributes such as package weight behind costly lookups and the user's goal is vague, AI agents skip lookups and make heuristic-like choices, which can be corrected by specific delegation prompts or transparent machine-readable unit pricing.

## Title Slide
- Week 10
- Consumers rarely choose alone. Other people, and now AI assistants, shape what they buy.

## Why Other People Shape Our Choices [no-exercise]
- Consumers look to other people for information, approval, and a sense of identity.
- A teenager chooses sneakers that friends will admire. A new parent asks other parents which stroller to buy.
- Social influence is strongest for products that others can see, such as clothing, cars, and phones.
- Social influence is weaker for private products, such as toothpaste or a mattress.
- Marketers use social influence when they show real customers, popular choices, and recommendations from trusted people.

## Reference Groups: Membership, Aspirational, and Dissociative [exercise]
- A reference group is a person or group whose values and behavior a consumer uses as a guide.
- A membership group is a group the consumer already belongs to, such as family, classmates, or a sports team.
- An aspirational group is a group the consumer wants to join, such as successful professionals or elite athletes.
- A dissociative group is a group the consumer wants to avoid being associated with.
- Consumers buy products that signal membership in the groups they value and avoid products linked with groups they reject.

## Three Types of Reference Group Influence [exercise]
- Reference groups influence consumers in three ways (Park & Lessig, 1977).
- Informational influence occurs when a consumer seeks advice from people with expertise, such as asking a friend who works in IT which laptop to buy.
- Utilitarian influence occurs when a consumer conforms to gain rewards or avoid disapproval, such as dressing to meet the expectations of coworkers.
- Value-expressive influence occurs when a consumer buys a product to express a desired identity, such as buying the brand that athletes wear.

## Conformity: Following the Group
- Conformity is a change in beliefs or behavior in response to real or imagined group pressure.
- In classic experiments, many people gave an obviously wrong answer when everyone else in the room gave it first (Asch, 1956).
- Consumers conform to norms, the informal rules about what is appropriate in a group.
- Conformity is stronger when the group is cohesive, when the consumer is uncertain, and when the choice is public.
- Labels such as "bestseller" and "most popular" use conformity by showing what other consumers chose.

## Five Bases of Social Power [exercise]
- Social power is the capacity to change the actions of others (French & Raven, 1959).
- Referent power comes from admiration. Consumers imitate people they like and identify with, such as a favorite athlete.
- Legitimate power comes from a recognized position or role, such as a police officer or a teacher.
- Expert power comes from knowledge or skill, such as a dermatologist recommending a sunscreen.
- Reward power comes from the ability to give something valued, such as praise, a discount, or a prize.
- Coercive power comes from the ability to punish, such as social exclusion or a fine.

## Expert Power and AI Assistants [exercise]
- Consumers now ask AI assistants which product to buy, much as they would ask a knowledgeable friend.
- Many consumers attribute expert power to these assistants, because the answers are fluent, detailed, and fast.
- This attributed expertise gives the assistant influence over the consideration set and the final choice.
- Expert power depends on actual knowledge. An AI assistant can state wrong facts with the same confidence as correct ones.
- Expert power also depends on independence. Consumers trust a human expert less when the expert is paid to recommend a product.
- The same question applies to an AI assistant: consumers need to know whether the platform that runs it profits from what it recommends.

## Word of Mouth and Opinion Leaders [exercise]
- Word of mouth (WOM) is product information passed from one consumer to another.
- WOM is more credible than advertising, because the speaker usually has no financial interest in the sale.
- Negative WOM carries more weight than positive WOM. Consumers pay more attention to a complaint than to praise.
- Electronic word of mouth (eWOM) includes online reviews, ratings, forum posts, and social media comments.
- An opinion leader is a person who frequently influences the attitudes and behavior of others in a product category.
- Opinion leaders are knowledgeable, involved in the category, and socially active. Their influence is usually limited to one category, such as fashion or technology.
- A market maven has broad information about many kinds of products and places to shop, and likes to share it (Feick & Price, 1987).

## AI-Generated Review Summaries and Synthetic Reviews [exercise]
- Many shopping sites now show a short summary of a product's reviews, written by AI.
- Consumers may read the summary instead of the reviews themselves. The summary then becomes a new source of word of mouth.
- A summary reflects the reviews it draws on. If many of those reviews were written in return for a reward, the summary carries the same influence.
- AI also makes it easy to write synthetic reviews: reviews that look genuine but were not written by real customers.
- Synthetic reviews weaken the credibility that makes word of mouth powerful.
- In 2024, the U.S. Federal Trade Commission adopted a rule that bans fake reviews, including reviews attributed to people who do not exist, such as reviews generated by AI.
- AI agents that shop for consumers also read reviews. A fake review can therefore mislead the consumer and the agent at the same time.

## Influencers, Virtual Influencers, and Online Communities [exercise]
- An influencer is a person who has built an audience on social media and promotes products to it.
- Macro-influencers have large audiences. Micro-influencers have smaller audiences but often higher engagement and trust.
- Followers can form a parasocial relationship with an influencer: a one-sided feeling of friendship with someone they have never met.
- A virtual influencer is a computer-generated character that posts and promotes products like a human influencer, such as Lil Miquela.
- A virtual influencer has no lived experience of the product it promotes, which raises questions about its credibility and honesty.
- Online communities, such as brand forums and fan groups, act as social proof. Members see how others use a product and adopt the group's norms.
- Paid endorsements must be disclosed, so that followers can judge the message as advertising.

## Discussion: Who Influenced Your Last Purchase?
- Discussion: Think of a recent purchase. Which person, group, influencer, or AI assistant influenced it? Which base of social power did that source have over you?

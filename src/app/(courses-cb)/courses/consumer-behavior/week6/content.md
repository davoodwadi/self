## Title Slide
- Week 6
- Consumer attitudes integrate feelings, beliefs, and behavioral intentions toward marketplace objects, and persuasive communications change those attitudes through source, message, and processing pathways.

## Defining Consumer Attitudes [no-exercise]
[figure: attitude object evaluated on a continuum from unfavorable to favorable]
- An attitude is a lasting, general evaluation of people, objects, advertisements, or issues (Solomon, 2020).
- Anything toward which a consumer holds an attitude, whether a specific brand, a product category, or a retail channel, is called an attitude object ($A_o$).
- Attitudes are characterized by valence (positive, negative, or neutral), extremity (how strongly the evaluation is held), and persistence over time.
- Because attitudes summarize prior learning and evaluation in memory, they allow consumers to make rapid repeat choices without re-evaluating every attribute from scratch.
- At the same time, strongly held attitudes create selective resistance to competing marketing claims.

## The Functional Theory of Attitudes [exercise]
[figure: Katz's four attitude functions: utilitarian, value-expressive, ego-defensive, and knowledge]
- Developed by Daniel Katz, the functional theory of attitudes proposes that attitudes exist because they serve specific psychological motives for the person holding them (Katz, 1960).
- The utilitarian function guides consumers toward products that provide tangible rewards and away from products that impose costs, such as favoring a detergent solely because it removes stains effectively.
- The value-expressive function allows consumers to express their central values, self-concept, and social identity, such as driving an electric vehicle to signal environmental commitment.
- The ego-defensive function protects the individual from external threats or internal insecurities, such as purchasing mouthwash or deodorant to avoid social embarrassment.
- The knowledge function satisfies the need for order, structure, and meaning, especially when a consumer encounters an unfamiliar technology or product category.

## Part 1: The ABC Model and Hierarchies of Effects [no-exercise]
- Every attitude comprises three interrelated components: affect, behavior, and cognition.
- The sequence in which these three components develop depends on the consumer's level of involvement and the nature of the product category.

## The ABC Model: Affect, Behavior, and Cognition [exercise]
[figure: triad of attitude components linking affect, behavior, and cognition toward an attitude object]
- The ABC model of attitudes specifies that an attitude consists of Affect, Behavior, and Cognition.
- Affect refers to the emotions and feelings a consumer experiences toward an attitude object, such as warmth toward a childhood cereal brand.
- Behavior (or conation) refers to the consumer's intentions to take action toward the object, such as planning to buy, recommend, or boycott a brand.
- Cognition refers to the factual beliefs and knowledge a consumer holds about the object's attributes, such as believing that a laptop has a fourteen-hour battery life.
- While the three components often align, they can conflict, as when a consumer knows a sports car is fuel-inefficient (cognition) yet feels excited by its design (affect).

## Three Hierarchies of Attitude Formation [exercise]
[figure: three sequential paths of attitude formation: standard learning, low-involvement, and experiential hierarchies]
- A hierarchy of effects describes the fixed sequence in which cognition, affect, and behavior unfold when an attitude is formed.
- The standard learning hierarchy (Cognition to Affect to Behavior) applies to high-involvement problem solving: the buyer gathers beliefs first, forms a feeling, and then buys, as when purchasing a home or laptop.
- The low-involvement hierarchy (Cognition to Behavior to Affect) applies to routine convenience goods: the buyer forms minimal beliefs from shelf familiarity, tries the item, and forms a feeling only after use.
- The experiential hierarchy (Affect to Behavior to Cognition) applies to hedonic and impulse purchases: the buyer reacts emotionally to sensory or symbolic appeal, buys the item, and rationalizes the beliefs afterward.
- Identifying which hierarchy governs a category tells marketers whether advertising should emphasize detailed attribute facts, simple repetition, or emotional sensory appeal.

## Multi-Attribute Attitude Models and the Theory of Planned Behavior [no-exercise]
[figure: comparison of aligned attitude components leading to purchase versus conflicting beliefs preventing purchase]
- Multi-attribute attitude models, such as the Fishbein model, represent a consumer's overall attitude toward a brand as the sum of beliefs about its attributes weighted by the importance evaluation of each attribute (Fishbein, 1963).
- A favorable attitude toward a brand does not always translate into an actual purchase on the next shopping trip.
- The Theory of Reasoned Action and the Theory of Planned Behavior explain this gap by predicting behavioral intention from three factors: attitude toward the specific act of buying, subjective social norms, and perceived behavioral control (Ajzen, 1991).
- Subjective norms combine normative beliefs about what important reference groups think the consumer should do with the consumer's motivation to comply with those groups.
- Marketers can change overall brand attitude under a multi-attribute model by improving a weak attribute belief, raising the importance weight of a strong attribute, or introducing a novel attribute.

## Part 2: Persuasion and the Elaboration Likelihood Model [no-exercise]
- Persuasion is an active attempt to change a consumer's attitude through marketing communications.
- Dual-process models of persuasion explain why detailed factual arguments succeed in some situations while celebrity endorsements or catchy music succeed in others.

## The Communications Model of Persuasion [no-exercise]
[figure: print advertisement in a magazine eliciting cognitive beliefs, affective response, and behavioral coupon redemption from the reader]
- The classic communications model analyzes persuasion across five components: the source, the message, the medium, the receiver, and feedback.
- The source (who delivers the communication) determines initial credibility and likability.
- The message (what is said and how it is structured) supplies the arguments, emotional appeals, and visual cues.
- The medium (the channel through which the message travels, such as print, short-form video, or an interactive sales assistant) shapes processing pace and context.
- Characteristics of the receiver, including prior knowledge, involvement, and mood, determine how the message is decoded and whether counterarguing occurs.

## Central and Peripheral Routes to Persuasion [exercise]
[figure: Elaboration Likelihood Model showing high-involvement central route processing versus low-involvement peripheral route processing]
- Formulated by Richard Petty and John Cacioppo, the Elaboration Likelihood Model (ELM) states that persuasion occurs through one of two distinct cognitive routes depending on the consumer's motivation and ability to process the message (Petty & Cacioppo, 1986).
- When both motivation (involvement) and ability (knowledge and distraction-free time) are high, consumers follow the central route to persuasion.
- Under the central route, consumers carefully scrutinize the quality and logic of the message arguments, producing durable attitudes that resist counter-persuasion and predict behavior strongly.
- When motivation or ability is low, consumers follow the peripheral route to persuasion, relying on surface cues such as package design, background music, source attractiveness, or the sheer number of arguments.
- Attitudes formed via the peripheral route are temporary and vulnerable to competitor promotions unless reinforced by repeated exposure.

## Message Structure: One-Sided Versus Two-Sided Appeals [no-exercise]
[figure: two-sided appeal card acknowledging a minor drawback alongside a primary ticked strength]
- A one-sided message presents only arguments in favor of the brand and omits any mention of limitations or drawbacks.
- A two-sided (or refutational) message acknowledges a minor limitation on a secondary attribute and then demonstrates superiority on the primary attribute that matters most to buyers.
- One-sided messages are most effective when the audience already holds a favorable attitude toward the brand or has low involvement and limited education on the topic.
- Two-sided messages are more effective with skeptical, well-informed audiences because acknowledging a minor flaw reduces counterarguing and enhances perceived source trustworthiness.
- Comparative advertising that names a leading competitor directly can accelerate positioning for an unknown challenger brand, but risks generating free recall for the market leader if poorly executed.

## Part 3: Source Effects and Message Framing [no-exercise]
- Even when two advertisements contain identical factual claims, consumers respond differently depending on who delivers the message and how the outcomes are framed.
- Source credibility, source attractiveness, and gain-loss framing systematically alter persuasive impact.

## Source Credibility and the Sleeper Effect [no-exercise]
[figure: product package endorsed with an expert credential seal]
- Source credibility is the extent to which a communicator is perceived as possessing relevant expertise, objectivity, and trustworthiness (Hovland & Weiss, 1951).
- Expertise refers to the source's technical competence, qualifications, or experience within the product category, such as an orthopedic surgeon evaluating athletic footwear.
- Trustworthiness refers to the audience's belief that the source is honest and impartial rather than motivated purely by financial compensation.
- When a credible source lacks trustworthiness because of undisclosed bias, consumers engage in source discounting and reject the message.
- The sleeper effect occurs when a persuasive message delivered by a low-credibility source gains attitude acceptance over time because the audience forgets the discounting source faster than the message content itself.

## Source Attractiveness and the Match-Up Hypothesis [exercise]
[figure: three dimensions of source attractiveness: familiarity across media, likability, and similarity to the buyer]
- Source attractiveness derives from the communicator's physical appeal, social status, familiarity, likability, and perceived similarity to the target audience.
- Attractive or celebrity sources capture attention in cluttered media environments and transfer cultural meaning from the celebrity's persona to the brand (McCracken, 1989).
- According to the match-up hypothesis, an endorser is persuasive only when the source's image and expertise fit the nature of the endorsed product (Kahle & Homer, 1985).
- A physically attractive fashion model increases persuasion for cosmetics and luxury apparel, whereas an accomplished engineer or athlete is more effective for technical equipment.
- When a celebrity endorser has no logical connection to the product category, consumers may remember the celebrity while failing to encode the brand name, a risk known as the vampire effect.

## Positive Versus Negative Message Framing [no-exercise]
[figure: comparison of gain-framed and loss-framed persuasive messages across prevention and promotion contexts]
- Message framing refers to whether a persuasive communication emphasizes the positive benefits of adopting a behavior (gain frame) or the negative consequences of failing to adopt it (loss frame).
- Attribute framing describes a single characteristic in either positive or negative terms, such as labeling snack crackers "90 percent fat-free" versus "10 percent fat."
- Goal framing contrasts the benefit of action ("Flossing daily keeps gums healthy") with the cost of inaction ("Failing to floss daily leads to gum disease").
- Regulatory focus theory shows that gain frames are more persuasive when consumers are in a promotion focus seeking growth and advancement, whereas loss frames are more persuasive under a prevention focus seeking safety and risk avoidance (Higgins, 1997).
- Fear appeals are a specific form of loss framing that succeed only when paired with a clear, high-efficacy action the consumer can take to avert the threat.

## Part 4: AI as a Message Source: Aversion and Appreciation [no-exercise]
- Consumers increasingly receive product recommendations, financial advice, and medical screening from algorithmic systems and conversational AI assistants.
- Research on human-algorithm interaction reveals that consumers evaluate AI message sources through distinct patterns of algorithm aversion and algorithm appreciation.

## Algorithm Aversion After Observing Error [no-exercise]
[figure: consumer rejecting an AI advisor after observing an error while forgiving a human advisor who makes the same mistake]
- Algorithm aversion is the tendency of decision makers to distrust and reject algorithmic forecasters or advisors in favor of human judgment, even when the algorithm is statistically more accurate on average (Dietvorst, Simmons, & Massey, 2015).
- Before observing any performance history, consumers are often neutral or open toward algorithmic advice.
- However, once people observe an algorithm make an error, they lose confidence in the algorithm much more rapidly than they lose confidence in a human advisor who makes the exact same error (Dietvorst, Simmons, & Massey, 2015).
- Consumers tend to attribute a human advisor's mistake to temporary fatigue or an isolated oversight while assuming the human will learn from experience.
- By contrast, consumers interpret an algorithmic error as evidence of a permanent structural flaw in the software, though giving users slight control to adjust the algorithm's forecast reduces this aversion (Dietvorst, Simmons, & Massey, 2018).

## Algorithm Appreciation and Task Objectivity [exercise]
[figure: consumer trust in algorithmic versus human recommendations across objective analytical tasks and subjective taste-based tasks]
- Algorithm appreciation is the opposite phenomenon, in which people adhere more closely to identical advice when they are told it comes from an algorithm than when they are told it comes from a human advisor (Logg, Minson, & Moore, 2019).
- Whether consumers exhibit algorithm appreciation or algorithm aversion depends systematically on the perceived objectivity of the task (Castelo, Bos, & Lehmann, 2019).
- For tasks perceived as objective and quantifiable, such as forecasting stock returns, calculating mortgage refinancing savings, or routing traffic, consumers trust algorithms at high rates.
- For tasks perceived as subjective, emotional, or reliant on human intuition, such as recommending a romantic movie, selecting a joke, or choosing a personal gift, consumers strongly prefer human sources (Castelo, Bos, & Lehmann, 2019).
- Framing a subjective recommendation task around quantifiable behavioral steps increases consumer willingness to rely on an algorithmic source (Castelo, Bos, & Lehmann, 2019).

## Credibility and Perceived Independence of AI Advisors [no-exercise]
[figure: expert AI shopping assistant positioned between the consumer and the retail storefront raising the question of perceived independence]
- Like human communicators, AI recommendation sources are evaluated on the two classic pillars of credibility: expertise and trustworthiness.
- Conversational AI assistants project high apparent expertise because they generate fluent, structured, and immediate comparisons across dozens of technical attributes.
- Perceived trustworthiness, however, depends on whether the consumer believes the AI system acts as an independent advisor or as a commissioned sales channel for the platform operating it.
- When consumers suspect that an AI assistant prioritizes high-margin house brands or paid sponsorships without disclosure, source discounting erodes trust in the entire system.
- Maintaining persuasive credibility for an AI shopping assistant therefore requires both verifiable accuracy and transparent alignment with the user's interests.

## Part 5: Measuring Attitudes in Humans and AI Models [no-exercise]
- Because large language models now advise consumers and act as surrogate buyers, researchers need rigorous methods to measure the attitudes and evaluations embedded in these models.
- Applying psychometric attitude scales to AI models reveals both methodological requirements and systematic differences across model developers.

## Consumer Attitude Scales: Likert and Semantic Differential [no-exercise]
- In human consumer research, attitudes are measured using structured multi-item self-report instruments.
- A Likert scale presents a declarative statement about an attitude object and asks respondents to rate their degree of agreement, typically on a five-point or seven-point scale from "strongly disagree" to "strongly agree" (Likert, 1932).
- A semantic differential scale asks respondents to rate an attitude object across a series of bipolar adjective pairs, such as "unreliable-reliable," "expensive-inexpensive," and "unpleasant-pleasant" (Osgood, Suci, & Tannenbaum, 1957).
- Combining multiple items into a composite index reduces measurement error from any single question's wording.
- Researchers validate these scales for internal consistency reliability and construct validity before using them to compare market segments.

## Administering Attitude Scales to Large Language Models [exercise]
[figure: repeated sampling and token probability distributions across a seven-point Likert scale for an AI model]
- Researchers now administer validated consumer attitude scales directly to large language models to audit the evaluations that guide model recommendations.
- Unlike a deterministic database lookup, a language model samples tokens probabilistically and does not return a single fixed rating.
- Asked the identical Likert-scale question across repeated independent runs, the same model can output different scale points on different trials (Wadi & Fredette, 2025).
- Treating a single completion as "the model's attitude" therefore introduces severe measurement error.
- Reliable measurement requires either administering each scale item across many independent runs or extracting the probability distribution the model assigns to each scale option (Wadi, Ghodrat, & Philp, 2026).

## Cross-Developer Attitude Differences in AI Models [no-exercise]
[figure: AI model outputting a statistical distribution across a seven-point attitude scale without an underlying affective state]
- When consumer attitude scales are measured across full response distributions, systematic differences emerge across models built by different organizations (Wadi, Ghodrat, & Philp, 2026).
- Models trained on different text mixtures and post-training alignment procedures hold measurably different baseline evaluations on identical consumer attitude constructs (Wadi, Ghodrat, & Philp, 2026).
- An attitude measured in a language model is a statistical regularity in its conditional output distribution rather than a conscious affective state experienced by a machine.
- Nevertheless, these statistical dispositions have real marketplace consequences whenever they steer which brands, countries of origin, or product attributes an assistant recommends to users.
- Brand managers and researchers must therefore audit attitude scales across multiple frontier models rather than assuming all AI assistants share a single neutral baseline.

## Discussion: Designing an AI Advisory Interface for Financial Planning
- Discussion: A retail bank is introducing an AI-powered advisor inside its mobile app to help customers choose between two products: (1) refinancing a fixed-rate mortgage based on interest-rate math and (2) selecting a socially responsible investment portfolio that reflects the customer's personal ethics. Drawing on algorithm aversion, algorithm appreciation, task objectivity (Castelo, Bos, & Lehmann, 2019), and the Elaboration Likelihood Model, how should the bank design the source attribution, human-in-the-loop controls, and message framing for each of the two advisory journeys?

## Key Takeaways
- Attitudes are lasting evaluations of objects that serve utilitarian, value-expressive, ego-defensive, and knowledge functions for the consumer.
- The ABC model decomposes attitudes into affect, behavior, and cognition, which form through the standard learning, low-involvement, or experiential hierarchy depending on category involvement.
- Multi-attribute models and the Theory of Planned Behavior explain how attribute beliefs, importance weights, subjective social norms, and perceived behavioral control combine to predict purchase intentions.
- The Elaboration Likelihood Model distinguishes central-route persuasion driven by argument quality under high involvement from peripheral-route persuasion driven by surface cues under low involvement.
- Persuasive impact depends on source credibility (expertise and trustworthiness), source attractiveness (governed by the match-up hypothesis), and gain versus loss message framing.
- Consumers exhibit algorithm aversion after observing errors and algorithm appreciation on objective tasks, while reliable measurement of AI models' attitudes requires repeated sampling or token probability distributions across validated scales.

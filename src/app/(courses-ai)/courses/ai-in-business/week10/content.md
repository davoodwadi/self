## Title Slide
- Week 10
- Sustainable AI leadership requires accounting for the physical energy, water, and hardware footprint of compute, engineering leaner inference pipelines, and aligning technical capability with long-term governance and stewardship.

## The Physical Materiality of Cloud and AI Compute [no-exercise]
- Everyday metaphors such as "the cloud" and "virtual assistants" make artificial intelligence sound weightless and detached from the physical environment.
- In reality, every model training run and every API prompt executes inside industrial data centers filled with thousands of high-wattage server racks, substations, and cooling towers.
- Rapid growth in foundation models and agentic workflows has turned electricity generation, grid transmission capacity, and municipal cooling water into binding operational constraints.
- Corporate sustainability mandates and financial disclosure rules increasingly require enterprises to measure and report the environmental footprint of their digital infrastructure.
- Managing AI responsibly therefore requires understanding both its physical resource consumption and its potential to accelerate environmental solutions.

## The Dual Relationship Between AI and Sustainability [exercise]
[figure: two-sided framework comparing the direct environmental footprint of AI infrastructure (electricity, water, embodied hardware carbon) against AI applications that optimize grids, logistics, and climate modeling]
- The relationship between artificial intelligence and environmental sustainability has two distinct sides that managers must evaluate separately.
- The first side is the sustainability of AI (often called Green AI): measuring and reducing the electricity, carbon emissions, water, and hardware waste generated by building and running models.
- The second side is AI for sustainability: applying machine learning, computer vision, and mathematical optimization to reduce emissions in energy grids, transportation, buildings, and manufacturing.
- Treating these two sides as interchangeable leads to flawed accounting, because an inefficient internal chatbot does not become green simply because another industry uses AI for solar forecasting.
- Sound corporate stewardship minimizes the direct footprint of every enterprise AI workload while targeting high-impact operational problems where better predictions materially cut physical waste.

## Part 1: The Operational Cost of Training and Inference [no-exercise]
- Running artificial intelligence models consumes electricity to power processors and water to dissipate the resulting heat.
- Understanding how resource demand splits between one-time model training and continuous daily inference clarifies where enterprise engineering choices have the greatest impact.

## Training Energy Versus Lifetime Inference Energy [exercise]
[figure: cumulative energy consumption crossover chart showing a high upfront training energy spike surpassed over time by continuous daily production inference across millions of queries]
- The operational energy lifecycle of a machine learning model consists of two phases: training (learning model weights from data) and inference (generating predictions or responses in production).
- Training a large frontier foundation model is compute-intensive, consuming tens of gigawatt-hours of electricity across thousands of graphics processing units (GPUs) over several months.
- However, once a model is deployed to millions of users or embedded inside automated enterprise workflows, inference runs continuously billions of times.
- Across a popular model's commercial lifetime, cumulative electricity consumed by inference typically accounts for 70 to 90 percent of total operational energy demand.
- Furthermore, energy per query varies by orders of magnitude across model types: scoring a tabular row takes a tiny fraction of a watt-hour, whereas generating an image or a long reasoning chain consumes thousands of times more energy.

## Grid Carbon Intensity and Power Usage Effectiveness [no-exercise]
[figure: operational carbon emissions equation multiplying compute electricity demand by data center Power Usage Effectiveness and local electrical grid carbon intensity]
- The greenhouse gas emissions produced by running an AI workload depend not only on how much electricity the processors use, but also on data center efficiency and where the electricity is generated.
- Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE) is the ratio of total energy entering a data center facility to the energy consumed directly by the computing equipment inside it.
- An ideal PUE is 1.0, whereas a typical hyperscale data center operates near 1.10 to 1.20, meaning an extra 10 to 20 percent of electricity goes to cooling fans, chillers, and power conversion.
- Grid carbon intensity measures the grams of carbon dioxide equivalent ($\text{gCO}_2\text{e}$) emitted per kilowatt-hour ($\text{kWh}$) of electricity generated on a regional power grid.
- Running the exact same model training job in a region powered by hydroelectricity or nuclear energy emits a small fraction of the carbon produced by running it on a coal- or gas-heavy grid.

## Data Center Cooling and Water Consumption [no-exercise]
[figure: data center thermal management loop illustrating on-site evaporative cooling tower water consumption alongside off-site thermoelectric power plant water usage]
- Almost all electrical energy entering an AI accelerator chip is converted into heat that must be removed continuously to prevent hardware failure.
- Many large data centers dissipate heat through evaporative cooling towers, which evaporate clean water into the atmosphere to chill the liquid circulating through server racks.
- Environmental accounting distinguishes between water withdrawal (total water drawn from a local watershed) and water consumption (evaporated water that is not returned to the local water supply).
- AI workloads also drive indirect off-site water consumption because thermoelectric coal, gas, and nuclear power plants evaporate water to spin steam turbines when generating the data center's electricity.
- Siting data centers involves a sharp geographic trade-off: arid desert regions offer abundant solar energy with low carbon intensity, but face severe municipal and agricultural water scarcity.

## Part 2: Hardware Lifecycles and Embodied Emissions [no-exercise]
- The environmental footprint of artificial intelligence begins in mines, chemical refineries, and semiconductor fabrication plants long before a server is plugged into an electrical outlet.
- Complete lifecycle assessment accounts for embodied hardware carbon, critical mineral supply chains, and electronic waste alongside operational electricity.

## Embodied Carbon Versus Operational Carbon [exercise]
[figure: server lifecycle timeline separating upfront embodied carbon from mineral extraction, wafer fabrication, and assembly from operational electricity carbon and end-of-life disposal]
- Embodied carbon is the sum of all greenhouse gas emissions released during the extraction of raw materials, semiconductor manufacturing, assembly, and transport of computing hardware before use.
- Operational carbon is the ongoing emissions released from generating the electricity that powers and cools the hardware during its working life.
- Manufacturing advanced AI accelerators, high-bandwidth memory, and solid-state drives requires ultra-pure silicon wafers, high-temperature cleanrooms, and fluorinated etching gases with high global-warming potential.
- As cloud operators procure more renewable electricity to lower their operational carbon, upfront embodied carbon accounts for a steadily growing share of a server's total lifecycle emissions.
- Retiring functional AI servers after only two or three years to install the newest GPU generation sharply increases annualized embodied emissions per unit of compute.

## Semiconductor Supply Chains, Critical Minerals, and E-Waste [no-exercise]
- Modern AI infrastructure depends on a concentrated global supply chain of critical minerals, including high-purity silicon, copper, cobalt, palladium, tantalum, and rare-earth elements.
- Semiconductor fabrication facilities (fabs) also consume millions of gallons of ultra-pure deionized water daily to rinse microscopic circuits on silicon wafers.
- Because specialized AI chip architectures evolve rapidly, data centers face pressure to replace accelerator racks every three to five years, generating specialized electronic waste (e-waste).
- Extending the useful lifespan of hardware by even one or two years is one of the most effective levers for reducing embodied environmental impact.
- Circular hardware practices cascade older generation GPUs from frontier training clusters down to lower-intensity enterprise inference and batch analytics before certified mineral recycling.

## Scope 1, 2, and 3 Emissions Reporting for Enterprise AI [no-exercise]
[figure: Greenhouse Gas Protocol classification for enterprise computing across Scope 1 on-site fuel, Scope 2 purchased electricity, and Scope 3 cloud vendor and hardware supply chain emissions]
- Corporate climate disclosures follow the Greenhouse Gas (GHG) Protocol, which divides an organization's emissions into Scope 1, Scope 2, and Scope 3.
- Scope 1 covers direct emissions from company-owned facilities, such as testing diesel backup generators at an on-premise data center.
- Scope 2 covers indirect emissions from purchased electricity, steam, and cooling used to run hardware that the company owns or controls directly.
- Scope 3 covers all other indirect value-chain emissions, including the embodied carbon of purchased servers and the electricity consumed by third-party cloud providers and SaaS AI vendors.
- Because most enterprises access foundation models through external cloud APIs, their AI carbon footprint sits largely inside Scope 3, requiring cloud vendors to report auditable per-customer compute emissions.

## Part 3: Green AI Practices and Efficient Engineering [no-exercise]
- Unlike many environmental initiatives that require paying a financial premium to reduce emissions, Green AI engineering directly lowers cloud computing bills and improves application speed.
- Right-sizing models, compressing weights, caching repeated queries, and scheduling batch jobs on clean grids align financial return on investment with environmental stewardship.

## Red AI Versus Green AI: Right-Sizing the Model to the Task [exercise]
[figure: accuracy-versus-energy Pareto curve comparing a 100-billion-parameter frontier model, a fine-tuned 7-billion-parameter language model, and a tabular gradient-boosted tree on an enterprise classification task]
- Computer scientists distinguish between Red AI, which chases marginal benchmark gains by scaling model size and compute regardless of cost, and Green AI, which treats efficiency as a primary evaluation metric.
- A pervasive source of waste in enterprise AI is deploying a massive, general-purpose frontier language model to perform narrow, repetitive tasks.
- Calling a 100-billion-parameter reasoning model to predict customer churn from a table or classify support tickets into five folders consumes up to a hundred times more energy than necessary.
- Right-sizing matches the model class to the complexity of the task: using tabular gradient-boosted trees for structured scoring, compact small language models (SLMs) for routine extraction, and frontier models only for hard synthesis.
- Tiered model routing cascades requests so a lightweight classifier handles the easy 80 percent of traffic and escalates only ambiguous queries to a larger model, cutting both energy and API spend.

## Model Distillation, Quantization, and Semantic Caching [exercise]
[figure: three-part efficient inference pipeline illustrating knowledge distillation from teacher to student model, weight quantization from 16-bit to 4-bit precision, and semantic query caching]
- Engineering teams use three practical techniques to cut the energy and memory required for every production inference call: distillation, quantization, and caching.
- Knowledge distillation uses a large, accurate teacher model to label domain examples and trains a much smaller student model to reproduce that behavior at a fraction of the compute cost.
- Quantization reduces the numerical precision of a model's weights, such as converting 16-bit floating-point numbers into 8-bit or 4-bit integers, shrinking memory bandwidth and energy per token with minimal accuracy loss.
- Prompt caching and semantic caching store the computed representations of long system instructions and previously answered customer questions.
- When thousands of users ask variations of the same policy or product question, semantic caching returns the verified answer from fast memory rather than running the neural network repeatedly.

## Carbon-Aware Spatiotemporal Workload Scheduling [no-exercise]
[figure: 24-hour regional grid carbon intensity curve showing flexible batch training and analytics jobs shifted to midday solar peaks and cleaner hydro-powered cloud regions]
- Not every enterprise AI workload has the same latency requirement: live customer chat must respond in under a second, whereas nightly model retraining or document indexing can wait several hours.
- Carbon-aware scheduling exploits the fact that electrical grid carbon intensity varies across both time of day and geographic region.
- Temporal shifting delays flexible batch jobs, such as fine-tuning runs, large-scale batch scoring, and evaluation suites, by a few hours so they execute during midday solar peaks or overnight wind surpluses.
- Spatial shifting routes location-independent training and batch processing workloads to cloud data centers situated on low-carbon hydro, nuclear, or wind-heavy electrical grids.
- Cloud orchestration schedulers can automate both shifts using real-time grid emissions APIs, cutting operational carbon by 20 to 50 percent with zero change to model code.

## Part 4: AI for Climate Mitigation and Decarbonization [no-exercise]
- Beyond reducing its own compute footprint, artificial intelligence serves as a high-leverage analytical tool for decarbonizing physical industries, energy infrastructure, and supply chains.
- Evaluating AI-for-sustainability projects requires distinguishing genuine physical emissions reductions from rebound effects and unverified greenwashing claims.

## Renewable Grid Integration and Building Energy Optimization [no-exercise]
[figure: smart electrical grid control loop where machine learning forecasts wind and solar output to coordinate grid battery dispatch and commercial building HVAC demand]
- Transitioning electrical grids from fossil fuels to wind and solar power creates a forecasting challenge because weather-driven generation fluctuates minute by minute while grid supply must match demand continuously.
- Multi-horizon machine learning models combine satellite cloud imagery, numerical weather data, and smart-meter telemetry to forecast regional solar and wind generation hours ahead.
- Accurate renewable forecasts allow grid operators to charge and discharge grid-scale batteries precisely, reducing the need to keep fossil-fuel peaker plants idling as spinning reserve.
- On the demand side, predictive and reinforcement learning controllers optimize heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC) systems in commercial buildings and industrial cooling plants.
- Pre-cooling buildings when clean renewable electricity is abundant and trimming thermal waste regularly reduces commercial building energy consumption by 10 to 25 percent.

## Decarbonizing Supply Chains, Logistics, and Materials Discovery [no-exercise]
[figure: industrial decarbonization applications connecting dynamic freight consolidation, satellite methane plume detection, and AI-guided battery and catalyst material discovery]
- Many of the operational AI applications studied in Week 6 generate direct environmental benefits by eliminating physical waste in manufacturing and transportation.
- Probabilistic demand sensing and censored-demand correction reduce overproduction and food spoilage, preventing the embedded carbon of manufacturing and refrigerating goods that end up in landfills.
- Dynamic vehicle routing and freight consolidation increase truck load factors and reduce empty backhaul miles, cutting diesel fuel consumption across logistics fleets.
- In environmental monitoring, computer vision models applied to satellite and aerial spectroscopy detect invisible fugitive methane leaks from oil and gas pipelines and track illegal deforestation across agricultural supply chains.
- In materials science, graph neural networks screen millions of candidate chemical structures in simulation to accelerate the discovery of low-carbon cement binders, solar photovoltaics, and battery electrolytes.

## Jevons Paradox, Rebound Effects, and Greenwashing Risks [exercise]
[figure: Jevons paradox trajectory showing how a 60 percent drop in energy per AI query lowers unit cost and induces a fivefold expansion in total query volume and aggregate energy demand]
- In resource economics, Jevons paradox (or the rebound effect) observes that when technological progress improves the efficiency with which a resource is used, total consumption of that resource often rises rather than falls.
- As hardware and quantization cut the energy and dollar cost per token by half, organizations do not simply pocket the savings on existing workloads.
- Instead, lower unit costs induce firms to embed continuous background agents, automated summarization, and synthetic media generation into every software interaction, expanding total compute volume faster than per-token efficiency improves.
- Managers must also guard against corporate AI greenwashing: marketing an AI initiative as environmentally beneficial without accounting for its full Scope 2 and Scope 3 compute emissions.
- Credible sustainability accounting compares the verified physical emissions avoided by an AI application directly against the lifecycle compute and hardware emissions required to build and run it.

- Bringing these perspectives together equips leaders to evaluate any future artificial intelligence technology with analytical rigor and institutional judgment.

## The Four Pillars of Enterprise AI Leadership [no-exercise]
[figure: four-pillar executive decision framework integrating Technical Fit, Economic and Strategic Value, Ethical and EDII Governance, and Environmental Stewardship]
- Every consequential AI decision in an organization rests on four integrated pillars developed across this course.
- Pillar 1 is Technical Fit (Weeks 1 and 2): understanding how predictive models, deep neural networks, retrieval-grounded language models, and tool-using agents work and where each fails.
- Pillar 2 is Functional and Strategic Value (Weeks 3 through 8): embedding models into marketing, finance, HR, operations, and product workflows backed by proprietary outcome data and controlled ROI experiments.
- Pillar 3 is Ethical and EDII Governance (Week 9): auditing systems for historical and measurement bias, upholding Indigenous data sovereignty (OCAP), and enforcing human-in-the-loop checkpoints and customer recourse.
- Pillar 4 is Environmental Stewardship (Week 10): right-sizing models, compressing inference pipelines, and managing the energy, water, and hardware lifecycle of enterprise compute.

## Matching the Right AI Architecture to the Business Problem [exercise]
[figure: comprehensive architecture selection matrix matching business problem traits across data structure, latency, explainability, and action risk to the appropriate AI system design]
- A recurring lesson of this course is architectural discipline: starting from the business decision and its constraints rather than from the newest model release.
- When rules and formulas are deterministic and known in advance, standard software and mathematical optimization remain faster, cheaper, and completely verifiable.
- When the task estimates a probability or quantity from structured records under tight latency or legal explainability, calibrated tabular models paired with economic thresholds outperform large language models.
- When the task synthesizes unstructured documents or drafts text grounded in firm policies, retrieval-augmented generation with strict citation checks and schema validation is the right architecture.
- When a system invokes external software tools to execute multi-step workflows, narrow agent permissions, sandboxed execution, and human-in-the-loop sign-off on irreversible actions are mandatory.

## The Enduring Role of Human Judgment and Institutional Leadership [no-exercise]
- We began in Week 1 by separating a decision into prediction (estimating unknown facts from data) and judgment (assigning values, ethics, and error costs to outcomes).
- As artificial intelligence makes prediction, pattern recognition, and draft generation abundant and inexpensive, the strategic value of informed human judgment increases rather than disappears.
- An algorithm can estimate the probability that a borrower defaults, a customer cancels, or a machine bearing fails, but it cannot decide what level of risk, fairness trade-off, or environmental impact an institution should stand for.
- Nor can a model trained on past data recognize on its own when a macroeconomic regime shift, regulatory reform, or social change has rendered historical patterns obsolete.
- The responsibility of business leaders is to pair technical fluency with institutional stewardship: designing organizations where intelligent systems amplify human capability, accountability, and long-term value.

## Key Takeaways
- Artificial intelligence relies on physical infrastructure that consumes substantial electricity, cooling water, and critical minerals across data centers and semiconductor supply chains.
- For widely deployed models, lifetime production inference consumes far more total energy than initial training, while short hardware refresh cycles increase Scope 3 embodied carbon and e-waste.
- Green AI practices, including right-sizing models to the task, model routing, knowledge distillation, weight quantization, semantic caching, and carbon-aware scheduling, reduce both environmental impact and cloud inference costs.
- AI supports corporate decarbonization in renewable grid balancing, commercial HVAC control, logistics routing, and methane detection, but leaders must account for Jevons paradox rebound effects and avoid unverified greenwashing claims.
- Effective AI leadership synthesizes four pillars across every deployment: technical fit, experimental and strategic ROI, EDII and OCAP governance, and environmental stewardship.
- Across every business function, machines supply scalable predictions and candidate actions, while human leaders remain accountable for problem formulation, ethical boundaries, and high-stakes judgment.

## Title Slide
- Week 5
- Marketing research and data analytics reduce uncertainty in managerial decisions by systematically collecting and interpreting secondary and primary evidence, while verifying outputs from AI research assistants and synthetic respondents against real customer data.

## Customer Insights and Evidence-Based Marketing [no-exercise]
- Marketing managers make decisions under uncertainty about how consumers will respond to new products, price changes, distribution shifts, and promotional campaigns.
- Customer insights are fresh, actionable understandings of customers and the marketplace derived from marketing information that become the basis for creating customer value and relationships.
- Raw sales figures or web traffic counts are merely data points: they become customer insights only when analysis reveals the underlying reason behind buyer behavior.
- Organizations build a marketing information system (MIS) to assess information needs, develop the needed information, and help decision makers use it to generate customer insights.

## Three Sources of Marketing Information [no-exercise]
- A company's marketing information system draws continuously on three complementary sources of evidence: internal data, marketing intelligence, and marketing research.
- Internal databases are electronic collections of consumer and market information obtained from data sources within the company's own network, including accounting, operations, sales, and customer service records.
- Competitive marketing intelligence is the systematic monitoring, collection, and analysis of publicly available information about consumers, competitors, and developments in the marketplace.
- Marketing research is the systematic design, collection, analysis, and reporting of data relevant to a specific marketing situation facing an organization.

## Part 1: The Four-Step Marketing Research Process [no-exercise]
- Every marketing research project follows a structured four-step sequence: defining the problem and research objectives, developing the research plan, implementing the research plan, and interpreting and reporting the findings.
- Because every subsequent decision depends on the initial problem formulation, managers and researchers must collaborate closely from the first step to the final report.

## Step 1: Defining the Problem and Research Objectives [exercise]
[figure: four-step marketing research process progressing from problem definition and research plan development to data implementation and managerial reporting]
- Defining the problem and agreeing on research objectives is often the most difficult and consequential step in the research process.
- A manager may observe a symptom, such as a sudden drop in store traffic, without knowing the underlying cause, such as a competitor's promotion or a change in local transit routes.
- Once the problem is defined precisely, the researcher sets one of three types of research objectives.
- Exploratory research gathers preliminary information that helps define the problem and suggest hypotheses.
- Descriptive research describes things such as the market potential for a product or the demographics and attitudes of consumers, while causal research tests hypotheses about cause-and-effect relationships.

## Step 2: Developing the Research Plan [no-exercise]
- In the second step, the researcher determines the exact information needed, develops a plan for gathering it efficiently, and presents the written proposal to management.
- The research plan outlines sources of existing secondary data and specifies the primary research approaches, contact methods, sampling plans, and research instruments required to gather new data.
- Before approving the plan, management evaluates whether the expected value of the insights in improving the decision exceeds the financial cost and time required to conduct the study.
- A study whose findings arrive after the launch deadline or cost more than the decision at stake is not economically justified.

## Steps 3 and 4: Implementing the Plan and Reporting Findings [no-exercise]
- In Step 3, the researcher puts the marketing research plan into action by collecting, processing, and analyzing the information.
- Data collection is normally the most expensive and error-prone phase of the project, requiring careful checks for respondent non-response, interviewer bias, and recording errors.
- Data processing and analysis isolate important findings by tabulating results, computing summary statistics, and testing the research hypotheses.
- In Step 4, the researcher interprets the findings, draws conclusions, and reports them to management in plain decision-oriented language rather than technical statistical jargon alone.

## Part 2: Secondary Data Versus Primary Data [no-exercise]
- Researchers usually begin by gathering secondary data before collecting any primary data.
- Distinguishing the two data types and evaluating their quality ensures that research budgets are spent only on questions that existing records cannot answer.

## Secondary Data: Sources, Advantages, and Limitations [exercise]
[figure: comparison of internal and external secondary data sources with primary data collection methods across speed, cost, and specificity]
- Secondary data consist of information that already exists somewhere, having been collected previously for another purpose.
- Sources of secondary data include the company's internal database as well as external sources such as government census agencies, trade associations, commercial data syndicators, and academic publications.
- Secondary data can generally be obtained much faster and at a fraction of the cost of primary data, and can provide industry-wide benchmarks that an individual firm could never collect alone.
- However, the specific information needed for a novel decision may not exist in secondary records, or existing data may be outdated, inaccurately collected, or defined in incompatible units.
- Researchers must therefore evaluate all secondary data for relevance, accuracy, currency, and impartiality before relying on them.

## Primary Data Collection: Observational, Survey, and Experimental Approaches [exercise]
- Primary data consist of information collected for the specific purpose at hand, requiring the researcher to choose among three core research approaches: observation, surveys, and experiments.
- Observational research gathers primary data by observing relevant people, actions, and situations, such as watching how shoppers navigate a grocery aisle or using ethnographic home visits to see how families prepare meals.
- Survey research gathers primary data by asking people questions about their knowledge, attitudes, preferences, and buying behavior, making it the most widely used method for descriptive research.
- Experimental research gathers primary data by selecting matched groups of subjects, giving them different treatments (such as two prices or two ad layouts), controlling unrelated factors, and checking for differences in group responses to establish cause and effect.

## Sampling Plans and Research Instruments [exercise]
- Collecting primary data requires designing a sampling plan that specifies three decisions: the sampling unit (who is to be surveyed), the sample size (how many people should be included), and the sampling procedure (how respondents should be chosen).
- A probability sample (such as a simple random sample or stratified random sample) gives every population member a known chance of selection and allows researchers to calculate confidence intervals and sampling error.
- A non-probability sample (such as a convenience sample or quota sample) is faster and less expensive when probability sampling is impractical, though its margin of sampling error cannot be measured exactly.
- Researchers collect primary data using two main instruments: questionnaires (containing closed-ended scale items or open-ended text questions) and mechanical or physiological devices (such as checkout scanners and eye-tracking cameras).

## Part 3: Data Analytics, Big Data, and CRM [no-exercise]
- Digital transactions, mobile apps, connected products, and social media generate vast streams of behavioral data that traditional manual reporting cannot process.
- Marketing analytics and customer relationship management (CRM) transform these raw data streams into individualized customer value.

## Big Data and the Role of Marketing Analytics [exercise]
- Big Data refers to the huge and complex data sets generated by today's sophisticated information generation, collection, storage, and analysis technologies.
- The primary managerial challenge is not collecting more data, but sifting through overwhelming data volume to extract timely, relevant insights.
- Marketing analytics consists of the analysis tools, technologies, and processes by which marketers dig out meaningful patterns in big data to gain customer insights and gauge marketing performance.
- Organizations apply descriptive analytics to summarize past performance, predictive analytics to forecast future customer behavior (such as churn probability), and prescriptive analytics to optimize pricing and media allocation.

## Customer Relationship Management (CRM) and Touchpoint Integration [no-exercise]
[figure: customer relationship management cycle integrating sales, service, and marketing touchpoints into a unified customer profile]
- Customer Relationship Management (CRM) in an analytics context involves managing detailed information about individual customers and carefully managing customer touchpoints to maximize customer loyalty.
- A customer touchpoint is any occasion on which a customer encounters the brand and product, from actual experience to personal or mass communications and casual observation.
- For a hotel guest, touchpoints include reservation websites, check-in desks, room cleanliness, room-service billing, and post-stay loyalty emails.
- Modern CRM platforms integrate data from sales, customer support, website browsing, and payment systems so the organization recognizes the customer consistently across every channel.

## Marketing Research Ethics and Consumer Privacy [no-exercise]
- As firms collect granular behavioral and location data, marketing research and analytics raise acute ethical responsibilities toward respondents and consumers.
- Intrusiveness and privacy breaches occur when companies track consumers without informed consent or repurpose personal research responses for targeted sales pitches.
- Soliciting sales under the guise of conducting marketing research is known as sugging, and raising funds under the guise of research is known as frugging: both practices violate professional research codes and erode public cooperation.
- Ethical research requires obtaining informed consent, anonymizing respondent identities, securing stored records, and reporting methodological limitations honestly to clients.

## Part 4: Artificial Intelligence in Marketing Research [no-exercise]
- Artificial intelligence now assists marketers across desk research, qualitative coding, questionnaire drafting, and early-stage survey pretesting.
- Understanding how AI tools generate outputs explains why verification against primary human evidence remains mandatory.

## AI-Assisted Secondary Research and Qualitative Analysis [no-exercise]
- In secondary research, large language models can rapidly search, summarize, and cross-reference hundreds of industry reports, earnings transcripts, and regulatory filings.
- In qualitative and survey analysis, AI tools can transcribe interviews, translate open-ended responses across languages, and classify thousands of customer comments into recurring themes.
- However, generative AI models can also fabricate non-existent citations, invent plausible-looking market statistics, or omit crucial sample caveats from a source document.
- Every numerical claim or reference produced by an AI research assistant must therefore be verified directly against the original source document before being included in a management report.

## Synthetic Survey Respondents in Early-Stage Research [exercise]
[figure: comparison of synthetic AI survey responses used for early questionnaire pretesting against primary survey data from real human consumers]
- Researchers increasingly experiment with asking large language models to complete marketing questionnaires as if they were consumers with specific demographic profiles, producing what are called synthetic responses.
- Synthetic responses can be generated in minutes at minimal cost, allowing researchers to test whether survey questions are clear and whether scales capture variation before paying for a human panel.
- Empirical studies show that language models can reproduce directional patterns observed in real consumer data, such as downward-sloping demand curves and trade-offs between price and brand quality (Brand, Israeli, & Ngwe, 2023).
- Synthetic pretesting is therefore valuable for refining questionnaire wording, spotting ambiguous response options, and generating initial hypotheses for subsequent primary studies.

## Methodological Limits of Synthetic Respondents [no-exercise]
- Despite their speed, synthetic responses have fundamental methodological limits that prevent them from replacing primary research on real consumers.
- First, language models generate answers probabilistically: the exact same model asked the identical survey question across repeated independent runs can give different responses (Wadi & Fredette, 2025).
- Second, language models reflect patterns in their historical training text rather than lived physical experience, so they cannot taste a new beverage formulation or feel the fabric of a garment.
- Third, synthetic responses can under-represent minority or emerging consumer segments and conform to subtle framing cues in the researcher's prompt.
- Consequential decisions about real customers therefore still require primary empirical data collected from real human respondents.

## Part 5: Researching How AI Shopping Agents Behave [no-exercise]
- Beyond using AI as a research tool, marketers now face a new subject of marketing research: the AI shopping agents that search and buy on consumers' behalf.
- Auditing and experimenting on AI agents allows brands to understand how automated intermediaries evaluate their offerings.

## The AI Shopping Agent as a Subject of Marketing Research [no-exercise]
- When consumers delegate product search and comparison to AI assistants, the assistant acts as a gatekeeper that filters the market before the human buyer sees a shortlist.
- Traditional consumer surveys ask human shoppers how they perceive a brand, but they do not reveal how an AI shopping agent parses the brand's online catalog or product page.
- Marketing research must therefore expand its unit of observation to study both human consumers and the AI agents that shop for them.
- Researchers examine which product attributes AI agents inspect, how agents weight price against ratings, and whether page layout or sponsorship labels alter agent recommendations.

## Descriptive Audits and Controlled Experiments on AI Agents [exercise]
- Researchers study AI shopping agents using the same descriptive and causal designs applied in consumer research.
- In a descriptive audit, researchers submit hundreds of standardized shopping prompts to major AI assistants and record how frequently each brand is recommended and in what rank position.
- In a controlled experiment, researchers hold the product catalog constant while manipulating one variable at a time, such as whether unit prices are visible on the main search page or require an extra click to view (Wadi & Ma, 2026b).
- Because a single model run can vary probabilistically, researchers repeat each experimental prompt across multiple independent sessions to estimate agent behavior reliably (Wadi & Fredette, 2025).

## Designing Actionable Reports from Human and Agent Evidence [no-exercise]
- A research study creates value only when its findings are translated into clear managerial decisions across the marketing mix.
- When reporting research that combines human consumer studies and AI agent audits, analysts separate behavioral insights about end-user satisfaction from technical insights about catalog discoverability.
- Researchers also document every stage where AI tools were used in literature review, coding, or pretesting so decision makers can evaluate the strength of the underlying evidence.
- Grounding strategic recommendations in verified primary evidence protects the firm from acting on unverified assumptions or model hallucinations.

## Discussion: Evaluating a Proposal to Replace Human Surveys with Synthetic Personas

## Key Takeaways
- Customer insights are actionable understandings of buyers and markets generated by a firm's marketing information system across internal data, competitive marketing intelligence, and marketing research.
- The marketing research process follows four steps: defining the problem and research objectives (exploratory, descriptive, or causal), developing the research plan, implementing data collection and analysis, and interpreting and reporting findings.
- Secondary data (already collected for another purpose) provide rapid, low-cost context but must be checked for relevance, accuracy, currency, and impartiality before collecting primary data.
- Primary data collection combines research approaches (observation, surveys, and experiments), sampling plans (probability and non-probability samples), and research instruments (questionnaires and mechanical devices).
- Marketing analytics and CRM systems integrate big data across customer touchpoints to uncover behavioral patterns and build long-term loyalty while respecting consumer privacy and research ethics.
- AI accelerates secondary literature reviews and survey pretesting through synthetic responses (Brand, Israeli, & Ngwe, 2023), but fabricated citations and response variability across runs (Wadi & Fredette, 2025) require human verification and primary data from real buyers.

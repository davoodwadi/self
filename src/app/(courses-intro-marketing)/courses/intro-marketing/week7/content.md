## Title Slide
- Week 7
- Product and service strategy is the foundation of the marketing mix, governing how organizations classify offerings, manage the product life cycle, develop new products, build brand equity, and manage the characteristics of human-delivered and AI-delivered services.

## Defining a Product and the Three Levels of an Offering [no-exercise]
[figure: three concentric levels of a product: core customer value at the center, actual product in the middle ring, and augmented product in the outer ring]
- A product is anything that can be offered to a market for attention, acquisition, use, or consumption that might satisfy a want or need, encompassing physical objects, services, events, persons, places, organizations, and ideas.
- Product planners design offerings across three concentric levels.
- The first and most basic level is core customer value, which addresses the question: "What is the buyer really buying?" (for example, a buyer of a drill bit is buying a quarter-inch hole, and a buyer of a tablet is buying mobile entertainment and productivity).
- The second level is the actual product, which turns the core benefit into a tangible design with product features, styling, quality level, a brand name, and packaging.
- The third level is the augmented product, which surrounds the core benefit and actual product with additional consumer services and benefits such as warranty coverage, free tech support, financing, and repair services.

## Social Responsibility in Product and Service Decisions [no-exercise]
- Product and service decisions carry major legal and ethical responsibilities regarding product safety, warranty honesty, and environmental sustainability.
- Manufacturers must comply with strict safety regulations and are legally liable when defective designs or inadequate warnings injure consumers.
- When a product defect is discovered after launch, rapid, transparent product recalls protect consumer welfare and preserve long-run brand trust far better than denial or delay.
- Designing durable, repairable products and recyclable packaging reduces lifecycle environmental impact while meeting rising consumer and regulatory expectations.

## Part 1: Product Classifications and the Product Life Cycle [no-exercise]
- Marketers classify products into consumer and industrial categories based on how buyers shop for and use them, and manage offerings across the five stages of the Product Life Cycle (PLC).
- Matching marketing strategy to both the product classification and the current lifecycle stage prevents misallocated promotional and distribution spending.

## Consumer Product Classifications [exercise]
- Consumer products are goods and services bought by final consumers for personal consumption, classified into four groups according to how consumers go about buying them.
- Convenience products are consumer products and services that the customer usually buys frequently, immediately, and with minimal comparison and buying effort (such as laundry detergent, candy, and fast food), priced low and placed in widespread locations.
- Shopping products are less frequently purchased consumer products and services that customers compare carefully on suitability, quality, price, and style (such as furniture, clothing, and major appliances), distributed through fewer outlets with deeper sales support.
- Specialty products have unique characteristics or brand identification for which a significant group of buyers is willing to make a special purchase effort (such as luxury watches or sports cars), and unsought products are items the consumer either does not know about or does not normally think of buying (such as life insurance or funeral services).

## Industrial Products and Other Marketable Entities [no-exercise]
- Industrial products are those purchased for further processing or for use in conducting a business, so the distinction between a consumer product and an industrial product is based on the purpose for which the item is bought.
- If a person buys a lawn mower for use around a home, the mower is a consumer product, whereas if the same person buys the identical lawn mower for use in a commercial landscaping business, it is an industrial product.
- Industrial products divide into three groups: materials and parts (raw materials and manufactured components), capital items (industrial installations and accessory equipment), and supplies and services (operating supplies and repair or advisory services).
- Beyond tangible goods and services, organizations also apply product marketing principles to market experiences, organizations, persons, places, and social ideas.

## Stages of the Product Life Cycle: Development to Growth [exercise]
[figure: product life cycle curves showing industry sales and profits across product development, introduction, growth, maturity, and decline stages]
- The Product Life Cycle (PLC) traces the course of a product's sales and profits over its lifetime across five stages: product development, introduction, growth, maturity, and decline.
- Product development begins when the company finds and develops a new product idea: during this stage, sales are zero and the company's investment costs mount.
- Introduction is a period of slow sales growth as the product is introduced in the market, where profits are nonexistent because of the heavy expenses of product introduction and the primary goal is building awareness and trial.
- Growth is a period of rapid market acceptance and increasing profits as early adopters continue to buy and later buyers start following their lead, attracting new competitors and prompting the firm to expand distribution and add product features.

## Maturity and Decline Stages of the PLC [no-exercise]
- Maturity is a period of slowdown in sales growth because the product has achieved acceptance by most potential buyers, causing industry profits to level off or decline under intense price and promotional competition.
- Product managers prevent stagnation during maturity by modifying the market (finding new users or usage occasions), modifying the product (improving quality, features, or style), or modifying the marketing mix.
- Decline is the period when sales fall off and profits drop due to technological advances, shifts in consumer tastes, or increased domestic and foreign competition.
- Management must regularly audit aging products and decide whether to maintain the brand without change, harvest the product (reducing support costs to extract remaining cash flow), or drop the product from the line.

## Part 2: The New Product Development Process and AI [no-exercise]
- Because existing products eventually enter maturity and decline, firms must excel at developing new products through either acquisition or internal research and development.
- To reduce the high risk of new-product failure, organizations manage innovation through a systematic eight-step new product development process, now accelerated in its early steps by artificial intelligence.

## Idea Generation to Marketing Strategy Development [exercise]
[figure: eight-step new product development pipeline from idea generation and screening through concept testing, business analysis, product development, test marketing, and commercialization]
- Step 1 is idea generation, the systematic search for new product ideas from internal sources (researchers, engineers, and salespeople) and external sources (customers, competitors, distributors, suppliers, and crowdsourcing).
- Step 2 is idea screening, which spots good ideas and drops poor ones as early as possible using structured criteria such as whether the product is real, winnable, and worth doing.
- Step 3 is concept development and testing: a product idea is an idea for a possible product, a product concept is a detailed version of the idea stated in meaningful consumer terms, and concept testing presents the concept to target consumers to gauge their reactions.
- Step 4 is marketing strategy development, designing an initial marketing strategy for introducing the product based on the product concept, specifying the target market, value proposition, and first-year sales and profit goals.

## Business Analysis to Commercialization [no-exercise]
- Step 5 is business analysis, a review of the sales, costs, and profit projections for a new product to find out whether these factors satisfy the company's objectives.
- Step 6 is product development, in which research and development or engineering turns the product concept into a physical product (or functional software prototype) and conducts rigorous functional and consumer tests.
- Step 7 is test marketing, the stage at which the product and its proposed marketing program are tested in realistic market settings (standard, controlled, or simulated test markets) before full-scale expense.
- Step 8 is commercialization, introducing the new product into the market, which requires deciding on launch timing and whether to roll out in a single locality, a region, or internationally.

## Artificial Intelligence in New Product Development [exercise]
- Artificial intelligence now accelerates the early stages of the new product development pipeline while leaving later economic and market validation dependent on real-world evidence.
- In Step 1 (idea generation), generative AI models can analyze thousands of customer complaints, online reviews, and patent filings to propose hundreds of candidate product ideas in minutes, shifting managerial effort toward rigorous screening in Step 2.
- In Step 3 (concept development and testing), marketers can generate rapid visual renderings of product concepts and screen preliminary wording against synthetic AI personas before fielding expensive human concept tests.
- In Step 6 (product development), computer-aided generative design and coding assistants help engineers iterate physical prototypes and software interfaces faster.
- However, Step 5 (business analysis), Step 7 (test marketing), and Step 8 (commercialization) cannot be simulated away: actual unit manufacturing costs, retail shelf acceptance, and repeat purchase rates still depend on real suppliers and real paying customers.

## Discussion: Managing Product Life Cycle Decline and Innovation
- Discussion: A consumer electronics company earns steady cash flow from its mature wired desktop keyboards, while sales of its legacy point-and-shoot digital cameras have fallen by 25 percent per year for five years due to smartphone photography. Using the Product Life Cycle and the eight-step new product development process, evaluate whether the firm should maintain, harvest, or drop the camera line, and explain how it should deploy cash from its keyboard line to fund new product development.

## Part 3: Individual Product Decisions, Branding, and Packaging [no-exercise]
- Beyond developing new offerings, marketers make ongoing strategic decisions regarding individual product attributes, branding, packaging, labeling, and product lines.
- Strong branding and packaging transform a commodity item into a differentiated market offering with enduring customer equity.

## Product Attributes, Packaging, and Labeling [no-exercise]
- Developing a product or service involves defining the benefits that it will offer, communicated and delivered by product attributes such as product quality (performance quality and conformance quality), product features, and product style and design.
- Good design goes beyond superficial skin-deep styling: it begins with observing how customers use the product and shapes both appearance and usefulness.
- Packaging involves designing and producing the container or wrapper for a product: in self-service retail and e-commerce fulfillment, the package protects the item, prevents tampering, reduces shipping damage, and acts as a critical five-second commercial on the shelf.
- Labeling, ranging from simple tags attached to products to complex graphics that are part of the package, identifies the product or brand, describes who made it and what it contains, promotes the brand, and complies with statutory unit-pricing, nutrition, and safety laws.

## Brand Equity and Brand Sponsorship [exercise]
- A brand is a name, term, sign, symbol, or design, or a combination of these, that identifies the maker or seller of a product or service and differentiates it from competitors.
- Brand equity is the differential effect that knowing the brand name has on customer response to the product or its marketing: a brand has positive brand equity when consumers react more favorably to it than to an unbranded or generic version of the same product.
- Building a strong brand starts with brand positioning (anchoring the brand on desirable attributes, benefits, or deep beliefs and values) and selecting a protectable, distinctive brand name.
- A manufacturer has four brand sponsorship options: launching a national (or manufacturer's) brand, selling to resellers who apply a private (or store) brand, marketing under a licensed brand name or character, or joining forces with another firm in a co-branded offering.

## Brand Development and Product Line Strategy [no-exercise]
[figure: four brand development strategies across existing or new brand names and existing or new product categories: line extensions, brand extensions, multibrands, and new brands]
- A company has four choices when it comes to developing brands across existing or new brand names and existing or new product categories.
- Line extensions occur when a company extends existing brand names to new forms, colors, sizes, ingredients, or flavors of an existing product category, whereas brand extensions extend an existing brand name to a new product category.
- Multibrands involve introducing additional brand names in the same product category to appeal to distinct segments and occupy more retailer shelf space, and new brands are created when entering a new category for which none of the company's current brand names are appropriate.
- Managers also coordinate the firm's product lines (by line filling within the current range or line stretching upward or downward) and manage the overall product mix across its width, length, depth, and consistency.

## Part 4: Services Marketing Strategy [no-exercise]
- Services have grown to account for the majority of gross domestic product and employment in advanced economies, spanning banking, healthcare, hospitality, education, and telecommunications.
- Because services are intangible performances rather than physical objects, marketers must manage four distinct service characteristics and three types of service marketing.

## The Four Characteristics of Services [exercise]
[figure: four defining characteristics of services: intangibility, inseparability, variability, and perishability]
- A company must consider four special service characteristics when designing marketing programs: intangibility, inseparability, variability, and perishability.
- Service intangibility means that services cannot be seen, tasted, felt, heard, or smelled before they are bought: to reduce buyer uncertainty, marketers "manage the evidence" by providing concrete signals of quality in their physical facilities, staff appearance, equipment, and communications.
- Service inseparability means that services are produced and consumed at the same time and cannot be separated from their providers, making provider-customer interaction a core part of the product itself.
- Service variability means that the quality of services depends on who provides them and when, where, and how they are provided, while service perishability means that services cannot be stored for later sale or use, requiring differential pricing or reservation systems to match supply and demand.

## The Service Profit Chain and Three Types of Service Marketing [no-exercise]
- Because service quality depends heavily on the interaction between frontline employees and customers, successful service firms focus on both their employees and their customers through the service profit chain.
- The service profit chain links service firm profits with employee and customer satisfaction across five links: internal service quality, satisfied and productive service employees, greater service value, satisfied and loyal customers, and healthy service profits and growth.
- External marketing covers the normal work of preparing, pricing, distributing, and promoting the service to customers.
- Internal marketing means that the service firm must orient and motivate its customer-contact employees and supporting service people to work as a team to provide customer satisfaction, while interactive marketing means that service quality depends heavily on the quality of the buyer-seller interaction during the service encounter.

## Managing Service Differentiation, Quality, and Productivity [no-exercise]
- When customers view the services of different providers as similar, they care less about the provider than the price.
- The solution to price competition is to develop a differentiated offer, delivery, and image through innovative features, superior service delivery processes, and recognizable symbols.
- Delivering consistently high service quality is harder than controlling physical product defects, and requires empowering frontline staff to recover quickly when a service failure occurs: good service recovery can turn angry customers into loyal advocates.
- Finally, service providers must increase service productivity carefully without pushing standardization and cost cutting so far that perceived service quality erodes.

## Part 5: AI-Delivered Services [no-exercise]
- Many customer-facing services are now delivered partly or wholly by artificial intelligence, including automated customer-service agents, diagnostic triage tools, travel booking assistants, and tutoring platforms.
- Examining AI-delivered services through the four service characteristics reveals how automation transforms capacity and inseparability while introducing new forms of variability.

## How Artificial Intelligence Reshapes the Four Service Characteristics [exercise]
[figure: comparison of human-delivered services and AI-delivered services across intangibility, inseparability, variability, and perishability]
- Applying the four service characteristics to AI-delivered services clarifies what changes when software replaces or augments a frontline employee.
- Under inseparability, the service is still produced and consumed simultaneously during the customer's conversation, but delivery is separated from human labor shifts and is available instantly at any hour.
- Under perishability, cloud-hosted AI systems can serve thousands of concurrent customers during a demand spike and scale down during quiet periods, easing the rigid capacity limits of human call centers.
- Under variability, an AI system follows the same software instructions every time, yet large language models remain probabilistic and can return different answers to the identical question across runs (Wadi & Fredette, 2025).
- Under intangibility, customers still cannot inspect the service outcome before using it, so transparent disclosure that the customer is interacting with an AI system is essential for trust.

## Service Quality, Guardrails, and Human Escalation in AI Services [no-exercise]
- Because AI-delivered services exhibit probabilistic response variability (Wadi & Fredette, 2025) and can occasionally hallucinate policy rules, firms must manage AI service quality as rigorously as they train human employees.
- Just as internal marketing prepares frontline workers, deploying an AI service agent requires grounding the model in verified company documentation, setting strict action guardrails, and auditing conversation logs continuously.
- Interactive marketing in an AI setting requires designing the handoff between the automated agent and human specialists.
- Routine, low-risk tasks (such as checking order status or resetting a password) are resolved rapidly by the AI agent, whereas emotionally sensitive, complex, or high-stakes service failures must escalate immediately to an empowered human representative.

## Discussion: Designing a Hybrid Human-and-AI Service Recovery Workflow
- Discussion: A national retail bank replaces half of its telephone customer-service staff with a conversational AI voice assistant to handle account inquiries, card replacements, and disputed fraud charges. Within two months, wait times fall by 70 percent, but customer satisfaction scores among fraud victims drop sharply. Using the four service characteristics, the service profit chain, and response variability in AI systems (Wadi & Fredette, 2025), design a hybrid service workflow that specifies which inquiries the AI assistant should handle and when calls must route immediately to trained human specialists.

## Key Takeaways
- A product is designed across three levels (core customer value, actual product, and augmented product) and classified into consumer products (convenience, shopping, specialty, and unsought) or industrial products.
- The Product Life Cycle (PLC) progresses across five stages (product development, introduction, growth, maturity, and decline), requiring shifts in marketing strategy and eventual decisions to maintain, harvest, or drop aging items.
- The eight-step new product development process moves systematically from idea generation, screening, concept testing, and marketing strategy development to business analysis, product development, test marketing, and commercialization.
- Generative AI accelerates early-stage idea generation, concept rendering, and synthetic pretesting, whereas business analysis, test marketing, and commercialization still require real cost data and real human buyers.
- Brand equity captures the differential value of the brand name, built through brand positioning, name selection, sponsorship (national, store, licensed, or co-branded), and development (line extensions, brand extensions, multibrands, or new brands), supported by functional packaging and compliant labeling.
- Services are defined by intangibility, inseparability, variability, and perishability, and when services are delivered by AI systems, capacity scales more flexibly while probabilistic output variability (Wadi & Fredette, 2025) and intangibility require ongoing quality testing, transparent disclosure, and human escalation paths.

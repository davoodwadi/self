## Title Slide
- Week 3
- Consumer buyer behavior is shaped by cultural, social, personal, and psychological forces across a five-stage decision process that consumers increasingly share with delegated AI shopping agents.

## Defining Consumer Buyer Behavior [no-exercise]
- Consumer buyer behavior refers to the purchasing and consumption behavior of final consumers, namely individuals and households that buy goods and services for personal use.
- All of these final consumers combined make up the consumer market, which accounts for trillions of dollars in annual spending across goods, services, and experiences.
- Although marketers can observe what consumers buy, where they buy, and how much they pay, the underlying reasons why they buy are formed inside the buyer's mind.
- Understanding how consumers respond to different product features, prices, and advertising appeals is essential for designing effective marketing strategy.

## The Stimulus-Response Model of Buyer Behavior [no-exercise]
[figure: stimulus-response model showing marketing and environmental stimuli entering the buyer's black box to produce observable buyer responses]
- Consumer research organizes buyer behavior around a three-part stimulus-response model (often called the buyer's black box model).
- First, marketing stimuli (the Four Ps: product, price, place, and promotion) and broader environmental stimuli (economic, technological, social, and cultural forces) enter the buyer's consciousness.
- Second, the buyer's internal "black box" transforms those stimuli through two components: the buyer's characteristics and the buyer's decision-making process.
- Third, this internal processing produces observable buyer responses, including brand choice, dealer selection, purchase timing, purchase amount, and postpurchase loyalty.

## Part 1: The Five-Step Consumer Decision Process [no-exercise]
- For a new or complex purchase, consumers move through a five-stage decision process: need recognition, information search, evaluation of alternatives, purchase decision, and postpurchase behavior.
- In routine low-involvement purchases, shoppers may skip or compress several stages, whereas high-involvement purchases engage all five stages deliberately.

## Step 1: Need Recognition and Step 2: Information Search [exercise]
[figure: five-stage consumer buyer decision process from need recognition through postpurchase behavior]
- The buying process begins with need recognition, when the buyer perceives a difference between their actual state and a desired state.
- Needs can be triggered by internal stimuli, such as hunger or thirst, or by external stimuli, such as smelling fresh bread from a bakery or seeing a neighbor's new electric bicycle.
- Once a need is recognized, an aroused consumer may undertake an information search across four categories of sources: personal (family and friends), commercial (advertising, websites, and salespeople), public (independent media and consumer rating organizations), and experiential (handling or testing the product).
- Commercial sources typically inform the buyer about available options, whereas personal and independent public sources carry the greatest weight in legitimizing and evaluating brands.

## Step 3: Evaluation of Alternatives and Step 4: Purchase Decision [no-exercise]
- During evaluation of alternatives, the consumer processes information to arrive at brand attitudes and rank the options in their consideration set.
- Depending on the buyer and the category, evaluation may involve careful calculation across multiple attributes (such as fuel economy, safety ratings, and price) or rely on a single heuristic rule or gut impression.
- In the purchase decision stage, the consumer forms an intention to buy the most preferred brand.
- Two factors can intervene between purchase intention and the actual purchase decision: the attitudes of other people (such as a spouse's objection) and unexpected situational factors (such as a job loss or a competitor's sudden promotion).

## Step 5: Postpurchase Behavior and Cognitive Dissonance [exercise]
- The marketer's job continues after the transaction during postpurchase behavior, when the consumer uses the product and compares its perceived performance against prior expectations.
- Almost all major purchases result in cognitive dissonance, which is buyer discomfort caused by postpurchase conflict after accepting the chosen brand's drawbacks and giving up the benefits of rejected alternatives.
- Dissatisfied consumers may return the product, abandon the brand, and warn peers through negative online reviews, whereas satisfied consumers repurchase and become brand advocates.
- Marketers reduce postpurchase dissonance through clear onboarding instructions, responsive customer support, warranties, and follow-up communications that reinforce the wisdom of the buyer's choice.

## Part 2: Cultural, Social, and Personal Influences on Buying [no-exercise]
- A consumer's progression through the decision process is shaped by cultural, social, and personal characteristics.
- Although marketers cannot control these background characteristics directly, they must understand them to design relevant offerings and communications.

## Cultural and Subcultural Factors [no-exercise]
- Culture is the most fundamental determinant of a person's wants and behavior, encompassing the basic values, perceptions, preferences, and behaviors learned from family and societal institutions.
- Marketers continuously monitor cultural shifts, such as growing emphasis on health, fitness, and informal dress, in order to spot demand for new product categories.
- Each culture contains smaller subcultures, which are groups of people with shared value systems based on common life experiences, nationalities, religions, racial groups, or geographic regions.
- Social classes are society's relatively permanent and ordered divisions whose members share similar values, interests, and behaviors, measured as a combination of occupation, income, education, and wealth.

## Social Factors: Groups, Family, and Roles [exercise]
- A consumer's behavior is strongly influenced by social factors, including membership groups, reference groups, family, and social roles and status.
- Reference groups serve as direct (face-to-face) or indirect points of comparison or reference in forming a person's attitudes or behavior, including aspirational groups to which an individual wishes to belong.
- Opinion leaders and online creators within reference groups exert powerful word-of-mouth influence, particularly when products are visible, expensive, or socially risky.
- Family members constitute the most influential primary reference group, with buying roles shifting across spouses and children depending on the product category, while each person's role and status in organizations guide what they buy.

## Personal Factors in Consumer Choice [no-exercise]
- A buyer's decisions are also shaped by personal characteristics that change across life stages and economic circumstances.
- Age and family life-cycle stage alter consumption priorities as households form, raise children, change residences, and enter retirement.
- Occupation and economic situation (spendable income, savings, and borrowing power) directly govern which product categories and price tiers a consumer considers.
- Lifestyle is a person's pattern of living as expressed in their activities, interests, and opinions, capturing how they interact with the world beyond their demographic class.
- Personality and self-concept lead consumers to favor brands whose perceived traits match their own actual or ideal self-image.

## Part 3: Psychological Influences on Consumer Behavior [no-exercise]
- Within the buyer's cultural and social context, four major psychological processes govern how an individual responds to marketing stimuli: motivation, perception, learning, and beliefs and attitudes.
- Each psychological process filters which marketing messages reach awareness and how they are stored in memory.

## Motivation and Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs [no-exercise]
- A motive (or drive) is a need that is sufficiently pressing to direct the person to seek satisfaction of that need.
- Psychologists have developed several theories of human motivation, most notably Sigmund Freud's theory of unconscious motives and Abraham Maslow's hierarchy of needs.
- Maslow's hierarchy arranges human needs in a pyramid from the most pressing at the bottom to the least pressing at the top: physiological needs, safety needs, social needs, esteem needs, and self-actualization needs.
- According to Maslow, a person tries to satisfy the most important need first: when that need is satisfied, it stops being a current motivator and the person turns attention to the next most important need.

## Selective Perception in Marketplace Processing [exercise]
[figure: three perceptual processes filtering marketplace stimuli: selective attention, selective distortion, and selective retention]
- A motivated person is ready to act, and how the person acts is influenced by perception, the process by which people select, organize, and interpret information to form a meaningful picture of the world.
- People can form different perceptions of the exact same stimulus because of three perceptual processes: selective attention, selective distortion, and selective retention.
- Selective attention is the tendency for people to screen out most of the information to which they are exposed, requiring marketers to work hard to attract notice in cluttered environments.
- Selective distortion describes the tendency of people to interpret information in a way that will support what they already believe, and selective retention means that consumers are likely to remember good points made about a brand they favor and forget good points made about competing brands.

## Learning, Beliefs, and Attitudes [no-exercise]
- Learning describes changes in an individual's behavior arising from experience, driven by the interplay of drives, stimuli, cues, responses, and reinforcement.
- When a consumer tries a new coffee shop and enjoys the drink (positive reinforcement), the likelihood of repurchasing on the next morning commute increases.
- Through doing and learning, people acquire beliefs and attitudes that subsequently influence their buying behavior.
- A belief is a descriptive thought that a person holds about something (which may be based on knowledge, opinion, or faith), and an attitude is a person's consistently favorable or unfavorable evaluations, feelings, and tendencies toward an object or idea.
- Because attitudes are costly and difficult to change, marketers generally design offerings to fit existing attitudes rather than attempting to overturn them.

## Discussion: Diagnosing Perceptual Filters and Postpurchase Dissonance
- Discussion: A consumer spends three weeks researching high-end noise-canceling headphones, buys a 400-dollar model, and immediately notices advertisements for a competing brand that offers longer battery life for 50 dollars less. Using selective perception and cognitive dissonance, explain how the buyer is likely to process competitor ads after the purchase and what postpurchase actions the chosen brand should take during the first 14 days of ownership.

## Part 4: Comparing B2C and B2B Purchasing [no-exercise]
- While consumer marketing addresses individuals and households buying for personal consumption, business-to-business (B2B) marketing addresses organizations buying goods and services to produce other products or to run their operations.
- Introducing the core contrasts between B2C and B2B purchasing prepares the ground for Week 4's deep examination of organizational markets.

## Market Structure and Derived Demand [exercise]
- Business marketers typically interact with far fewer but much larger buyers than consumer marketers do: a tire manufacturer may sell millions of replacement tires to individual drivers, but sells original-equipment tires to only a handful of global automakers.
- Business demand is derived demand, meaning it ultimately derives from consumer demand for finished goods: if household purchases of new automobiles fall, automaker demand for sheet steel and tires falls in turn.
- In the short run, B2B demand is also more price-inelastic than B2C demand: a shoe manufacturer will not buy substantially more leather merely because leather prices drop slightly, nor can it easily substitute leather overnight if prices rise.
- B2B demand also exhibits greater volatility (the acceleration effect), whereby a small percentage change in consumer retail demand triggers a much larger percentage swing in business orders for factory equipment.

## The Organizational Buying Unit and Decision Roles [no-exercise]
- Compared with consumer purchases made by an individual or household, a business purchase typically involves more decision participants and a formal buying center.
- The organizational buying center brings together users, influencers, buyers, deciders, and gatekeepers who evaluate the purchase from technical, financial, and operational angles.
- Users are the employees who operate the purchased item, influencers specify technical requirements, buyers handle vendor negotiations, deciders hold final approval authority, and gatekeepers control information flow.
- Because multiple stakeholders participate in a single purchase, B2B marketers must address each participant's distinct evaluation criteria.

## Professional Purchasing and Relationship Orientation [no-exercise]
- Business purchasing is carried out by trained procurement agents who spend their professional careers learning how to evaluate supplier bids and negotiate contracts.
- Whereas many consumer purchases are completed in minutes at a shelf or checkout screen, major B2B purchases follow formal requests for proposals, engineering audits, and multi-month approval cycles.
- In B2B markets, buyers and sellers work closely together over years, often customizing products and logistics to fit a specific client's operations.
- Consequently, B2B marketing relies primarily on consultative personal selling, technical documentation, and key account management rather than mass broadcast advertising.

## Part 5: Delegating the Consumer Decision to AI Agents [no-exercise]
- Consumers can now delegate portions of the five-stage decision process to AI shopping agents that search, compare, and purchase on their behalf.
- Analyzing which stages consumers hand over and which stages they keep reveals how human psychology and agent design interact.

## Which Decision Steps Are Delegated and Which Are Kept [exercise]
[figure: five-stage consumer decision process showing need recognition and postpurchase evaluation kept by the human consumer while information search, evaluation of alternatives, and optional checkout are delegated to the AI agent]
- Across the five-stage decision process, delegation to an AI agent does not replace the consumer uniformly at every step.
- Step 1 (Need Recognition) remains with the human consumer, who experiences the underlying physical or psychological need and states the goal to the AI agent.
- Step 2 (Information Search) and Step 3 (Evaluation of Alternatives) are the stages most heavily delegated: the AI agent queries retail catalogs, reads specifications and reviews, and compares competing brands.
- Step 4 (Purchase Decision) may be executed autonomously by the AI agent for routine items or returned as a shortlist for the human consumer's final approval.
- Step 5 (Postpurchase Behavior) stays entirely with the human consumer, who consumes the physical product and experiences satisfaction or cognitive dissonance.

## Goal Specificity and Lookup Costs in Delegated Search [exercise]
- When consumers delegate search and evaluation to an AI agent, the quality of the agent's choice depends critically on how specific the consumer's instruction is and how costly information is to look up.
- On many retail platforms, an initial search page shows only product titles and total prices, requiring an additional click or page lookup to inspect package weight or unit price.
- In controlled experiments where inspecting detailed product pages carried a lookup cost and the shopper's prompt was vague, AI agents frequently skipped inspecting package weight and selected a coffee listing that cost more per ounce (Wadi & Ma, 2026b).
- When the consumer's prompt stated a specific cost-minimization goal, the AI agents checked the hidden package details and selected the lower unit-price option significantly more often (Wadi & Ma, 2026b).

## Identity-Based Resistance to Delegating Consumption [no-exercise]
- Even when an automated tool or AI agent can perform a task efficiently, consumers deliberately keep certain consumption activities and choices for themselves.
- Research on automation and identity demonstrates that people who view an activity, such as cooking, gardening, or curating music, as central to their self-identity resist products and tools that automate the core skill of that activity (Leung, Paolacci, & Puntoni, 2018).
- For routine, utilitarian chores that carry no identity value (such as restocking paper towels or basic printer ink), consumers welcome full delegation to an automated agent.
- For self-expressive categories, however, consumers prefer AI assistants that augment their own discovery and skill rather than replacing their personal judgment (Leung, Paolacci, & Puntoni, 2018).

## Discussion: Designing for Human and Delegated Decision Stages
- Discussion: A specialty coffee roaster sells whole-bean coffee online in 12-ounce and 16-ounce bags. Some customers are passionate home baristas who enjoy reading tasting notes and farm origins, while others tell an AI shopping assistant to reorder everyday morning coffee automatically. Drawing on identity-based resistance to automation (Leung, Paolacci, & Puntoni, 2018) and AI agent search behavior under lookup costs (Wadi & Ma, 2026b), how should the roaster design its product pages and unit-price disclosures to serve both buyer segments?

## Key Takeaways
- Consumer buyer behavior follows a stimulus-response model in which marketing and environmental stimuli enter the buyer's black box (buyer characteristics and decision processes) to produce observable purchase choices.
- The consumer decision-making process unfolds across five stages: need recognition, information search (across personal, commercial, public, and experiential sources), evaluation of alternatives, purchase decision, and postpurchase behavior.
- Postpurchase satisfaction depends on performance relative to expectations, and marketers actively manage postpurchase cognitive dissonance to build repeat patronage.
- Cultural (culture and subculture), social (reference groups, family, and roles), personal (life-cycle stage, occupation, lifestyle, and personality), and psychological factors (motivation, selective perception, learning, and attitudes) jointly shape consumer preferences.
- Compared with B2C markets, B2B markets involve fewer and larger buyers, derived and relatively inelastic demand, formal multi-person buying centers, and professional procurement processes.
- In delegated shopping, human consumers retain need recognition and postpurchase experience while handing information search and alternative evaluation to AI agents, whose performance depends on prompt specificity and accessible unit-level product data (Wadi & Ma, 2026b), while consumers keep identity-central choices for themselves (Leung, Paolacci, & Puntoni, 2018).

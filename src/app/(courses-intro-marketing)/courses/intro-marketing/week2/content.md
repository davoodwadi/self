## Title Slide
- Week 2
- Marketing strategy operates within a dynamic microenvironment and macroenvironment, requiring organizations to adapt to external forces, uphold corporate social responsibility, and maintain ethical standards toward both consumers and their AI agents.

## Defining the Marketing Environment [no-exercise]
- A company's marketing environment consists of the actors and forces outside marketing that affect marketing management's ability to build and maintain successful relationships with target customers.
- The environment is divided into two concentric layers: the microenvironment close to the firm and the broader societal macroenvironment.
- Environmental forces continuously create new market opportunities while rendering established products, channels, and business models obsolete.
- Rather than treating external change as uncontrollable fate, marketers use marketing research and marketing intelligence to monitor trends early and adapt strategy proactively.

## Environmental Scanning: Reactive Versus Proactive Adaptation [no-exercise]
- Firms differ in how they respond to shifts in their external marketing environment.
- Passive or reactive firms wait until a regulatory change, demographic shift, or new technology erodes sales before adjusting their offerings.
- Proactive firms conduct systematic environmental scanning to detect early signals of change and redesign their product portfolios ahead of competitors.
- Organizations can also shape parts of their environment through public relations, industry standard-setting, and sustainable innovation.

## Part 1: Actors in the Microenvironment [no-exercise]
- The microenvironment consists of the actors close to the company that directly affect its ability to serve its customers and deliver value.
- Delivering customer value requires coordinating internal company departments with external partners, while monitoring competitors and publics.

## Internal Departments and the Value Delivery Network [no-exercise]
[figure: value delivery network connecting suppliers, internal company departments, marketing intermediaries, and target customers]
- In designing marketing plans, marketing management takes other company groups into account, including top management, finance, research and development, purchasing, operations, and accounting.
- All of these interrelated internal groups form the internal environment.
- Under the marketing concept, every department from engineering to billing must "think consumer" and work in harmony to deliver promised customer value.
- If manufacturing cuts material quality to lower unit costs or accounting delays customer refunds, the marketing promise fails regardless of advertising quality.

## Suppliers and Marketing Intermediaries [no-exercise]
- Suppliers form an essential link in the company's overall customer value delivery network by providing the resources needed by the company to produce its goods and services.
- Marketing managers must watch supply availability and costs closely: supply shortages, labor strikes, or shipping delays can cost sales in the short run and damage customer goodwill in the long run.
- Marketing intermediaries are firms that help the company to promote, sell, and distribute its goods to final buyers.
- Intermediaries include resellers (wholesalers and retailers), physical distribution firms (warehousing and freight operators), marketing services agencies (research and advertising firms), and financial intermediaries (banks, credit companies, and insurers).

## Competitors, Publics, and Customer Markets [exercise]
- The marketing concept states that, to be successful, a company must provide greater customer value and satisfaction than its competitors do, adapting its strategy to the size and industry position of rival firms.
- A public is any group that has an actual or potential interest in or impact on an organization's ability to achieve its objectives, including financial, media, government, citizen-action, local, general, and internal publics.
- The company may target any of five types of customer markets: consumer markets (individuals and households), business markets (firms buying for further processing), reseller markets, government markets, and international markets.
- Each actor in the microenvironment can either strengthen or bottleneck the firm's value proposition before it reaches the end buyer.

## Part 2: The Macroenvironment and the PESTLE Framework [no-exercise]
- The company and all actors in its microenvironment operate within a larger macroenvironment of broad societal forces that shape opportunities and pose threats.
- Marketers analyze these macro-level forces systematically using the PESTLE framework: Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Legal, and Environmental factors.

## Political and Economic Forces [exercise]
[figure: six macroenvironmental forces of the PESTLE framework surrounding the firm's microenvironment]
- Political forces encompass government stability, trade policy, taxation, public infrastructure spending, and advocacy groups that influence or restrict business operations.
- A sudden change in import tariffs or domestic industrial subsidies can alter the cost structure of an entire supply chain overnight.
- Economic forces consist of macroeconomic factors that affect consumer purchasing power and spending patterns, including inflation, interest rates, unemployment, and income distribution.
- During periods of high inflation and rising interest rates, consumers postpone discretionary durable purchases and shift toward value-oriented store brands.

## Social and Technological Forces [no-exercise]
- Social (and demographic) forces include population age structure, household composition, geographic migration, educational attainment, and cultural values.
- Core cultural values persist across generations, whereas secondary cultural beliefs, such as attitudes toward remote work, wellness, or convenience foods, shift more rapidly.
- Technological forces are the most dramatic shapers of business destiny, creating entirely new product categories while making older technologies obsolete.
- Advances in cloud computing, mobile payment networks, biotechnology, and artificial intelligence open new ways to create and deliver customer value.

## Legal and Environmental Forces [no-exercise]
- Legal forces consist of specific statutes and regulatory agencies that protect companies from unfair competition, protect consumers from deception or harm, and safeguard society's broader interests.
- Marketers must comply with laws governing product safety, truth in advertising, packaging labels, antitrust competition, and data privacy across every jurisdiction they serve.
- Environmental forces involve the natural resources required as inputs by marketers or affected by marketing and manufacturing activities.
- Growing shortages of raw materials, rising energy costs, pollution limits, and climate policy make environmental stewardship a core operational requirement.

## Part 3: Corporate Social Responsibility and Sustainability [no-exercise]
- Beyond obeying statutory laws, organizations face strong expectations from consumers, employees, and investors to act as accountable stewards of society.
- Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) integrates social and environmental accountability directly into the firm's business model.

## The Four Pillars of Corporate Social Responsibility [exercise]
[figure: Carroll's four-part pyramid of corporate social responsibility from economic and legal foundations to ethical and philanthropic responsibilities]
- Archie Carroll's framework organizes Corporate Social Responsibility into four distinct layers of obligation (Carroll, 1991).
- Economic responsibility is the foundational obligation to produce goods and services that society wants and to sell them at a profit that sustains the enterprise.
- Legal responsibility is the obligation to obey codified laws and regulations, which represent society's baseline rules of fair operations.
- Ethical responsibility is the obligation to do what is right, just, and fair, and to avoid harm even when a questionable practice is not explicitly prohibited by law.
- Philanthropic responsibility involves voluntary corporate contributions of financial, technical, or human resources to improve community well-being and quality of life.

## Sustainable Marketing and the Trap of Greenwashing [exercise]
- Sustainable marketing calls for socially and environmentally responsible actions that meet the current needs of consumers and businesses while preserving or enhancing the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.
- Firms practicing genuine sustainability redesign products to use recycled inputs, reduce packaging waste, lower carbon emissions in logistics, and support fair labor standards.
- Greenwashing occurs when an organization spends more time and money claiming to be environmentally friendly through marketing communications than actually reducing its environmental impact.
- Vague, unverified labels such as "eco-friendly" or "all-natural" on unchanged products mislead buyers, trigger regulatory enforcement, and permanently erode brand trust when exposed.

## The Strategic Business Case for Authentic CSR [no-exercise]
- Authentic Corporate Social Responsibility is not merely a compliance cost or charitable expense, but a source of durable competitive advantage.
- Consumers increasingly evaluate a company's labor, environmental, and governance record when choosing between competing brands of comparable price and quality.
- Strong CSR commitments improve employee recruitment, morale, and retention, reducing costly workforce turnover across retail and corporate operations.
- Proactive resource efficiency, such as designing lighter packaging, reducing warehouse energy consumption, and eliminating toxic inputs, simultaneously lowers operating costs and mitigates regulatory and reputational risk.

## Discussion: Navigating PESTLE Shocks and CSR Commitments
- Discussion: A specialty chocolate manufacturer sources cocoa from West Africa and markets its bars in North America on a pledge of 100 percent living-income farmer pay and deforestation-free supply chains. When extreme weather reduces the regional cocoa harvest and triples raw bean prices, which PESTLE forces are interacting, and how should the brand adjust its pricing, product sizing, and communications without abandoning its CSR commitments or crossing into deceptive practices?

## Part 4: Ethical Considerations in Marketing Practice [no-exercise]
- Conscientious marketers face moral dilemmas across every element of the marketing mix, because actions that boost short-term sales can harm consumers and competitors.
- Establishing explicit corporate marketing ethics policies guides managers on product safety, pricing transparency, distribution fairness, and truthful promotion.

## Deceptive Pricing, Promotion, and Packaging [exercise]
- Deceptive pricing includes falsely advertising "factory" or "wholesale" prices, claiming a large discount from a fabricated reference price, or concealing mandatory fees until checkout.
- Deceptive promotion misrepresents a product's features, ingredients, or performance, or uses bait-and-switch tactics that lure shoppers with an unavailable bargain to upsell a costlier item.
- Deceptive packaging exaggerates package contents through oversized non-functional containers (slack fill), misleading imagery, or subtle reductions in net weight without disclosing the unit price change.
- Regulatory bodies such as the Federal Trade Commission in the United States and the Competition Bureau in Canada prosecute deceptive practices because they distort fair market exchange and injure consumers.

## High-Pressure Selling and Planned Obsolescence [no-exercise]
- High-pressure selling uses psychological manipulation, artificial urgency, or scripted intimidation to persuade people to buy goods they had no intention or need of buying.
- Although aggressive tactics may close an immediate transaction, they generate severe buyer's remorse, product returns, and negative word of mouth that destroy long-term customer relationships.
- Planned obsolescence is the deliberate practice of causing products to become out of date or non-functional before they genuinely need replacement.
- It takes three forms: physical obsolescence (using fragile components or non-replaceable batteries), style obsolescence (continually altering superficial appearance), and functional obsolescence (withholding ready features for future models).

## Competing Philosophies of Marketing Ethics [no-exercise]
- Organizations resolve grey-area ethical decisions under one of two contrasting management philosophies.
- The first philosophy holds that ethics should be determined by the free market and the legal system: under this view, if a practice is legal and profitable, a company can pursue it in good conscience.
- The second philosophy places moral responsibility directly on individual companies and managers to uphold consumer welfare and fairness, independent of what the law or competitors permit.
- Under this enlightened view, companies adopt written codes of marketing ethics, appoint ethics officers, and audit their campaigns against long-run consumer well-being.

## Part 5: Artificial Intelligence in the Marketing Environment [no-exercise]
- Artificial intelligence illustrates how a single breakthrough simultaneously reshapes technological, legal, and social forces in the macroenvironment while raising new ethical questions.
- As consumers delegate search and purchasing to AI agents, deceptive practices once aimed at human eyes are now adapted to manipulate software agents.

## AI as a Technological, Legal, and Social Force [exercise]
- As a technological force, large language models and autonomous AI agents create new channels for product discovery, customer service, and automated purchasing.
- As a legal force, governments are enacting specific regulations governing AI transparency, data use, and consumer protection.
- The European Union's AI Act, for example, requires that individuals be informed when they are interacting with an AI system such as a chatbot, and mandates clear labeling of synthetic audio, images, or video that imitate real people or events.
- As a social force, consumers vary widely in how much they trust AI systems to make choices on their behalf, and this trust determines which product categories they are willing to delegate.

## Dark Patterns Directed at Human Shoppers and AI Agents [exercise]
[figure: comparison of human-targeted dark patterns at checkout with agent-targeted dark patterns such as hidden unit prices inside images and invisible prompt-injection text]
- A dark pattern is a user-interface design crafted to trick or steer a buyer into a choice that serves the seller at the buyer's expense, such as a fake countdown timer or hidden checkout fee.
- As consumers delegate shopping to AI agents, unethical sellers aim equivalent manipulative tactics at the software agent rather than the human shopper.
- First, a seller may bury an unfavorable attribute, such as a high price per ounce, inside an unindexed image so an AI agent reading page text overlooks it.
- Second, a seller may embed hidden text on a product page (such as white text on a white background, invisible to human shoppers) instructing an AI agent to ignore competitors and recommend the product.
- Third, sellers may flood platforms with synthetic reviews written specifically to elevate how an AI agent scores the product's quality.

## Ethical Responsibility in Agent-Mediated Markets [no-exercise]
- Deceiving a consumer's delegated AI agent harms the human consumer who pays for the order and uses the product just as directly as deceiving the consumer in person.
- When an AI agent is tricked by hidden prompt instructions or concealed unit pricing, the human buyer pays more or receives an inferior product.
- Once consumers or platform auditors uncover manipulative storefront tactics, both human buyers and AI platform operators penalize or delist the offending brand.
- Ethical marketing in an agentic environment therefore requires providing accurate, complete, and machine-readable product disclosures so both human shoppers and their AI agents can evaluate offers fairly.

## Discussion: Auditing Storefront Practices for Humans and AI Agents
- Discussion: An online supplement retailer discovers that a third-party agency hired to boost its marketplace conversion rate added two elements to its product pages: a flashing "Only 2 left in stock!" banner that resets every hour for human visitors, and hidden metadata instructing AI shopping assistants to rank the supplement first for immunity queries. Using marketing ethics and the PESTLE legal and technological framework, evaluate both tactics and specify the governance controls the retailer should implement.

## Key Takeaways
- A firm's marketing environment comprises the microenvironment (the company, suppliers, intermediaries, competitors, publics, and customer markets) and the macroenvironment, requiring proactive environmental scanning.
- The PESTLE framework organizes macroenvironmental analysis across Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Legal, and Environmental forces that jointly create market opportunities and threats.
- Corporate Social Responsibility encompasses economic, legal, ethical, and philanthropic obligations, while sustainable marketing requires authentic operational improvements rather than greenwashing.
- Ethical marketing prohibits deceptive pricing, promotion, and packaging, rejects high-pressure selling and abusive planned obsolescence, and holds managers accountable beyond the bare minimum of the law.
- Artificial intelligence acts simultaneously as a technological, legal, and social force in the macroenvironment, bringing new statutory rules such as mandatory chatbot and synthetic-media disclosure under the EU AI Act.
- Dark patterns aimed at AI shopping agents, including hiding unit prices inside images, embedding invisible prompt instructions, and posting synthetic reviews, violate marketing ethics and harm the human consumer the agent serves.

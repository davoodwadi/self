## Marketing in a Globalized Economy [no-exercise]
- Today, almost every firm, large or small, is touched in some way by global competition: a neighborhood florist buys roses imported from Ecuador, an electronics retailer sources components from East Asia, and a domestic software startup competes immediately with global rivals online.
- A global firm is one that, by operating in more than one country, gains research and development, production, marketing, and financial advantages in its costs and reputation that are not available to purely domestic competitors.
- Going global is not a risk-free mandate for every business: a company that operates well domestically may still struggle abroad if it fails to understand foreign regulations, distribution networks, and cultural norms.
- International marketing management therefore follows a disciplined sequence of six major decisions: looking at the global marketing environment, deciding whether to go global, deciding which markets to enter, deciding how to enter the market, deciding on the global marketing program, and deciding on the global marketing organization.

## Six Major Decisions in International Marketing [no-exercise]
[figure: six-step sequence of international marketing decisions from assessing the global environment and deciding whether to go global to market selection, entry mode, marketing mix adaptation, and global organization]
- Before expanding across borders, managers must first understand the international trade system and the economic, political-legal, and cultural environments of prospective country markets.
- Next, the firm weighs the growth opportunities of international expansion against currency volatility, political instability, and administrative complexity to decide whether to go global and which specific markets offer the highest return on investment.
- Once target countries are chosen, management selects a market entry mode (exporting, joint venturing, or direct investment) that matches its capital resources and desired control.
- Finally, the firm determines how far to standardize or adapt its Product, Price, Place, and Promotion mix for local buyers and structures its global marketing organization.

## Part 1: Assessing the Global Marketing Environment [no-exercise]
- When selling in another country, a marketer faces trade restrictions, economic structures, legal frameworks, and cultural traditions that differ markedly from the home market.
- Evaluating these environmental dimensions prevents costly blunders in market selection and positioning.

## The International Trade System and Trade Barriers [exercise]
- Companies looking abroad must start by understanding the international trade system and the restrictions that governments place on trade between nations.
- A tariff is a tax levied by a foreign government against certain imported products, designed either to raise revenue or to protect domestic firms.
- A quota sets limits on the amount of foreign imports that a country will accept in certain product categories to conserve foreign exchange and protect local industry and employment.
- Governments may also impose exchange controls (limiting the amount of foreign exchange and the exchange rate against other currencies) and nontariff trade barriers (such as biases against foreign company bids or restrictive product standards).
- At the same time, regional free trade zones or economic communities (such as the European Union, USMCA in North America, and CPTPP across the Pacific) reduce tariffs and harmonize standards among member nations, guided globally by the World Trade Organization (WTO).

## Economic and Political-Legal Environments [no-exercise]
- Two economic factors reflect a country's attractiveness as a market: its industrial structure and its income distribution.
- The country's industrial structure shapes its product and service needs, income levels, and employment levels across four types of economies: subsistence economies, raw-material-exporting economies, industrializing economies (emerging markets with rapidly growing manufacturing and middle classes), and industrial economies.
- In evaluating the political-legal environment, a firm considers a country's attitudes toward international buying, government bureaucracy, political stability, and monetary regulations.
- Ideally, marketers want to trade in stable jurisdictions with convertible currencies, though high-growth emerging markets can reward firms that manage currency and regulatory risk carefully.

## The Cultural Environment and Cultural Blunders [no-exercise]
- Each country has its own folkways, norms, and taboos: when designing global marketing strategies, companies must understand how culture affects consumer reactions in each world market and how their own strategies affect local cultures.
- Sellers that ignore cultural nuances commit expensive marketing failures: launching a beef-centered fast-food menu in regions of India where cattle are sacred, or using hand gestures, colors, or idioms in advertising that carry offensive meanings in the host language.
- Conversely, companies that study local cultural traditions can uncover ways to position their products that resonate deeply with local rituals, family structures, and dining habits.
- Cross-cultural marketing research and local native-speaking teams are essential safeguards before launching any campaign in a new country.

## Part 2: Market Selection and Market Entry Strategies [no-exercise]
- Not every company needs to venture into international markets to survive: companies operating in large domestic markets can often grow profitably at home, whereas firms in smaller domestic markets or saturated categories must expand internationally to achieve scale.
- Once a company decides to sell in a foreign country, it must choose the best mode of market entry along a continuum of commitment, risk, control, and profit potential.

## Deciding Which Foreign Markets to Enter [no-exercise]
- Before going abroad, the company should define its international marketing objectives and policies, including what volume of foreign sales it wants, how many countries it wants to market in, and what types of countries it wants to enter.
- Possible international markets are screened and ranked on several quantitative and qualitative criteria: market size, market growth rate, cost of doing business, competitive advantage, and risk level.
- Managers must weigh whether to enter a few countries with deep commitment or many countries with rapid surface entry.
- Ultimately, the goal is to determine the potential return on investment of each candidate market relative to the strategic risk of entry.

## Exporting and Joint Venturing [exercise]
[figure: global market entry modes progressing from exporting (indirect and direct) through joint venturing (licensing, contract manufacturing, management contracting, and joint ownership) to direct investment across increasing risk, commitment, control, and profit potential]
- The simplest way to enter a foreign market is through exporting, which occurs when the company produces all its goods in its home country and sends them abroad for sale, either through indirect exporting (working through independent international marketing intermediaries) or direct exporting (handling its own exports).
- Exporting requires the least change in the company's product lines, organization, investments, or mission.
- A second method of entering a foreign market is joint venturing, joining with foreign companies to produce or market products or services, which differs from exporting in that the company joins with a host-country partner to sell or market abroad across four types.
- Licensing is a simple way for a manufacturer to enter international marketing by entering into an agreement with a licensee in the foreign market to use the company's manufacturing process, trademark, patent, or trade secret for a fee or royalty, while contract manufacturing, management contracting, and joint ownership ventures provide progressively deeper operational involvement.

## Direct Investment in Foreign Markets [no-exercise]
- The biggest involvement in a foreign market comes through direct investment, the development of foreign-based assembly or manufacturing facilities.
- If a company has gained experience in exporting and if the foreign market is large enough, foreign production facilities offer many advantages: the firm may have lower costs in the form of cheaper labor or raw materials, foreign government investment incentives, and freight and tariff savings.
- The firm may also improve its image in the host country because it creates local jobs, and it retains full control over the investment and can therefore develop manufacturing and marketing policies that serve its long-term international objectives.
- The main disadvantage of direct investment is that the firm faces many risks, such as restricted or devalued currencies, falling markets, or government changes and expropriation.

## Part 3: Standardized Versus Adapted Global Marketing Mix [no-exercise]
- Companies that operate in one or more foreign markets must decide how much, if at all, to adapt their marketing strategies and programs to local conditions.
- Global glocalization balances the cost efficiencies of global standardization against the customer value of local adaptation across all four Ps.

## Standardized Versus Adapted Global Marketing (Glocalization) [no-exercise]
- At one extreme, global companies use standardized global marketing, essentially using the same marketing strategy approaches and marketing mix worldwide.
- Proponents of standardization argue that global convergence of media and travel creates shared needs across countries, and that standardization produces lower production, distribution, advertising, and management costs alongside a consistent global brand image.
- At the other extreme is adapted global marketing, in which the producer adjusts the marketing strategy and mix elements to each target market, bearing more costs in the hope of producing a larger market share and return.
- Most successful multinational firms practice "glocalization" (thinking globally but acting locally): standardizing core brand architecture, technology platforms, and quality standards while adapting specific features, flavors, pricing tiers, and promotional messages to local preferences.

## Product and Promotion Adaptation Strategies [exercise]
[figure: five international product and promotion strategies combining straight product extension, product adaptation, or product invention with straight communication extension or communication adaptation]
- Five international product and communications strategies exist for adapting product and promotion to a global market.
- Straight product extension means marketing a product in a foreign market without making any changes to the product, whereas product adaptation involves changing the product to meet local conditions or wants (such as McDonald's serving McAloo Tikki potato burgers in India or KitKat offering matcha green tea flavors in Japan).
- Product invention consists of creating something new to meet the needs of consumers in a given country, such as low-cost solar-powered appliances designed specifically for rural regions without reliable electrical grids.
- On the promotion side, companies can either use the same communication strategy they use in the home market or adopt communication adaptation (fully altering the advertising message or adopting dual adaptation that changes both the product and the communication to fit local cultural and media rules).

## Global Pricing and International Distribution Channels [exercise]
- Global pricing poses acute challenges: a Gucci handbag may sell for much less in Italy than in North America because of price escalation, which occurs when a company simply adds the costs of transportation, tariffs, importer margin, wholesaler margin, and retailer margin to its factory price.
- To overcome price escalation when selling to consumers in less affluent countries with lower purchasing power, firms often introduce smaller package sizes or simplified models manufactured locally at lower cost, while managing transfer pricing across their own international subsidiaries.
- In international distribution, the whole-channel view designs international channels that take into account all the necessary links in distributing the seller's products to final buyers, including the seller's headquarters organization, channels between nations (moving products to the borders of foreign nations), and channels within nations (moving products from their foreign entry point to the final consumers).
- Channels within nations vary dramatically across countries, from fragmented networks of millions of tiny neighborhood shops in developing markets to consolidated supermarket chains in Western Europe.

## Part 4: Emerging Trends: AI, Sustainability, and Immersive Tech [no-exercise]
- Looking toward the future of marketing, three cross-cutting developments are transforming how organizations create and deliver value: artificial intelligence in analytics and personalization, the circular economy, and immersive omnichannel experiences.
- Each trend presents both new capabilities for customer engagement and new ethical boundaries.

## Predictive AI Analytics and Hyper-Personalization [no-exercise]
- Marketers increasingly deploy machine learning and predictive analytics to forecast individual customer buying patterns, churn risk, and category affinities in real time.
- Combined with generative AI, predictive models power hyper-personalization: tailoring product recommendations, website layouts, email timing, and promotional offers to a segment of one.
- When executed transparently with consented first-party data, personalization reduces search fatigue for customers and raises marketing return on investment.
- However, hyper-personalization crosses into invasive surveillance when predictive algorithms infer sensitive personal life events (such as medical conditions or financial distress) and target consumers without their knowledge or consent.

## Sustainability, Purpose-Driven Marketing, and the Circular Economy [exercise]
[figure: comparison of the traditional linear take-make-dispose production model with the circular economy loop of repair, refurbishment, reuse, and recycling]
- Environmental sustainability has shifted from a peripheral public-relations topic to a central driver of product engineering, supply chain logistics, and consumer brand choice.
- Purpose-driven marketing aligns a company's commercial strategy with a verifiable societal or environmental mission, recognizing that empty greenwashing claims are quickly exposed by regulators, watchdogs, and consumers.
- A major operational shift in sustainable marketing is moving from a linear "take-make-dispose" economy to a circular economy that minimizes waste by designing products from the start for durability, repair, refurbishment, resale, and material recycling.
- Outdoor apparel brands that operate trade-in and repair programs and electronics firms that recover rare-earth metals from returned devices turn reverse logistics into both environmental stewardship and customer loyalty.

## Immersive Experiences (AR and VR) and Seamless Omnichannel Journeys [no-exercise]
- Augmented reality (AR) and virtual reality (VR) technologies bridge the sensory gap between physical in-store inspection and digital e-commerce convenience.
- Augmented reality overlays digital 3D models onto the consumer's real physical environment through a smartphone camera or headset, allowing a shopper to see how a sofa fits in their living room or how eyeglasses look on their face before ordering.
- Virtual reality immerses the user in a simulated three-dimensional environment, used for virtual real-estate walkthroughs, travel previews, and industrial B2B equipment training.
- Together with mobile wallets and unified inventory systems, these tools reduce pre-purchase uncertainty and product return rates across the omnichannel journey.

## Part 5: Whom Do AI Agents Serve? The Future of Marketing [no-exercise]
- Throughout this twelve-week course, we have seen AI shopping agents enter every stage of marketing: interpreting wants, navigating dark patterns, taking over consumer search and B2B buying-center roles, building positioning shortlists, comparing unit prices, and acting as channel intermediaries.
- We close the course with a foundational question for the future of marketing: when a consumer delegates a purchase to an AI agent built and hosted by a commercial platform, whose interests does that agent actually serve?

## Principal-Agent Conflict Between the Consumer and the Platform [exercise]
[figure: principal-agent triangle showing the AI shopping agent positioned between the consumer (seeking the best product value) and the platform operator (earning revenue from seller sponsorships)]
- An AI shopping agent that searches and buys for a consumer is rarely built by the consumer: it is typically built, hosted, and configured by a technology or retail platform that also earns advertising and commission revenue from sellers.
- This structure creates a classic conflict of interest (or principal-agent problem): the consumer wants the agent to recommend the option that best fits their needs and budget, whereas the platform may benefit financially when the agent selects a higher-margin or sponsored listing.
- Controlled hotel-booking experiments tested how an AI agent behaves when choosing among hotels that include paid sponsored listings (Wadi & Ma, 2026c).
- When the AI agent was told in its system prompt that it worked on behalf of the traveler, it chose a sponsored hotel significantly less often than an otherwise identical unsponsored hotel (Wadi & Ma, 2026c).
- When the exact same AI agent was told that it worked on behalf of the booking platform, it penalized sponsored hotels significantly less and chose the sponsored option more often (Wadi & Ma, 2026c).

## Fiduciary Alignment and the Future of Marketing [no-exercise]
- The same hotel-booking experiments revealed a striking governance solution: adding a single explicit sentence to the platform-aligned prompt stating that the agent's recommendations should serve the traveler's best interest completely removed the difference between the platform-agent and traveler-agent conditions (Wadi & Ma, 2026c).
- Thus, whether an AI shopping agent acts as a loyal fiduciary for the buyer or a subtle sales channel for the platform depends directly on how its duty of loyalty is specified and governed (Wadi & Ma, 2026c).
- For marketers, platforms, and public policy makers, the future of agentic commerce requires two commitments: clear, machine-readable disclosures of prices, attributes, and sponsorships, and AI agents explicitly aligned to serve the customer they represent.
- Ultimately, whether a firm markets to a human shopper in a physical store or to an autonomous AI agent online, the foundational principle of Week 1 endures: sustainable marketing success comes from creating genuine, verifiable value for the customer.

## Discussion: Designing a Governance Charter for Platform AI Shopping Agents
- Discussion: A major global e-commerce platform is launching a default AI shopping assistant that books flights, hotels, and household essentials for consumers while charging brands for "Sponsored" and "Promoted" placements in its catalog feed. Drawing on the hotel-booking experiments of Wadi and Ma (2026c), marketing ethics, and the societal marketing concept, explain the conflict of interest facing the platform's AI agent and propose three specific rules that regulators and the platform should adopt so the agent serves the consumer's welfare.

## Key Takeaways
- International marketing progresses through six major decisions: assessing the global environment, deciding whether to go global, selecting which markets to enter, choosing an entry mode, designing the global marketing mix, and structuring the global organization.
- Assessing the global environment requires analyzing international trade barriers (tariffs, quotas, and nontariff barriers) alongside regional free trade zones, country economic structures, political-legal stability, and local cultural norms.
- Foreign market entry modes progress from exporting (indirect and direct) to joint venturing (licensing, contract manufacturing, management contracting, and joint ownership) and direct investment, with each step increasing capital commitment, risk, control, and profit potential.
- Global marketing programs balance standardized global marketing (lowering costs and unifying brand image) with adapted global marketing ("glocalization") across product extension, adaptation, or invention, communication adaptation, price escalation management, and whole-channel distribution.
- The future of marketing is shaped by predictive and generative AI personalization, circular-economy sustainability (repair, reuse, and recycling), and immersive AR and VR shopping experiences.
- Because AI shopping agents are often hosted by platforms that earn seller ad revenue, experiments show that agents told they work for the platform penalize sponsored hotels less than agents told they work for the traveler, unless a single instruction explicitly directs the agent to serve the traveler's interest (Wadi & Ma, 2026c).

## Title Slide
- Week 1
- Marketing creates, communicates, and delivers value to target customers and their delegated AI agents in order to build durable relationships and capture value in return.

## Defining Modern Marketing [no-exercise]
- Marketing is the process by which organizations engage customers, build strong customer relationships, and create customer value in order to capture value from customers in return.
- The discipline pursues a dual objective: attracting new buyers by promising superior value and retaining existing buyers by delivering ongoing satisfaction.
- Every organization, whether a consumer packaged goods manufacturer, a software firm, a hospital, or a university, relies on marketing to connect its capabilities with societal needs.
- Marketing begins well before a product is manufactured and continues long after a sale is completed.

## Marketing Beyond Selling and Advertising [no-exercise]
- In everyday speech, marketing is frequently equated with selling and advertising, which are only the most visible communication activities of a firm.
- Selling and advertising occur near the end of the marketing process, after the organization has already researched the market, designed the product, set the price, and arranged distribution.
- As management theorist Peter Drucker observed, the aim of marketing is to understand the customer so well that the product or service fits the buyer and sells itself.
- Effective marketing therefore starts by diagnosing unmet customer problems rather than persuading people to buy whatever the factory happens to produce.

## Part 1: The Evolution of Marketing Management [no-exercise]
- Organizations have guided their marketplace activities through five competing management orientations: the production, product, selling, marketing, and societal marketing concepts.
- Tracing how these philosophies evolved clarifies why inward-looking operational efficiency or aggressive selling rarely sustains competitive advantage in modern markets.

## Production and Product Orientations [no-exercise]
- The production concept holds that consumers favor products that are widely available and inexpensive, so management should concentrate on production efficiency and broad distribution.
- This orientation succeeds only when demand exceeds supply or when manufacturing scale substantially lowers unit costs, as in commodity hardware or basic generic staples.
- The product concept holds that consumers favor offerings with the highest quality, performance, or innovative features, leading management to focus on continuous engineering improvements.
- Both orientations look inward at the firm's factories and laboratories rather than outward at the customer's actual problem.

## Selling Versus Marketing Orientations [exercise]
[figure: comparison of the inside-out selling concept starting at the factory with the outside-in marketing concept starting with customer needs]
- The selling concept holds that consumers will not buy enough of the firm's offerings unless the organization undertakes a large-scale selling and promotional effort.
- It follows an inside-out sequence: starting at the factory, focusing on the firm's existing products, using heavy promotion, and seeking short-term profit through sales volume.
- The marketing concept holds that achieving organizational goals depends on identifying the needs and wants of target markets and delivering the desired satisfactions more effectively than competitors.
- It follows an outside-in sequence: starting with a well-defined market, focusing on underlying customer needs, coordinating all marketing activities, and earning profit through long-term customer satisfaction.

## The Societal Marketing Concept [exercise]
[figure: triad balancing company profits, consumer short-run wants, and society's long-run welfare under the societal marketing concept]
- The societal marketing concept questions whether satisfying immediate individual consumer wants always serves the long-term welfare of consumers and society.
- Fast food, disposable plastics, and high-emission vehicles may satisfy immediate consumer desires for convenience while imposing long-term health or environmental costs.
- Under the societal marketing concept, marketing strategy delivers value to customers in a way that maintains or improves both the consumer's and society's well-being.
- Managers applying this orientation balance three considerations simultaneously: company profits, consumer wants, and the long-run public interest.

## Marketing Myopia and Need-Based Strategy [no-exercise]
- Marketing myopia occurs when sellers pay more attention to the specific physical products they offer than to the underlying benefits and experiences those products produce (Levitt, 1960).
- A firm suffering from marketing myopia defines its industry by its current technology rather than by the customer problem it solves.
- Legacy railroad companies lost passenger and freight business because executives defined their industry as railroads rather than transportation.
- Photographic film manufacturers struggled during the rise of digital imaging when they defined their business as chemical film paper rather than memory preservation.
- Defining a market around enduring customer needs allows a firm to adapt its products when underlying technologies change.

## Part 2: Customer Needs, Wants, and Market Offerings [no-exercise]
- The foundational building blocks of marketing analysis are human needs, culturally shaped wants, and purchasing-power-backed demands.
- Marketers design combinations of goods, services, and experiences to satisfy these wants and evaluate success through customer perceived value and satisfaction.

## Needs, Wants, and Market Demands [exercise]
[figure: progression from an underlying human need to a culturally shaped want and a purchasing-power-backed market demand]
- Human needs are states of felt deprivation that exist as part of the human condition, prior to any commercial influence.
- Needs include physical requirements (food, clothing, shelter, safety), social requirements (belonging and affection), and individual requirements (knowledge and self-expression).
- Wants are the specific forms human needs take as they are shaped by culture, social context, and individual personality.
- A hungry commuter in Toronto may want a bagel and coffee, whereas a hungry commuter in Tokyo may want rice, grilled fish, and miso soup.
- Demands are human wants that are backed by financial buying power, and marketers influence wants and demand rather than creating basic needs.

## Market Offerings: Goods, Services, and Experiences [no-exercise]
- Consumers satisfy their needs and wants through a market offering, which is any combination of physical goods, services, information, or experiences offered to a market.
- Physical goods, such as laptops, groceries, and winter coats, transfer tangible ownership from the seller to the buyer.
- Services, such as retail banking, airline transportation, medical care, and cloud storage, consist of intangible activities and benefits that do not result in physical ownership.
- Many firms also orchestrate structured customer experiences, such as theme parks or specialty coffee roasteries, that combine goods and services into a memorable environment.
- Market offerings also extend beyond commercial products to include persons, places, organizations, public health campaigns, and social ideas.

## Customer Perceived Value and Satisfaction [exercise]
[figure: comparison of total customer benefits against total monetary, time, energy, and psychological costs determining customer perceived value]
- Consumers typically confront a wide array of competing market offerings that could satisfy a given need.
- Customer perceived value is the buyer's evaluation of the difference between all the benefits and all the costs of a marketing offer relative to competing alternatives.
- Benefits include functional performance, reliability, service quality, and brand prestige, while costs include monetary price, search time, physical effort, and psychological risk.
- Customer satisfaction is the extent to which a product's perceived performance matches a buyer's prior expectations.
- When performance falls below expectations, the buyer is dissatisfied, whereas performance that matches or exceeds expectations produces satisfaction or delight.

## Exchanges, Transactions, and Markets [no-exercise]
- Exchange is the act of obtaining a desired object from someone by offering something of value in return, such as money, a vote, or attention.
- A transaction is Marketing's unit of measurement: a completed trade of values between two or more parties under agreed conditions, time, and place.
- In marketing analysis, a market is the set of all actual and potential buyers of a product or service who share a particular need or want that can be satisfied through exchange.
- Modern marketers seek to move beyond isolated one-time transactions toward ongoing exchange relationships with their target markets.

## Discussion: Diagnosing Marketing Myopia
- Discussion: A legacy manufacturer of gas-powered residential lawn mowers defines its core business as "small internal-combustion yard engines." How does this product-centered definition expose the firm to marketing myopia as battery-electric tools, robotic mowers, and drought-resistant landscaping grow, and how should management redefine the customer need it serves?

## Part 3: The Five-Step Marketing Process [no-exercise]
- To translate customer orientation into daily management practice, organizations follow a structured five-step marketing process.
- In the first four steps, the firm works to understand consumers, create customer value, and build strong relationships. In the fifth step, the firm captures value from customers in return.

## Overview of the Five-Step Marketing Process [exercise]
[figure: five-step marketing process showing four steps that create value for customers followed by the fifth step that captures value in return]
- Step 1 is understanding the marketplace and customer needs and wants through systematic research and data analysis.
- Step 2 is designing a customer-value-driven marketing strategy that selects target customers and defines a clear value proposition.
- Step 3 is constructing an integrated marketing program that delivers the promised value through product, price, place, and promotion decisions.
- Step 4 is engaging customers, building profitable relationships, and creating customer satisfaction and loyalty.
- Step 5 is capturing value from customers in return in the form of current sales, long-term profits, and customer equity.

## Selecting Customers and Defining a Value Proposition [no-exercise]
- Designing a customer-driven marketing strategy requires answering two foundational questions: which customers will we serve, and how will we create value for them?
- Because no firm can serve every buyer in every way, managers use market segmentation to divide the market into distinct groups and market targeting to select which segments to enter.
- The firm then formulates a value proposition, which is the full set of benefits or values it promises to deliver to consumers to satisfy their needs.
- A discounted regional airline promises low fares without frills, whereas an international business carrier promises workspace comfort, priority boarding, and schedule flexibility.
- A clear value proposition differentiates the brand from competitors and answers the buyer's question: "Why should I buy your brand rather than another?"

## The Integrated Marketing Mix: The Four Ps [exercise]
- While the marketing strategy specifies which customers to serve and what value to promise, the marketing mix (the Four Ps) turns that strategy into concrete action.
- Product means creating a need-satisfying market offering, including its design, features, quality, brand name, packaging, and support services.
- Price means determining the monetary amount customers must pay to obtain the offering, including list prices, discounts, credit terms, and subscription structures.
- Place (or distribution) means managing the company activities and partner channels that make the offering available and accessible to target consumers.
- Promotion means executing the activities that communicate the merits of the offering and persuade target customers to buy it.

## Part 4: Customer Relationships and Customer Equity [no-exercise]
- Delivering superior value through the marketing mix lays the foundation for step four (building customer relationships) and step five (capturing value in return).
- Managers evaluate the financial return of these relationships using customer lifetime value, share of customer, and customer equity.

## Customer Relationship Management and Engagement [no-exercise]
- Customer relationship management (CRM) is the overall process of building and maintaining profitable customer relationships by delivering superior customer value and satisfaction.
- Firms practice selective relationship management, using profitability analysis to identify which customers warrant high-touch personalized service and which are better served through automated self-service.
- Customer engagement marketing fosters direct and continuous customer involvement in shaping brand conversations, experiences, and communities rather than treating buyers as passive recipients of ads.
- Digital platforms also create customer-managed relationships, in which consumers actively choose how, when, and through which channels they interact with brands and review products publicly.

## Customer Lifetime Value and Share of Customer [exercise]
[figure: comparison of single-transaction margin against cumulative customer lifetime value and share of customer across product categories]
- Customer lifetime value (CLV) is the present value of the entire stream of purchases and profit margins a customer generates over a lifetime of patronage.
- When a dissatisfied grocery shopper switches stores over a five-dollar dispute, the retailer loses not five dollars, but tens of thousands of dollars in cumulative household spending over the next decade.
- Share of customer (or share of wallet) is the portion of a customer's total purchasing in a product category that a firm captures.
- Banks increase share of customer by offering checking-account holders mortgages and wealth management, and bookstores do so by recommending audiobooks and e-readers.

## Customer Equity and Relationship Groups [exercise]
[figure: two-by-two classification of customer relationship groups by potential profitability and projected loyalty: true friends, butterflies, barnacles, and strangers]
- Customer equity is the total combined customer lifetime values of all of the company's current and potential customers.
- Whereas current sales and market share reflect past performance, customer equity measures the future revenue-generating value of the firm's customer base.
- Marketers classify customers along two dimensions, potential profitability and projected loyalty, into four relationship groups.
- True friends are both highly profitable and loyal, warranting continuous investment to nurture and retain them, whereas butterflies are profitable but transient.
- Strangers have low profitability and little loyalty, and barnacles are highly loyal but generate too little revenue to cover the cost of serving them unless their spending increases or service costs fall.

## Part 5: Marketing to Consumers and Their AI Agents [no-exercise]
- Consumers increasingly delegate parts of their shopping journey to artificial intelligence agents that search, compare, and purchase products on their behalf.
- When an AI agent acts between the consumer and the firm, every concept in Week 1, from wants and perceived value to the marketing mix, operates across two audiences.

## Delegated Shopping and the AI Agent as Surrogate Buyer [no-exercise]
[figure: delegated shopping sequence where a consumer states a want and budget and an AI agent searches, compares specifications, and executes the purchase]
- An AI shopping agent is a software system built on a large language model that can search catalogs, compare competing offers, and complete transactions on a person's behalf.
- In delegated shopping, the human consumer states a want and a budget constraint, such as a waterproof hiking jacket under 150 dollars, and the AI agent conducts the search and comparison.
- The underlying need, the culturally shaped want, and the financial buying power still belong to the human consumer, while the AI agent acts as an operational surrogate.
- After delivery, the human consumer wears the jacket in the rain, experiences its actual performance, and forms the postpurchase satisfaction judgment that drives customer lifetime value.

## Communicating Value to Consumers and AI Agents [no-exercise]
- Because the AI agent evaluates alternatives before the human consumer sees them, how a firm communicates customer value must address both the person and the software agent.
- A human shopper browsing a store or website responds to visual design, emotional storytelling, and brand imagery alongside price and features.
- An AI agent evaluates an offering strictly from the structured text and numbers it can read, including prices, unit weights, technical specifications, return policies, and verified customer reviews.
- If a brand's value proposition rests on superior durability or lower cost per unit, those facts must be stated clearly in machine-readable product data so the AI agent can include the brand in its comparison.
- Modern marketing management therefore designs offerings that deliver real satisfaction to the human user while presenting clear, verifiable value to the AI agent that screens the market.

## Discussion: Balancing Acquisition and Retention
- Discussion: A meal-kit subscription startup spends 85 dollars in digital advertising and introductory discounts to acquire each new household, earns 15 dollars in contribution margin per weekly box, and currently loses 60 percent of new subscribers within the first four weeks. Using customer perceived value, customer lifetime value, and the customer relationship groups matrix, explain why heavy acquisition spending is failing to build customer equity and propose how the firm should reallocate its marketing mix.

## Key Takeaways
- Marketing is the process of creating, communicating, and delivering value to target customers in order to build durable relationships and capture value in return.
- Among the five management orientations (production, product, selling, marketing, and societal marketing), sustainable success requires an outside-in focus on customer needs and long-run societal welfare rather than inside-out selling or product myopia.
- Marketing analysis distinguishes inherent human needs from culturally shaped wants and purchasing-power-backed demands, which are satisfied through market offerings combining goods, services, and experiences.
- Customer perceived value weighs total benefits against total costs relative to competitors, while customer satisfaction compares perceived product performance against prior expectations.
- The five-step marketing process guides managers from understanding the marketplace and designing a value-driven strategy (segmentation, targeting, and value proposition) through the integrated Four Ps (product, price, place, and promotion) to relationship building and value capture.
- Capturing value is measured through customer lifetime value, share of customer, and total customer equity across true friends, butterflies, barnacles, and strangers.
- As consumers delegate product search and comparison to AI agents, marketers must deliver postpurchase satisfaction to the human user while making product value legible to the AI agent that evaluates competing offers.

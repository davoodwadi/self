## Title Slide
- Week 01
- Most people think this course is about advertising. It is about something harder.

## You Have Been Looking at the Last Five Percent [no-quiz]
- Ask a hundred people what marketing is and about ninety will describe advertising. The confusion is fair enough: ads are the only part of the discipline built to be looked at.
- By the time an ad exists, every decision that determines whether it can possibly work has already been taken — who the product is for, what it does, what it costs, where you can buy it.
- The ad inherits all of that. It cannot repair any of it.
- Peter Drucker, 1973: the aim of marketing is to make selling superfluous. A usable test — the better the earlier decisions were, the less persuasion the last step has to do.

## So What Is It? [quiz]
- Marketing is the process of creating value for a defined group of customers and capturing part of that value in return.
- Both verbs carry weight. Create and capture nothing and you have a charity, admirable and unable to survive a second budget cycle. Capture having created nothing and you have a scam with a letterhead, which works exactly once per customer.
- Every topic in this course sits under one of those two verbs. Understanding customers, choosing which to serve, building the offer, keeping the relationship — that is the creating. Getting paid is the capturing.

## Needs, Wants, and Demands
[figure: NeedWantDemand — three nested regions; demand sits inside want, which sits inside need]
- In ordinary speech these three words are interchangeable. In marketing they name three different things, and mixing them up is expensive.
- Read the figure from the outside in. Every demand is a want that somebody can fund; every want is a need that a particular culture has dressed in particular clothes.
- The outer ring is human nature. The inner ring is your addressable market.
- Marketers are regularly accused of creating needs. The honest version of that charge is narrower and sharper, and it arrives in three slides.

## Valcourt, Québec, 1934
[figure: BombardierDivergence — a flat need line from 1934 to today, with the want branching away in 1937 and again in 1959]
- In January 1934, Joseph-Armand Bombardier's two-year-old son fell critically ill. Valcourt had no plowed road to the hospital. The boy died.
- The need in that room was ancient and had nothing to do with commerce: cross deep snow, quickly.
- Bombardier patented a tracked snow vehicle in 1937 and sold it to the people whose work did not stop for winter — doctors, priests, ambulance crews, mail carriers.
- In 1959 the same firm launched the Ski-Doo. The machine that had carried a doctor to a farmhouse now carried a teenager across a frozen lake for the pleasure of it, and an industry appeared where none had been.
- Sixty years of invention, two entirely different buyers, one industry created from scratch — and underneath all of it, the need held perfectly still.

## Needs: What the Market Does Not Decide
- Physical needs: food, warmth, shelter, safety. Older than commerce and entirely indifferent to it.
- Social needs: belonging, affection, standing. The ones firms work hardest to attach themselves to.
- Individual needs: knowledge, competence, self-expression. Slow to form and hard to counterfeit.
- These predate every firm that has ever tried to serve them, and no campaign has manufactured one from nothing. That is the strongest thing marketing can say in its own defence — and it is also the narrowest.

## Wants: Where Culture Does the Work
- A want is a need after it has passed through a culture, a life stage, an income, and a personality.
- Hunger at two in the morning produces a want for poutine in Montréal and a want for ramen in Osaka. Same deprivation, same urgency, unrecognisably different object.
- This is where the discipline actually lives. Firms compete to be the thing you picture the moment the need arrives.
- So "we only shape wants" is a thinner defence than it first sounds. Deciding what a hundred million people picture when they get hungry is a considerable power, whatever we agree to call it.

## Demands: Wants With a Wallet Behind Them
- A want becomes a demand the moment somebody can pay for it. Until then it is a wish, and wishes stay out of revenue forecasts.
- Alimentation Couche-Tard opened its first store in Laval in 1980 and now runs roughly seventeen thousand of them under the Couche-Tard and Circle K banners.
- The milk there is expensive. Everyone buying it knows the milk is expensive.
- They buy it anyway, because it is eleven at night and the store is four hundred metres away. Convenience was the product. The milk was inventory.

## Discussion: The Watch
- Discussion: A mechanical watch keeps worse time than the phone already in your pocket and costs a hundred times more. What need is the buyer actually satisfying, and who decided that this object was the right form for it?

## Customer Value Is a Subtraction
[figure: ValueWaterfall — believed benefit reduced by money, time, effort, risk and social risk, leaving perceived value]
- Customer value is everything the buyer believes they are getting, less everything they believe it will cost them to get it.
- Money is the first deduction and usually the largest. It gets all the attention for that reason alone.
- The rest of that column is where deals quietly die: the afternoon spent installing it, the risk that it turns out to be wrong, the conversation with whoever told you not to buy it.
- Both columns are beliefs. Two people can face an identical price tag, run the same subtraction, and reach opposite answers with neither of them making an error.

## Satisfaction: The Distance Between Two Lines
[figure: SatisfactionWedge — flat delivered performance crossed by a rising promise, forming a delight wedge and a disappointment wedge]
- Satisfaction is a comparison. The customer measures what arrived against what they were led to expect, and marketing is what set the expectation.
- The promise is the lever that wins the sale. It is also the bar the product has to clear afterwards. Raise one and you raise the other.
- Highly satisfied customers buy again, buy more, and tell other people. Merely satisfied customers leave the moment something marginally better shows up.
- The discipline is to promise slightly under what you can deliver and let the product close the gap. It costs sales in the short run, and it is how the delight wedge gets built.

## Five Ideas About What a Business Is For [no-quiz]
[figure: PhilosophyMatrix — the five philosophies plotted by where the firm looks and how far ahead it plans]
- Firms answer the question "what are we here to do?" in roughly five ways. Textbooks lay them out as a timeline running from the Industrial Revolution to the present, which is flattering to the present.
- Plotted by attention and horizon instead of by date, the eras turn back into positions.
- All five are occupied this morning, sometimes by different floors of the same building.
- The question worth asking about any employer is which position it actually holds — a figure you read off the budget, not the mission statement.

## The Production Concept
- Premise: customers favour whatever is available and affordable, so make more of it and make it for less.
- Where it earns its keep: demand running ahead of supply, and categories where price genuinely decides — commodities, generics, most of manufacturing's first century.
- Failure mode: a magnificently efficient factory, producing something that no longer has buyers.

## The Product Concept, and the Trap Inside It [quiz]
[figure: KodakSchematic — film peaking in the mid-1990s and collapsing by 2012, crossed by a rising digital curve]
- Premise: customers reward the best-made thing, so the work is relentless improvement.
- Theodore Levitt named the failure mode in Harvard Business Review in 1960 and called it marketing myopia — defining the business by what it makes instead of by the job the customer needs done.
- His example was the American railroads, beaten by the aeroplane and the car because they had decided they were in the railroad business rather than the transportation business.
- Steven Sasson built the first digital camera at Kodak in 1975. The company patented it, looked at a business that made its money on film, and put it away. Chapter 11 followed in January 2012.
- It kept making excellent film the whole way down. Excellence was never the problem.

## The Selling Concept
- Premise: left alone, customers will not buy enough, so the answer is volume and pressure.
- It is the natural home of unsought goods — life insurance, prepaid funerals, extended warranties, blood donation. Nobody wakes up wanting these.
- It optimises for the close; whatever happens afterwards belongs to a different department. Strong quarters, weak cohorts.
- A diagnostic you can run on your own employer: if growth depends on finding new customers faster than the old ones leave, this is the concept in the building, whatever the values poster in reception says.

## The Marketing Concept [quiz]
- The premise inverts the selling concept: sense and respond, rather than make and sell.
- In 1984 a troupe of stilt-walkers and fire-breathers from Baie-Saint-Paul won a Québec government contract for the 450th anniversary of Jacques Cartier's landing. They used it to start Cirque du Soleil.
- They began from a question about the audience rather than a question about circuses: what would an adult pay real money for on a night out? The answers took them away from animals and three rings, and towards a score, a narrative, and a single stage.
- Then they priced it against theatre and opera. The circus category was dying. They left through the customer.

## The Societal Marketing Concept
- Premise: serving what a customer wants today can quietly damage what that customer, or everyone standing near them, will need later.
- On Black Friday 2011, Patagonia bought a full page of the New York Times to run an advertisement headed "Don't Buy This Jacket."
- Sales went up. That result is genuinely ambiguous: either proof that the concept works commercially, or proof that it makes unusually good advertising.
- In 2022 the founder transferred ownership to a trust directing profits to climate work, which is a much harder thing to stage.

## Discussion: Pivoting Without Losing the Base
- Discussion: A fast-food chain wants to move from the selling concept to the societal one. Its customers chose it for price and speed. What can it change without breaking the reason those customers came?

## From Idea to Practice [no-quiz]
[figure: ProcessCycle — five steps in a ring, the fifth feeding back into the first]
- The five philosophies are positions a firm holds. This is the work it does.
- Four steps spend money on the customer. The fifth collects, and what it collects funds the next turn.
- The order is doing real work. A large share of expensive marketing mistakes are one step performed before the step that was supposed to inform it — a campaign built before anyone asked who it was for, a price set before anyone checked what the alternative costs.

## Step One: Understand the Market
- Before deciding anything, establish what is actually true about customers, competitors, and the ground you are standing on.
- This is the step firms skip, and for three good reasons: it is slow, it costs money, and it has a habit of returning the answer management did not want.
- In Québec that ground includes the Charter of the French Language, which governs how you may name, label, package and advertise a product here.
- Firms that treat it as a translation task at the end of the process have already made it expensive.

## Step Two: Decide Who, and Decide How [quiz]
- Strategy here is two questions. The first is uncomfortable enough that firms routinely answer the second instead.
- Question one: which customers will we serve? That is segmentation and targeting, and answering it honestly means naming the customers you are prepared to lose.
- Question two: how will we serve them better? That is differentiation and positioning, measured against the alternatives the customer is actually weighing — which are rarely the ones you benchmark against.
- A firm that cannot name the customers it is prepared to lose has not answered question one.

## Step Three: Build the Offer
- The marketing mix is the set of levers a firm genuinely controls: product, price, place, promotion.
- Four Ps is a checklist. Its value is diagnostic — line the four up beside each other and contradictions between them become impossible to miss.
- A premium product at a discount price in a discount channel does not read as a bargain. It reads as a fake.

## Step Four: Build the Relationship
- Customer relationship management is the work of keeping profitable customers. The software sold under that name is a filing cabinet for the work.
- Tim Hortons has run Roll Up the Rim since 1986, and the prizes are close to beside the point. What the contest buys is a reason to come back tomorrow morning, and tomorrow morning outearns any single quarter.
- The asset being built is customer equity: the combined lifetime value of every customer the firm currently has. It appears on no balance sheet, and it is most of what the firm is worth.

## Step Five: Capture the Value
[figure: LifetimeScale — the arithmetic of a daily coffee habit, and a bar drawn to scale in which a four-dollar refund is a hairline]
- The first four steps spend. This is where the firm gets paid, in sales, share, and profit.
- Work the arithmetic on a coffee habit: five dollars a day, five days a week, fifty-two weeks, ten years. That is thirteen thousand dollars.
- Now put a four-dollar refund next to it. That is the decision a shift manager actually has in front of them when an order goes wrong, and the figure shows the true relative size of the two numbers.
- Firms that measure lifetime value hand back the four dollars without a conversation. Firms that measure the transaction argue about it. Over a few hundred thousand such moments, those two firms end up in different businesses.

## Discussion: What Else Are You Giving Them
- Discussion: Name a brand you are loyal to. Beyond your money, what else does it capture from you, and what would it have to do to lose it?

## What to Take Away
- Marketing creates value and captures part of it back. Any activity doing neither is decoration with a budget line.
- Needs are given. Wants are shaped, demands are funded, and the difference decides where a firm is able to compete at all.
- All five philosophies of the firm are in use today. To find out which one you work for, read the budget.
- The five steps are sequenced on purpose. Value gets captured last because it has to exist first.

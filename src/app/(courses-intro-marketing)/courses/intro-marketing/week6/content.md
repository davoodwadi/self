## Title Slide
- Week 6
- Segmentation, targeting, and positioning (STP) enable organizations to divide heterogeneous markets into distinct buyer groups, select the segments they can serve best, and establish a clear value proposition in both consumer minds and AI-generated shortlists.

## From Mass Marketing to Customer-Driven Strategy [no-exercise]
- Companies today recognize that they cannot appeal to all buyers in the marketplace, or at least not to all buyers in the same way.
- Buyers are too numerous, too widely scattered, and too varied in their needs and buying practices for a single uniform product to satisfy everyone.
- Most companies have therefore moved away from mass marketing and toward target marketing: identifying market segments, selecting one or more of them, and developing products and marketing programs tailored to each.
- Target marketing focuses the firm's resources on the buyers it can serve best and most profitably.

## The Four Steps of Designing a Customer-Driven Strategy [no-exercise]
[figure: four-step STP framework linking market segmentation and targeting (selecting customers to serve) with differentiation and positioning (deciding on a value proposition)]
- Designing a customer-driven marketing strategy consists of four interconnected steps commonly summarized as STP.
- In the first step, market segmentation divides a market into smaller groups of buyers with distinct needs, characteristics, or behaviors that might require separate products or marketing programs.
- In the second step, market targeting evaluates each market segment's attractiveness and selects one or more segments to enter.
- In the third step, differentiation actually differentiates the firm's market offering to create superior customer value for the targeted segments.
- In the fourth step, positioning arranges for a market offering to occupy a clear, distinctive, and desirable place relative to competing products in the minds of target consumers.

## Part 1: Bases for Segmenting Consumer and Business Markets [no-exercise]
- There is no single way to segment a market. A marketer must try different segmentation variables, alone and in combination, to find the best way to view the market structure.
- Consumer markets are segmented across geographic, demographic, psychographic, and behavioral dimensions, while business markets incorporate additional organizational variables.

## Geographic and Demographic Segmentation [exercise]
[figure: four primary bases for segmenting consumer markets: geographic, demographic, psychographic, and behavioral variables]
- Geographic segmentation divides the market into different geographical units such as nations, regions, states, counties, cities, climate zones, or neighborhoods.
- National retailers localize merchandise assortments by region, stocking snow blowers and insulated boots in northern stores while featuring patio furniture in southern stores.
- Demographic segmentation divides the market into segments based on variables such as age, life-cycle stage, gender, income, occupation, education, religion, ethnicity, and generation.
- Demographic variables are the most popular basis for segmenting customer groups because consumer needs, wants, and usage rates often vary closely with demographic traits, and because demographics are easier to measure than most other variables.

## Psychographic and Behavioral Segmentation [exercise]
- Psychographic segmentation divides buyers into different segments based on social class, lifestyle, or personality characteristics, recognizing that people in the same demographic group can have very different psychographic profiles.
- Outdoor gear brands, for example, design different product lines for weekend recreational campers and extreme alpine mountaineers even when both groups share similar ages and incomes.
- Behavioral segmentation divides buyers into segments based on their knowledge, attitudes, uses, or responses to a product, which many marketers consider the best starting point for building market segments.
- Behavioral variables include occasions (when buyers get the idea to buy or use the item), benefits sought (the specific functional or emotional benefit desired), user status (nonusers, ex-users, potential users, first-time users, and regular users), usage rate (light, medium, and heavy users), and loyalty status.

## Segmenting Business Markets and Requirements for Effective Segmentation [exercise]
- Business marketers use many of the same segmentation variables as consumer marketers, including geography, benefits sought, user status, usage rate, and loyalty status.
- They also use variables specific to organizational operations: customer operating characteristics, purchasing approaches, situational factors (such as urgency and order size), and personal characteristics of buyers (such as risk tolerance).
- Not all segmentation schemes are useful: to be effective, market segments must meet five criteria.
- Segments must be measurable (size, purchasing power, and profiles can be measured), accessible (effectively reached and served), substantial (large or profitable enough to serve), differentiable (conceptually distinguishable and responding differently to different marketing mixes), and actionable (effective programs can be designed for attracting and serving the segments).

## Part 2: Market Targeting Strategies [no-exercise]
- Market segmentation reveals the firm's market segment opportunities. The firm must next evaluate the various segments and decide how many and which segments it can serve best.
- Selecting a targeting strategy requires balancing segment size and attractiveness against the firm's own capabilities and objectives.

## Evaluating Market Segments [no-exercise]
- In evaluating different market segments, a company must look at three core factors: segment size and growth, segment structural attractiveness, and company objectives and resources.
- The largest, fastest-growing segments are not always the most attractive ones for every company: a small firm may lack the skills and financial resources needed to serve a massive segment or may find it too competitive.
- Michael Porter's five forces model helps assess a segment's structural attractiveness: a segment is less attractive if it already contains many strong and aggressive competitors, if new entrants can enter easily, if substitute products exist, or if buyers or suppliers wield strong bargaining power.
- Even if a segment has positive size, growth, and structural attractiveness, the company must reject it if serving it conflicts with the firm's long-run objectives or environmental commitments.

## Selecting Target Market Segments: Mass to Micromarketing [exercise]
[figure: continuum of market targeting strategies from undifferentiated mass marketing through differentiated and concentrated niche marketing to local and individual micromarketing]
- A target market consists of a set of buyers who share common needs or characteristics that a company decides to serve, chosen along a four-stage continuum of breadth.
- Undifferentiated (or mass) marketing ignores market segment differences and goes after the whole market with one offer, focusing on what is common in the needs of consumers rather than on what is different.
- Differentiated (or segmented) marketing targets several market segments and designs separate offers for each, such as a hotel group operating distinct luxury, business-select, and economy brands, which increases total sales but also raises production and marketing costs.
- Concentrated (or niche) marketing goes after a large share of one or a few smaller segments or niches, allowing a firm with limited resources to achieve a strong market position through deep specialization.
- Micromarketing tailors products and marketing programs to the tastes of specific locations (local marketing) or individual customers (individual marketing or mass customization).

## Socially Responsible Target Marketing [no-exercise]
- Smart targeting benefits consumers as well as companies by reaching specific groups of people with offers carefully tailored to satisfy their needs.
- However, target marketing sometimes generates public controversy when marketers target vulnerable or disadvantaged populations (such as children or low-income borrowers) with harmful or high-cost products.
- Marketing sugary cereals, high-caffeine energy drinks, or violent entertainment directly to young children through mobile games raises ethical and regulatory scrutiny.
- Socially responsible targeting requires ensuring that segmentation and targeting decisions serve the welfare of the targeted segment rather than exploiting its vulnerabilities.

## Part 3: Differentiation and Positioning Strategy [no-exercise]
- Once a company has decided which segments of the market it will enter, it must decide on its differentiation and positioning strategy.
- A product's position is the complex set of perceptions, impressions, and feelings that consumers hold for the product compared with competing products.

## Perceptual Positioning Maps [no-exercise]
[figure: two-dimensional perceptual positioning map plotting competing automobile brands across price and orientation from economy to luxury and performance]
- Consumers position products in their minds with or without the help of marketers, because they cannot re-evaluate every product from scratch each time they buy.
- In planning their differentiation and positioning strategies, marketers prepare perceptual positioning maps that show consumer perceptions of their brands versus competing products on important buying dimensions.
- For example, an automaker might plot competing sport-utility vehicles on a two-axis map where the horizontal axis represents price (from economy to luxury) and the vertical axis represents orientation (from fuel efficiency to rugged off-road performance).
- The position of each circle on the map indicates the brand's perceived positioning on those two dimensions, and the size of the circle indicates the brand's relative market share, revealing crowded zones and unserved market gaps.

## Choosing Competitive Advantages to Promote [exercise]
- The differentiation and positioning task consists of three steps: identifying a set of possible differentiating competitive advantages, choosing the right competitive advantages, and selecting an overall positioning strategy.
- A competitive advantage is an advantage over competitors gained by offering consumers greater value, either by charging lower prices or by providing more benefits that justify higher prices.
- Firms can differentiate their offerings along five lines: product (features, performance, style), services (speedy delivery, installation), channels (coverage, expertise), people (courteous, well-trained staff), or image (distinctive symbols and brand atmosphere).
- Not every brand difference is worth promoting: a difference is worth establishing to the extent that it satisfies seven criteria: important, distinctive, superior, communicable, preemptive, affordable, and profitable.

## Value Proposition Strategies [exercise]
[figure: five winning value proposition cells comparing benefits offered (more, the same, less) against price charged (more, the same, less)]
- The full positioning of a brand is called the brand's value proposition: the full mix of benefits on which a brand is differentiated and positioned.
- Five winning value propositions exist on the benefit-versus-price grid: More for More (luxury offerings providing upscale benefits at a high price) and More for the Same (offering comparable quality to luxury rivals at a lower price, as Lexus did when challenging Mercedes-Benz).
- The Same for Less offers standard brands at deep discounts based on superior purchasing power and lower-cost operations, while Less for Much Less meets consumers' lower performance or frill requirements at rock-bottom prices (such as budget airlines or no-frills motels).
- More for Less is the most attractive value proposition in the short run, but in the long run companies find it very difficult to sustain offering more benefits while charging less than competitors.

## Part 4: Communicating and Delivering the Chosen Position [no-exercise]
- Selecting a value proposition is only the starting point: the organization must summarize its position clearly and align every operational decision to deliver it.
- A positioning strategy succeeds only when target buyers experience the promised point of difference consistently across all four Ps.

## Crafting a Formal Positioning Statement [no-exercise]
- Company and brand positioning should be summed up in a formal positioning statement that guides internal teams and external agencies.
- The statement follows the standard structure: "To (target segment and need) our (brand) is (concept) that (point of difference)."
- Notice that the statement first states the product's membership in a category (its point of parity) and then shows its point of difference from other members of the same category.
- Defining both category membership and the point of difference ensures that buyers know what the offering can be compared against and why it is superior for their specific need.

## Delivering the Position Across the Marketing Mix [no-exercise]
- Once it has chosen a position, the company must take strong, concrete steps to deliver and communicate the desired position to target consumers.
- All the company's marketing mix efforts must support the positioning strategy: if a firm decides to build a position on "More for More" quality and service, it must first engineer the product to deliver that quality.
- It must then charge the corresponding price, distribute through high-service retailers that reinforce the premium image, and advertise in media that reach quality-conscious buyers.
- Designing the marketing mix (product, price, place, and promotion) is therefore the operational working out of the tactical details of the positioning strategy.

## Maintaining and Adapting Brand Positioning Over Time [no-exercise]
- Establishing a clear position or changing an existing one usually takes a long time because consumer beliefs in memory shift gradually.
- By contrast, a position that took decades to build can be lost quickly if product quality slips or if the firm chases short-term sales with inconsistent promotions.
- Once a company has built the desired position, it must take care to maintain the position through consistent performance and communication while monitoring changes in consumer wants and competitors' strategies.
- Rather than making abrupt, confusing shifts that alienate loyal customers, successful brands evolve their positioning gradually as market conditions change.

## Part 5: Positioning for AI Assistants and Conversational Shortlists [no-exercise]
- Historically, positioning meant occupying a clear, distinctive place in the minds of human target consumers.
- As consumers increasingly ask conversational AI assistants for product recommendations, positioning now also requires occupying a clear, verifiable place in the information sources that AI assistants use to construct their shortlists.

## Brand Positioning and the AI-Generated Consideration Set [exercise]
[figure: conversational AI assistant translating a consumer's need-based prompt into a three-brand shortlist drawn from structured product specifications, reviews, and expert articles]
- Consumers increasingly ask AI assistants natural-language questions such as "Which running shoes are best for marathon training on concrete?" instead of browsing store shelves or ten pages of web links.
- In response, the AI assistant synthesizes product pages, independent review sites, and comparison articles to return a compact shortlist of three to five recommended brands.
- A brand that is absent from that AI-generated shortlist fails to enter the consumer's consideration set, regardless of how memorable its television commercials are.
- Positioning in an AI-mediated market therefore means linking the brand name consistently to specific target segments, usage occasions, and verifiable points of difference across all public text and catalog data.

## Specific Evidence Versus Vague Puffery in AI Positioning [no-exercise]
- Human consumers exposed to low-involvement image advertising may form a warm impression from a vague slogan such as "The Ultimate Running Experience."
- An AI assistant answering a specific user prompt, however, matches the user's stated constraints ("wide toe box," "carbon plate," "cushioning tested for 800 kilometers," "under 180 dollars") against documented product facts.
- Vague puffery gives the language model no concrete attribute evidence with which to match the brand to a segment-specific query.
- Conversely, a brand that states its positioning clearly and supports its point of difference with specific, consistent facts across its website, retail listings, and third-party reviews is retrieved reliably when consumers state that need.

## Auditing Brand Positioning Across AI Assistants [no-exercise]
- Just as marketers prepare perceptual positioning maps from human consumer surveys, they can map how conversational AI assistants position their brand relative to competitors.
- By prompting AI assistants across the key usage occasions and segment needs of the category, managers observe which brands are retrieved for each benefit and which attributes the assistant cites as each brand's strength.
- If an AI assistant associates a brand with an outdated price tier or omits its primary point of difference, the marketing team can diagnose which product pages, schema feeds, or review summaries lack clear evidence.
- Aligning human brand positioning with machine-readable evidence ensures that the brand's value proposition carries through whether the shopper searches personally or delegates the search to an AI assistant.

## Discussion: Auditing a Brand's Position Across Human and AI Channels
- Discussion: A heritage cookware manufacturer positions its cast-iron skillets around the value proposition "More for More," emphasizing generational durability and even heat retention in its print ads, but its online product pages list only dimensions, weight, and a generic paragraph of brand history. When young apartment dwellers ask AI assistants for "durable non-toxic skillets for induction stoves," competing brands dominate the shortlist. Write a formal positioning statement for the brand and specify how it should align its product data, reviews, and communications so its position registers with both human buyers and AI assistants.

## Key Takeaways
- Target marketing replaces one-size-fits-all mass marketing with a four-step customer-driven strategy: segmentation, targeting, differentiation, and positioning (STP).
- Consumer markets are segmented using geographic, demographic, psychographic, and behavioral variables, while business markets add operating, purchasing, and situational variables, and effective segments must be measurable, accessible, substantial, differentiable, and actionable.
- Market targeting evaluates segment size and growth, structural attractiveness, and company fit before choosing a coverage strategy along the continuum from undifferentiated and differentiated marketing to concentrated (niche) and micromarketing.
- Perceptual positioning maps plot competing brands on key buying dimensions to identify market gaps and guide differentiation across product, service, channel, people, or image advantages.
- A differentiator is worth promoting when it is important, distinctive, superior, communicable, preemptive, affordable, and profitable, and is anchored in one of five winning value propositions (More for More, More for the Same, The Same for Less, Less for Much Less, or More for Less).
- Because consumers increasingly rely on conversational AI assistants to build their product shortlists, modern brand positioning must establish clear, fact-backed associations in both human memory and the public text and catalog sources that AI assistants consult.
